# Object-state evolution as a procedural signal

An instructional procedure can be described as a sequence of actions, or as a sequence of changes in the objects those actions act on. The ProcObject-10K authors take the second view. They "formulate a procedure as a sequence of temporally grounded object state transitions with localized evidence and precondition constraints."[^c1] The reason they give is that an action does not fix its result: "the same action can produce different outcomes depending on execution conditions," and that outcome "alters the set of valid subsequent actions."[^c2][^c3] Their example is that "cracking an egg enables mixing, and mixing" leaves a liquid state that later cooking needs.[^c4] On this view the state of an object is the link between one step and the next, so a question about a procedure can be aimed at that link rather than at the action alone.

The question is built around the object. One template of the benchmark is written "How does the [OBJECT] change from [ACTION A] to [ACTION B]?"[^c5] Its answer must say what happened to the object between two actions, and the evidence is the time span in which the change is visible. The clips are cut to match. The authors "apply a temporal sliding window over N segments" of consecutive actions, and they keep windows that give "causally coherent state transitions."[^c6][^c7] Dense captions focus on a few key objects before any question is written, so the question writer sees an object's states rather than the whole scene.[^c8] The five question types are "Precondition Grounding, Object State Evolution, Counterfactual" reasoning, mistake recognition and readiness assessment.[^c9]

The authors argue that earlier benchmarks do not test this link. They say that "current benchmarks fail to evaluate whether models reason over object state dynamics," and that such benchmarks let a model score "instead allowing strong performance through reliance on action patterns."[^c10][^c11] This is an argument from benchmark design. The text read does not run a control that measures how much of a score on an action-centric benchmark rests on action patterns.

What the benchmark measured is a gap, not a single score. Thirteen MLLMs answered and localized; the abstract reports that "models produce plausible answers while failing to localize the supporting evidence".[^c12] The gap is the separation between answering and grounding, which is the same split that [[grounding-answer-dual-axis-audit]] and [[conjunctive-answer-grounding-credit]] make per item for other benchmarks. The authors also train on object-level pseudo labels with spatial and temporal constraints, and report that this "improves both grounding accuracy and reasoning performance."[^c13]

## Limits

- The premise that procedures are driven by object state is the authors' framing. It rests on citations to prior work and on the benchmark's own construction. The text read gives no comparison showing that state questions are harder or more diagnostic than action questions on the same clips (my reading).
- The claim that action-centric benchmarks can be passed through "action patterns" is asserted, not measured. How much of a current model's score on those benchmarks depends on such patterns is not established here.
- The labels are generated by a vision-language model. The pipeline is "VLM generated annotations followed by model-based and manual verification."[^c14] The text read does not report how often the state labels were wrong after verification.
- The domains named are cooking, assembly and medical procedures. Transfer to procedures in which objects do not change state, or to non-instructional video, is not tested here.
- The object state is one choice of unit for a question. No experiment in the text read compares state-centred and action-centred questions on the same clips, so it is not shown that the object is the better unit (my inference).
