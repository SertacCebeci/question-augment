Abstract
Across all fields of academic study, well-respected experts cite their sources when sharing information. While
large language models (LLMs) excel at synthesizing information, they do not provide reliable citation to sources,
making it difficult to trace and verify the origins of the information they present. In contrast, search engines make
sources readily accessible to users and place the burden of synthesizing information on the user. Through a survey,
we find that users prefer search engines over LLMs for high-stakes queries, where concerns regarding information
provenance outweigh the perceived utility of LLM responses. To rigorously examine the interplay between the
verifiability and utility of information-sharing tools, we introduce the extractive-abstractive spectrum, in which
search engines and LLMs are extreme endpoints encapsulating multiple unexplored intermediate operating points.
Search engines are extractive because they respond to queries with snippets of sources with links (citations)
to the original webpages. LLMs are abstractive because they address queries with answers that synthesize
and logically transform relevant information from training and in-context sources without reliable citation. We
define five operating points that span the extractive-abstractive spectrum and conduct human evaluations on
seven systems across four diverse query distributions that reflect real-world QA settings: web search, language
simplification, multi-step reasoning, and medical advice. As outputs become more abstractive, we find that
perceived utility improves by as much as 200%, while the proportion of properly cited sentences decreases
by as much as 50% and users take up to 3 times as long to verify cited information. Our findings recommend
distinct operating points for different domain-specific LLM systems. Additionally, our failure analysis informs
strategies for citation identification following generation, information retrieval tasks specific to citations, and
LLM systems that leverage multiple operating points. By studying the extractive-abstractive spectrum, we can
create high-utility LLM systems that empower users to easily verify provided information.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly capable of answering questions fluently and effectively [Ouyang
et al., 2022, OpenAI et al., 2024]. Users should be able to develop trust in LLMs to provide reliable answers, even on
unfamiliar subjects. However, current LLMs lack accuracy in long-tail knowledge settings [Sun et al., 2023], align with
unreliable sources3 [Anagnostidis and Bulian, 2024], and disseminate biased perspectives and misinformation [Urman
and Makhortykh, 2023]. In light of such shortcomings, it is critical to equip users in verifying LLM-generated claims,
especially as LLMs are applied more widely across domains and users become more dependent on them.
To meet the need for verifiability, numerous generative search engines, including Google Gemini, Perplexity, and
Microsoft Copilot, have combined LLMs with search engines to provide citations. However, these systems struggle
to provide high quality citation; Liu et al. [2023] show that only 74.5% of citations are accurate and only 51.5%
of generated sentences requiring citation are actually cited to sources correctly, across several deployed systems.

Consequently, search engines remain an appealing option for users prioritizing verifiability. Search engine outputs
have high verifiability because users obtain information from the sources themselves, but they sacrifice the fluency,
concision, and synthesis of LLM responses; search engine users must open webpages, identify relevant information,
and synthesize information across multiple sources. Our user survey reflects this contrast between search engines and
LLMs; the former are preferred for reliable answers from a variety of original sources, while the latter are instead
preferred for convenient and personalized answers (Figure 1, top left).
In this work, we interrogate whether we can achieve verifiability while retaining the benefits of LLM responses by
studying the landscape of verifiable systems between search engines and LLMs. We view search engines and LLMs as
two extremes on the extractive-abstractive spectrum. Search engines are extractive with respect to known sources in
that they answer queries with snippets and urls of the sources themselves. Accordingly, citations are inherent to search
engine outputs. In contrast, LLMs are abstractive in that they synthesize content across disparate sources, often without
providing citation. Our work identifies five operating points that span the extractive-abstractive spectrum and make
distinct guarantees to users (Figure 1). For instance, the quoted operating point takes a step from extraction toward
abstraction by extracting direct quotations from multiple sources and incorporating them inline (with citations) into a

single response. Another operating point is the entailed generation, where each sentence in the response is textually
entailed by cited quotes from sources. Despite intense scrutiny over improving citation systems [Nakano et al., 2021,
Gao et al., 2022, Menick et al., 2022, Thoppilan et al., 2022, Slobodkin et al., 2024], existing work predominantly seeks
to achieve a single operating point—the entailed generation. At the same time, quotation and paraphrase have received
relatively little attention in cited generation systems, despite having long been quintessential communication modes in
journalism and media [Gibson and Hester, 1999].
We perform careful human evaluations of seven different systems across the extractive-abstractive spectrum over four
query distributions.4 An ideal system provides relevant and fluent answers that are verifiable, i.e., have exact and
thorough citations that users can verify with minimal effort. To this end, we obtain human evaluations of the quality of
answers (perceived utility and fluency), citation quality (precision and coverage), and the complexity of verification
(time-to-verify). Our results show trade-offs in fluency, perceived utility, and verifiability—we observe as much as a
200% increase in perceived utility and 50% decrease in the proportion of properly cited sentences from extractive to
abstractive cited generation systems, in addition to users taking up to three times as long to verify cited information.
Deployed systems are subject to these trade-offs as well. Despite having high utility, Google Gemini only properly cites
15.0% of generated sentences.5
The four query distributions we evaluate represent real world question-answering (QA) tasks. These tasks include
simplifying the style in which information is presented relative to the original sources [Jeblick et al., 2024, Choi et al.,
2016], multihop reasoning where conclusions must be drawn from premises across multiple sources [Ho et al., 2020],
answering Google Search queries submitted by users [Kwiatkowski et al., 2019], and providing high-stakes medical
advice [Zhu et al., 2020].
By characterizing the trade-offs of generations across the extractive-abstractive spectrum, we provide concrete recommendations for different application settings. Furthermore, we perform an extensive failure analysis that highlights five
perceived utility failure modes, six citation quality failure modes, and three abstention modes. Our analysis highlights
opportunities for future work in identifying citations after generation, information retrieval tasks specific to citations,
and LLM systems that leverage multiple operating points.
Our main contributions are as follows:
1. We run a human preference survey to understand the goals of evaluating different cited generation systems.
2. We define the extractive-abstractive spectrum with five notable operating points that make distinct guarantees
to users.
3. After using human evaluation to characterize the trade-offs between generation quality and verifiability across
seven system implementations spanning the extractive-abstractive spectrum, including Google Gemini, we
recommend different operating points for different application settings.
4. We provide a thorough failure analysis and discuss future work to improve verifiable LLM systems across the
extractive-abstractive spectrum.

Human Preference Survey: LLMs versus Search Engines

Over the last 30 years, search engines have prevailed as the main tool to seek information. With LLM systems as new
contenders for meeting user information needs, it is timely to understand what we gain and what we lose between the
search engine and LLM system user experiences.
In a survey of 200 individuals, we find that users prefer search engines to LLMs without citations, like ChatGPT and
Claude, for answering high-stakes questions, while they prefer LLMs for research-intensive tasks with lower stakes
(Figure 2). Not only do users have platform preferences, but their preferences are informed by specific reasons that
reflect a lack of trust in LLMs. Users prefer search engines for the variety of available sources, understanding of
information provenance, and reliability of provided information, in contrast to preferring LLMs for convenience and
personalized answers (Figure 1, top left). Our evaluations of citation quality and perceived utility are motivated by the
lack of user confidence in LLMs to provide supporting sources and in search engines to provide personalized answers.
While user preferences may change as LLMs become increasingly commonplace, these results indicate that users
perceive different information-seeking tasks as being best served by entirely different systems. Accordingly, there is an
exciting opportunity to design systems preferred by users across a broad range of queries (e.g. all queries in Figure 2).

Cited Generation Formalization

We consider the task of cited generation for queries in the open-ended QA setting. Given a user-provided natural
language query, x, the systems we study produce a natural language generation y that contains inline citations to sources.
The generation y is partitioned into n attributable units, U = {u1 , ..., un }, which our work defines as passages of text,
often sentences. Attributable unit ui is cited to mi quotes from sources, Qi = {qi,1 , ..., qi,mi }, where qi,j is a direct
quote from a known source. Accordingly, any claim contained within qi,j is interpreted in the context of its original
source text. We refer to the set of source texts from which quotes across all Qi originate as S. Lastly, Qi does not
contain any cited quotes if ui does not require citation.6 For example:

4

The Extractive-Abstractive Spectrum

A response to a query is extractive with respect to cited source quotes if the response is composed of the source quotes
themselves. For example, search engines respond to queries with snippets of webpages. On the other hand, a response is
abstractive with respect to cited source quotes if the cited information is reworded, pruned, supplemented, and logically
transformed, e.g. the abstractive generation in Figure 1 (bottom). We define notable operating points (OPs) over the
extractive–abstractive spectrum and describe our implementation of each operating point, as well as additional baselines.
4.1

Operating Point Definitions

Early works on automatic summarization frame abstraction as a means to achieving coherence [McKeown et al., 1999,
Knight and Marcu, 2002]. Other works discern the abstraction of generated summaries with respect to source text using
n-gram based metrics [Ladhak et al., 2022, Dreyer et al., 2023, Weller et al., 2024]. However, neither coherence nor
n-gram based metrics capture how generated text semantically relates to source text. In contrast, Jing and McKeown
[1999] study how humans use the semantic operations of paraphrase and information removal to write abstractive
summaries of a source text. In the same spirit, we define operating points across the extractive-abstractive spectrum that
correspond to distinct user experiences regarding the semantic relation between generations and cited source quotes.
Our definitions for the quoted, paraphrased, and entailed generations draw from work on meaning relations [Gold et al.,
2019] applied to the setting of citations by Worledge et al. [2024]. Figure 1 (bottom) provides an example of each OP.
Extractive Output: An extractive output is a set of quotes from the sources themselves. In the context of extractive
outputs, we refer to these quotes as snippets, as in Google Search. Citations are inherent to this operating point.
Formally, ∀ ui , |Qi | = 1, ui = qi,1 , where ui may be multiple sentences.
Quoted Generation: A quoted generation achieves grounding by incorporating quotes of claims from known sources
inline with unquoted words used sparingly to facilitate fluency. The quotes are taken word-for-word from the sources,
are not misrepresented from the original context, and are clearly demarcated from non-quoted text in the generation.
Formally, ∀ ui , Qi ⇔ ui and every qi,j is a quoted substring of ui .
Paraphrased Generation: A paraphrased generation achieves grounding by rephrasing and rewording information
from cited source quotes. Each sentence in the paraphrased generation conveys the same information as its cited source
quotes; the generation does not remove any information, does not contract logical steps of reasoning, and does not add
new claims, relative to the cited quotes. Formally, ∀ ui , Qi ⇔ ui .
Entailed Generation: An entailed generation achieves grounding by rephrasing, rewording, removing, and logically
transforming information relative to cited source quotes. Each sentence in the entailed generation conveys no more
information than its cited source quotes; the generation may remove extraneous information and contract logical steps
of reasoning, but does not add new claims, relative to the cited quotes. Formally, ∀ ui , Qi ⇒ ui .
Abstractive Generation: Each sentence of an abstractive generation may contain claims that are not cited
(ui,uncited claims ), in addition to claims that are cited (ui,cited claims ). Accordingly, an abstractive sentence is not necessarily
grounded and may convey more information than the cited source quotes. Formally, if ui,cited claims ̸= ∅, then Qi ⇒
ui,cited claims .
We note that the quoted OP is distinct from more abstractive OPs for two reasons. First, quotation marks delineate
cited content from uncited words in the generation. Second, because quotes in the generation exactly match text from a
source, users only need to ensure the quote is not taken out of context from the source when verifying a cited quote. In
contrast, when verifying citations from more abstractive OPs, users have the additional burden of ensuring that the cited
sentence is faithfully re-written from the source.

While other OPs of interest may lie on the extractive-abstractive spectrum, we highlight the OPs defined above
because they either correspond to deployed systems or bridge the gap between familiar user experiences. For instance,
conventional search engines provide extractive responses. To the best of our knowledge, prior work has neither defined
nor implemented quoted and paraphrased cited generations in the context of cited question-answering LLM systems. We
refer readers interested in systems from prior work and their relation to our operating points to Sections 10.2 and 10.1.
Most prior work on cited LLM generations seek to implement the entailed operating point. LLM generations without
citations are a commonplace example of the abstractive generation, where there are no cited claims (ui,cited claims = ∅).
The internals of deployed generative search engines are not public, but we speculate they fall around the entailed and
abstractive operating points.
4.2

Reference Operating Point Implementations

We design reference OP implementations to study trade-offs across the extractive-abstractive spectrum. In order to
make each OP comparable to the others, we ground responses with the same set of source quotes for each query.7 We
describe the reference implementation for each OP and provide further details in the appendix (13.6).
The extractive output is a list of source snippets. Each snippet is about 1000 characters long and contains a quote
used in the reference quoted generation. These snippets are either retrieved or gold-standard snippets (when they are
provided by a dataset). To retrieve snippets given a query, we scrape text from the top 50 Google Search results for the
query, chunk the text into snippets about 1000 characters long, and select the ten most relevant snippets to the query
using Dense Passage Retrieval [Karpukhin et al., 2020].
The quoted generation is generated from GPT-4 with few-shot examples, the query, and relevant source snippets
provided in-context. We instruct the model to respond to the query using word-for-word quotes from the provided
sources. The prompt with few-shot examples is provided in the appendix (13.8.1). Citations are identified by matching
quotes in the generation with their word-for-word counterparts in the snippets provided in-context, using string matching.
We generate each reference quoted generation 10 times and keep the generation with the fewest unquoted words. Across
our evaluated queries, 82% of words in quoted generations are quoted and on average, quotes are 14.5 words long. We
denote this implementation as Mquoted (x, S).
Each of the paraphrased, entailed, and abstractive generations is a revision of the quoted generation. These
generations are obtained by prompting GPT-4 with few-shot examples, the query, and the corresponding quoted
generation (not the source snippets). The paraphrased instruction requests a rewrite, the entailed instruction requests
a more concise generation, and the abstractive instruction requests the model to improve the quoted generation by
adding additional information. The prompts for each OP are provided in the appendix (13.8.2, 13.8.3, 13.8.4). For
each sentence in the generation, citations are obtained via few-shot prompting and a citation instruction that identifies
supporting quotes from those used in the quoted generation. Each supporting quote is cited within its original snippet.
The prompts for citation identification are available in the appendix (13.8.5). We denote the paraphrased implementation
as Mparaphrased (x, Mquoted (x, S)), with the same function signature applying to Mentailed and Mabstractive .
The citations provided by all reference generations are both corroborative and contributive attributions; they provide
users with sources that both support claims in the generation and contributed to the creation of the generation [Worledge
et al., 2024].
4.3

Additional Evaluated Systems

In addition to the reference implementations of the OPs, we study two more methods of cited generation. One method
combines publicly available APIs for generation and citation, while the other is a deployed system. Further details
about these implementations are included in the appendix (13.6.2, 13.6.3).
The GPT-4 + Vertex generation is another instantiation of the abstractive OP. The response is generated by conditioning
GPT-4 on the query, without retrieved sources. Using Google’s Vertex API8 , we identify citations for individual
generated sentences from snippets retrieved using Google search and a semantic similarity model9 , in addition to any
gold-standard source snippets provided by the query dataset. Because cited sources are only retrieved and identified
after response generation, we refer to these citations as post-hoc citations.

5

Evaluation of the Extractive-Abstractive Spectrum

We conduct human evaluations of the reference, GPT-4 + Vertex, and Gemini generations. To capture the quality and
verifiability of generations from each operating point, we record five metrics over four query distributions.
5.1

Metrics

An ideal cited generation addresses the query fluently, effectively, and verifiably. For the first two properties, we remove
citation markers and evaluate fluency and perceived utility for the entire generation. To measure verifiability, we record
the precision of each citation, the citation coverage of each sentence, and the time to verify each sentence. We define
each metric below.
Fluency: Annotators are asked to count misprints, incoherent sentences, and abrupt transitions between sentences
against fluency (Fig. 12). The fluency of a response to a query is evaluated on a three-point scale, 3 being fluent and 1
not being fluent.
Perceived Utility: Annotators are asked to consider the extent to which they believe a generation addresses the query,
is concise, and is written in an appropriate style (Fig. 12). We note that perceived utility does not necessarily measure
the correctness of generated text because annotators may not have the domain knowledge to fact-check generated
information. The utility of a generation for a query is evaluated on a three-point scale, 3 being high utility and 1 being
low utility.
Citation Precision: A citation accompanying text is precise if the cited source supports at least one claim made in the
text [Liu et al., 2023]. An imprecise citation is one that is either irrelevant to the text, or, in the worst case, disagrees
with the text. For each citation in a generation, annotators are given the binary task of determining whether the cited
source supports at least one claim in the generated sentence containing the citation. We leave the task of identifying
claims within a sentence to the discretion of the human annotators and their experience as information-seeking users.
Annotators are trained to use the intuitive test from Rashkin et al. [2022] where a cited source, s, supports a claim, c, if
the annotator believes it is truthful to say, "According to s, c."
Citation Coverage: A sentence is covered if the cited sources accompanying the sentence support all claims made
in the sentence. We consider citation coverage as a quantity that best captures a user’s verifiability concerns; even
with perfect citation precision, it is possible for generated claims to be unsupported. For each sentence in a generation,
annotators are given the binary task of determining whether all claims in the sentence are supported by at least one
citation accompanying the sentence. Annotators are trained to use the intuitive test from Rashkin et al. [2022] to
determine whether the cited sources together support the generated sentence.
Time to Verify (T2V): T2V is the wall clock time it takes a human to determine whether a generated sentence is
covered by its accompanying citations. T2V captures the complexity a user faces when verifying a sentence with cited
sources. To account for differences in reading speeds between annotators throughout the study, we report relative T2V,
which is the average normalized T2V; each T2V value is normalized by its annotator’s average T2V over sentences
from quoted generations. We measure T2V over the task of determining the coverage of a single sentence, rather than
the precision of a citation, because determining coverage more holistically represents the user experience of verifying
select sentences. We discuss and present results on other variants of relative T2V, including absolute T2V and the time
to verify entire responses in the appendix (13.3).
While the fluency and perceived utility ratings are given for the entire response, our results for citation precision,
coverage, and time-to-verify only consider sentences that require citation. We use the Vertex API to identify filler
sentences, such as "That’s a good question", that do not require citation and find there are few filler sentences across all
generations. Results for citation precision, coverage, and T2V over all sentences are nearly identical to those only over
sentences that require citation and are included in the appendix (13.2).

5.2

Query Distributions

We obtain human evaluations for each of seven cited generations over 480 open-ended question-answering (QA) queries,
drawn from four query distributions that reflect different information-seeking scenarios. The reference implementations
of the operating points require sources to create the cited generations. For each query distribution, we either retrieve
sources from Google or use gold-standard sources. Gold-standard sources are sources provided by the query dataset
that are guaranteed to answer the query. Our snippet retrieval pipeline is detailed in Section 4.2.
Natural Questions (NQ) [Kwiatkowski et al., 2019]: The NQ query distribution is composed of real user queries to
Google Search. We retrieve the sources for each NQ query.
Explain to a Third Grader (Eta3G): The Eta3G query distribution is a variant of the NQ query distribution
[Kwiatkowski et al., 2019] where NQ queries are prepended with the prefix, "Explain to a third-grader: ". Queries from
this distribution not only seek relevant information in response to the query, but also require that the information is
presented in a style suitable for a third-grade reading level and maturity. We retrieve the sources for each Eta3G query.
Wikipedia and Wikidata Multi-Hop (2WikiMH) [Ho et al., 2020]: Answering queries from 2WikiMH requires
combining premises from multiple sources to draw logical conclusions not necessarily present in any individual source.
The queries themselves are about the relation between people, places, dates, and other proper nouns, e.g. "Who is the
paternal grandfather of Prince Alexander Of Imereti (1760–1780)?" We use the gold-standard source snippets provided
by 2WikiMH for each query.
Multiple Answer Spans Healthcare (MASH) [Zhu et al., 2020]: The queries from the MASH distribution are from
the perspective of consumers seeking medical advice, a high risk setting where unreliable information has consequences
on human health. We use the gold-standard source snippets from WebMD articles provided by MASH for each query.
5.3

Evaluation Procedure

Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) annotators evaluated cited generations for fluency, utility, citation precision, citation
coverage, and T2V. MTurk workers residing in the United States with HIT success rates over 96% were invited to
read the annotation guidelines (Fig. 11) and take a screener HIT to qualify for the study. We provided annotators
with high performance on the screener with personalized feedback and only admitted those who acknowledged the
correspondence. Thirty-one annotators were ultimately admitted to the study. Throughout the study, we maintained an
active presence through email and a dedicated channel on the Turker Nation Slack workspace.
We assigned each annotator to evaluate an equal number of generations from each reference operating point and an equal
number of GPT-4 + Vertex and Gemini generations. In order to avoid any effects of familiarization with the queries and
sources on T2V, no annotator evaluated citations for the same query in more than one OP. We provide further details
about the evaluation procedure, annotator compensation, annotation guidelines, and a pilot study performed before the
MTurk evaluations in Appendix 13.4.

6

Results and Analysis

6.1

Summary of results across the extractive-abstractive spectrum

While fluency and perceived utility increase as generations become more abstractive relative to cited sources, verifiability
degrades. In particular, perceived utility increases by over 200% and citation coverage decreases by over 50% between
the extractive and GPT-4 + Vertex abstractive generations (Figure 3, left and middle). Simultaneously, humans take three
times longer to verify cited sentences from GPT-4 + Vertex generations relative to those from quoted generations (Figure
3, right). The deployed Gemini system is Pareto-dominated by the entailed generations; while Gemini generations
demonstrate similar fluency and perceived utility compared to the entailed generations, they exhibit over 80% lower
coverage (Figure 3, left and middle). Humans also take significantly longer to verify cited sentences from Gemini than
from the entailed generations (Figure 3, right).
6.1.1

Perceived Generation Quality

Generations that are more abstractive with respect to sources are more fluent and have higher perceived utility than
generations that are more extractive (Figure 3 left). Fluency is lowest for the extractive OP, which provides sources in a
list without smooth transitions. The quoted OP significantly improves fluency by incorporating source quotes inline
within a single response, but the flexibility of the paraphrased, entailed, and abstractive OPs leads to more natural and
fluent generations.

Similarly, the extractive OP exhibits the lowest perceived utility; source snippets often contain irrelevant information

6.1.2

Citation Precision Results

Citation precision is relatively similar (95%-100%) across the reference OP generations, but is significantly lower for
the GPT-4 + Vertex and Gemini generations (Figure 3, middle). This result indicates that the paradigm employed by
the reference implementation of first choosing source quotes to cite and then generating a response using them, or
more succinctly, "attribute first, then generate" [Slobodkin et al., 2024], mitigates the trade-off between abstraction and
precision observed by Liu et al. [2023] among deployed implementations of the entailed OP. In contrast, methods like
the GPT-4 + Vertex pipeline and Gemini that provide post-hoc citations are more susceptible to precision errors. We
discuss examples, categories, and frequencies of precision errors by implementation in Section 8.2.
6.1.3

Citation Coverage Results

As generations become more abstractive, citation coverage trends downward in Figure 3 (middle). As expected,
coverage drops significantly for the reference abstractive generations—by definition, the abstractive OP contains claims
not entailed by cited sources. Coverage is even lower for GPT-4 + Vertex and Gemini generations, at 43.4% and 15.0%
respectively. The low coverage of Gemini generations indicate that achieving high coverage in deployed settings may
present additional challenges, such as cost and latency. Regardless, the finding that Gemini and abstractive generations
have both high perceived utility and low coverage leads to the concern that convincing generations may lead users to
believe insufficiently supported information.
6.1.4

Relative T2V Results

As generations become increasingly abstractive with respect to cited source quotes, humans take longer to verify
sentences with accompanying citations (Figure 3, right). Relative to quoted generations, we find that some abstractive
generations take up to three times as long to verify. The quoted OP significantly expedites human verification of cited
text in comparison to all other OPs; simply rewording quoted generations to be paraphrased generations increases T2V
by over 40%. The positive relation between T2V and generation abstraction reflects the cognitive load placed on users
verifying citations by reworded and logically transformed cited source quotes.

Discussion: Optimal operating point differs across tasks

Due to the observed trade-offs across the extractive-abstractive spectrum, there is no one-size-fits-all operating point
across applications. In particular, the low verification complexity and high citation coverage of more extractive OPs
are often desirable in high-stakes settings that require frequent verification, as indicated by our user preference survey
(Figure 1, top left; Figure 2). On the other hand, more abstractive generations with higher fluency and perceived utility
may be more desirable for settings where verification is less important. The extractive-abstractive trade-offs motivate a
segmentation of QA settings into three categories (Table 2). Model developers should consider the priorities of their
application area in the context of trade-offs across the extractive-abstractive spectrum and design their cited generation
systems to achieve favorable operating points.

7.1

High-stakes settings with dispersed information in presentable form

In some QA settings, queries require a search over refined corpora for information that is already written in an
appropriate form, but may be dispersed over multiple sources. In this search setting, LLMs can play a critical role in
processing queries to aid with retrieval and in generating responses that stitch together information across multiple
sources. Due to the importance of verifiability in these high-stakes settings and the limited involvement of LLMs in
12

A P REPRINT

generating responses, these settings are best served by more extractive operating points, such as quoted and extractive
generations.
An example of this setting is the use of LLMs to assist lawyers in writing legal documents. Errors in legal documents
arising from misinformation from LLM systems lead to serious consequences, including suspension of legal licenses,
fines, and reputational harm.12,13 Because verifiability is critical in legal applications of LLMs, extractive and quoted
operating points may best suit this application, given the current limitations of LLMs. For example, Harvey14 has
deployed an LLM-based instantiation of the extractive operating point for law firms. As long as lawyers themselves
are formulating the legal arguments, the logical transformations achieved by more abstractive operating points are not
necessary, whereas citations to relevant sections of case materials are critical.
7.2

Low-stakes settings with information in need of refinement

Other settings require reformulating and recombining information from original sources into a different style. In cases
where information errors have insignificant consequences, verifiability is a low priority. Accordingly, the abstractive
operating point is ideal for these settings.
Low-stakes LLM applications seeking creativity, such as creative writing assistance [Mirowski et al., 2023] or brainstorming light-hearted ideas for further consideration [Shaer et al., 2024], do not require verifiability. Rather, these
applications intend to generate novel content not necessarily found in existing sources; the abstractive operating point
is the only operating point that can achieve this goal. Citations may still be desired to determine whether generated
content is taken from other sources. In particular, Lee et al. [2023] study verbatim, paraphrase, and idea plagiarism,
which roughly correspond to the cases of quoted, paraphrased, and entailed cited generations, without citation.
7.3

High-stakes settings with information in need of refinement

In other QA settings, queries require reformulating information from one or more sources into a different style while
providing high verifiability. These are the most challenging settings to address because verifiability trades off with
generation abstraction.
Healthcare is a high-stakes domain for LLM applications; errors in generation may lead to serious consequences
regarding patient health and legal action. Tasks such as transforming patient visit dialogue into medical notes [Biswas
and Talukdar, 2024, Leong et al., 2024] require high faithfulness to the original dialogue and substantive style change
from colloquial patient descriptions of health issues to standard medical terminology. Similarly, tasks such as the
simplification of patient medical records for layperson understanding [Jeblick et al., 2024, Choi et al., 2016] or the
simplification of legal contracts for lower reading grade levels [Ali et al., 2024] also require both high verifiability and
significant changes in style. While the extractive and quoted OPs prioritize verifiability, they are not expressive enough
to effectively transform the style of the generation for these applications (see Section 8.1.4). Paraphrased and entailed
generations provide more of a balance between verifiability and stylistic change. However, the risk of an erroneous
generation leading to severe consequences remains significant; experts are still required to verify generations for these
applications, underscoring the importance of simple verification [Ali et al., 2024, Mathews, 2024, Jeblick et al., 2022].
Under current extractive-abstractive spectrum trade-offs, none of the operating points achieve high verifiability and
perceived utility simultaneously, as is desirable for high-stakes tasks that require frequent verification in addition to
style change and logical transformation. Such applications would be best served by future implementations of the
entailed operating point that further improve citation quality and new strategies for citation that reduce verification
complexity. In particular, novel approaches to citation in settings simplifying terminology should be designed to be
accessible and educational to the target audience.

Sometimes, a generation has low utility because it only enumerates the premises of the answer, without drawing the

8.1.4

Utility failure: Unsuitable style

Quoted generations may also have low utility due to being written in a style that does not suit the query. Table 4 shows
that this failure mode is most common in quoted generations for the Eta3G query distribution, which follows from
relatively few internet sources being written for third-graders.
The quoted generation in Example 6.3 relies on vocabulary, such as "bicameral", "interim", and "synodical", that

generation is written in a significantly more appropriate style than the quoted generation.

mode affects any OP implementation that generates responses using in-context sources, such as the reference OP

accompanied by gold-standard sources, suggesting that even among relevant sources, some are more interpretable than

Table 7 shows that a complete lack of citation is the most common failure mode of the quoted generations. This means

9

Discussion: Recommendations for future work

Given the results of our failure analysis, we identify three key areas for future work in improving cited generation
systems across the extractive-abstractive spectrum. Our discussion here is complementary to our recommendations for
cited generation systems in applied settings included in Section 7.
9.1

Improving post-hoc citations

The low citation precision and coverage of GPT-4 + Vertex and Gemini, relative to those of the reference implementations,
indicate that post-hoc citations are challenging to identify. The predominant precision failure for GPT-4 + Vertex and
Gemini citations is when semantic similarity between an irrelevant source and a generated sentence is mistaken for
the source entailing the generated sentence (Section 8.2.2). This result reflects the tendency of models to rely on the
spurious correlation of word overlap between source and target sentence for the natural language inference (NLI) task
of determining whether the source entails the target sentence [McCoy et al., 2019]. Post-hoc citation identification is
19

A P REPRINT

further complicated by the practical implementation of selecting candidate sources for their semantic similarity to the
generated text; we emphasize the importance of ensuring the robustness of NLI methods from the benchmark setting to
downstream application settings.
While continued improvement in NLI capabilities is critical for post-hoc citation identification, we highlight that
these two tasks are not identical. NLI classifies the meaning relation between a text and a single source. In contrast,
citation identification is a combinatorial search problem: given a text and a set of candidate sources, the task is to
identify a subset of the sources that together entail the text. For instance, a response to a query that requires multi-hop
reasoning may only be entailed by a set of sources and no individual source alone. For instance, the claim “The
maternal grandfather of X is Z” is only entailed by the union of sources that state “The mother of X is Y” and “The
father of Y is Z”; neither of these sources alone entail the claim. The combinatorial complexity of identifying post-hoc
citations makes the paradigm of grounding a generation in specific source quotes pre-designated for citation particularly
appealing.
The especially low citation coverage of GPT-4 + Vertex and Gemini generations, with the majority of uncovered
sentences without any citation, may reflect the failure mode of seeking sources that entail the claim individually. After
all, the Vertex API provides scores indicating the extent to which individual sources support the provided text and
Gemini provides at most one citation per sentence. Unfortunately, the naive approach for identifying an entailing subset
of sources requires combinatorial applications of the NLI module. To address this, we hope to see future work on
citation identification that first decomposes text into sub-claims and then individually supports each sub-claim with a
single source. Min et al. [2023] shows promising results with such decompositions for fact verification, while Wanner
et al. [2024] demonstrates that decomposition strategy affects verification results. Further work is required to obtain
effective claim decompositions, especially on generations for multi-hop queries, where important sub-claims may be
implicit. Furthermore, citation identification for users requires low verification complexity. Accordingly, the subset of
cited sources should not be redundant; the removal of any one source should nullify the entailment relation. Future
work on these initial results from fact verification should also ensure the resulting set of cited sources is deduplicated
and verifiable by humans with low T2V.
9.2

Task-specific information retrieval

Throughout the extractive-abstractive spectrum, information retrieval (IR) is fundamental for different steps in cited
output generation. We encourage system developers to implement distinct IR systems for three different tasks. The first
task of query-source retrieval identifies sources likely to contain an answer, given a query. Query-source retrieval is
broadly recognized as a fundamental IR task for QA systems that ground generations in relevant sources [Karpukhin
et al., 2020]. However, even if a relevant source is retrieved, we observe in Section 8.1.5 that responses grounded
in retrieved sources may focus on irrelevant sections of the source. This failure mode illuminates a second finergrain query-quote retrieval task of identifying specific quotes within a retrieved source, given a query. This task is
fundamental to the quoted OP and the "attribute-first-then-generate" paradigm from Slobodkin et al. [2024], which both
require identifying quotes within relevant sources to answer a given query. For the quoted OP, this retrieval task has
additional objectives such as identifying concise quotes that are also possible to fluently incorporate inline.
The third task of text-entailment retrieval identifies sources that are not only relevant to a given text, but entail the
text. Systems that identify post-hoc citations rely on this task. This task is often implemented in two stages. First,
sources are retrieved for their semantic similarity to the given text. Second, an NLI method is applied to re-rank the
retrieved sources. We observe in Section 8.2.2 that the most common citation precision failure is due to the cited source
having semantic similarity to the text, rather than sharing an entailment relation with the text. While shortcomings
in current NLI systems contribute to this failure mode, it also motivates an IR system that seeks entailment relations,
rather than semantic similarity, from the start. Roth et al. [2009] has considered this IR task before, but to the best of
our knowledge, modern dense retrieval methods have not yet been used to implement this task.
9.3

Leveraging different OPs within the same system

Some LLM systems need to handle a variety of queries. In Section 7 we discuss how different queries are best served by
different operating points; to best serve users across all queries, we recommend that general-purpose systems consider
routing different types of queries to different operating points. For instance, a query for health advice should be routed
for a more extractive generation than a query for birthday party ideas because health advice carries higher stakes.
In light of the perceived utility failures in Section 8.1, queries requiring style change or concise logical conclusions
should be routed to the entailed or abstractive operating points. A thoughtful method for routing queries to different
points across the extractive-abstractive spectrum must either leverage personalized verifiability preferences or learn a
nuanced understanding of the potential downstream consequences of different queries on human decisions and actions.
20

A P REPRINT

Alternatively, systems can directly provide users with a choice between different operating point generations for any
given query.
We also recommend that system developers consider interpolating between operating points within a generation for an
individual query. For example, pertinent information in a generation may need to be more verifiable than other parts of
the generation. Such pertinent information could be quoted and the rest of the generation could be written as a more
abstractive OP, similar to how quotations are used in journalism to communicate important points from relevant sources.
This approach requires a method that recognizes certain sentences or claims as requiring more verifiability than others;
it is a generalization of the task of determining which sentences require citation, which itself is challenging (Appendix
13.2). By leveraging multiple operating points within the same system or generation, developers can design systems to
provide useful and verifiable generations for diverse queries.

10

Related Works

10.1

Entailed Generation Systems

Many works have proposed systems that achieve the entailed operating point. For example, Nakano et al. [2021] use
reinforcement learning from human preferences (RLHP) to fine-tune WebGPT which interacts with a web interface and
generates responses with citation to web page passages. Menick et al. [2022] also use RLHP to fine-tune GopherCite to
generate responses with citation to succinct source quotes retrieved through Google Search. Thoppilan et al. [2022]
fine-tune LaMDA models to generate responses with citation to URLs. Gao et al. [2022] and Chen et al. [2023] propose
systems that identify citations to evidence snippets after a response has been generated and revised in accordance
with retrieved sources, rather than generating the original response and citations from retrieved sources. Shao et al.
[2024] and Jiang et al. [2024] generate long reports with citations to entailing source snippets and entire sources,
respectively. Slobodkin et al. [2024] propose the "Attribute First, then Generate" method, in which specific source
quotes are designated as citations for each sentence even before the response is generated. This paradigm is shared by
our reference implementation, in which the specific quotes incorporated into the quoted generation are then used to
generate the paraphrased, entailed, and abstractive cited generations. While the aforementioned systems implement the
entailed operating point, our work studies different operating points spanning the extractive-abstractive spectrum.
10.2

Extractive and Quoted Generation Systems

Extractive generations have long been considered for QA [Kolomiyets and Moens, 2011]. More recent works explore
the use of quotations in generations, although selected quotes are often entities or sentence fragments and are too short
to contain full claims. Notably, Schuster et al. [2024] propose the task of Semi-Extractive Multi-Source Question
Answering (SEMQA), in which short quotes of factual dates, entities, and statements are "glued together" with generated
text. Both Weller et al. [2024] and Zhang et al. [2024] propose stepping away from citations in favor of improving
verifiability by quoting pre-training data, using prompting and reward-base optimization respectively. In these works
quoting pre-training data, verifiability is measured with a character 25-gram metric that accepts short quotations. It
is unclear where these methods fall on the extractive-abstractive spectrum. While these works require source quotes
to be substrings of the generated sentence, our quoted operating point additionally specifies that claims within cited
source quotes should entail the generated sentence. Additionally, we provide a quoted generation implementation
that leverages up-to-date non-parametric knowledge, but future implementations of the quoted operating point could
leverage parametric content as well.
10.3

Faithfulness in relation to abstraction

Prior work in the area of automatic text summarization studies the relationship between summary abstraction and
faithfulness [Ladhak et al., 2022, Dreyer et al., 2023]. Through generating faithfulness-abstractiveness trade-off curves
across multiple models, Ladhak et al. [2022] demonstrate that the abstraction of a summary with respect to the original
text, as measured by n-gram metrics, is correlated with the unfaithfulness of the summary across models. Dreyer et al.
[2023] study this same trade-off for individual models. In contrast to the extractive-abstractive operating points, papers
in this line of work focus on a smooth notion of abstraction, rather than distinct levels of abstraction that correspond to
interpretable differences in user experience. Furthermore, these works consider neither the task of citation identification
nor the verification complexity experienced by users.
In the setting of cited LLM generations, trade-offs between cited generation desiderata have been observed in audits of
deployed systems. In studying deployed citation systems that fall under the entailed operating point, Liu et al. [2023]
present positive correlation between citation precision and generation extractiveness relative to cited web pages, as
measured by BLEU and BERTScore, and negative correlation between citation precision and perceived utility. This
21

A P REPRINT

work explores noteworthy trade-offs associated with different levels of abstraction achieved within a single operating
point, while we study trade-offs across the entire extractive-abstractive spectrum.
10.4

Evaluating verifiability

Prior works have also established guidelines for evaluating citations. Rashkin et al. [2022] define the attributed to
identified sources (AIS) framework used for human evaluations of whether a statement is interpretable and supported
by provided sources. Expanding from the AIS framework, Bohnet et al. [2023] propose a framework for attributed
question answering and Gao et al. [2023] propose an automated evaluation framework for the fluency, correctness, and
citation quality of cited generations and demonstrate their correlation with human evaluations.
Prior work has also considered the wall clock time of citation evaluations. Rashkin et al. [2022] record completion
times across the tasks of rating both interpretability and citation coverage for entire responses as a notion annotation
quality. Schuster et al. [2024] also reports annotation duration timed across multiple tasks, including those unrelated to
citation accuracy evaluation. Most similar to our work, Slobodkin et al. [2024] record annotator completion times for
entire responses across only the citation coverage task as a measure of citation verification complexity. Rashkin et al.
[2022] observes that annotators became faster at the AIS evaluation throughout their study, perhaps due to increased
familiarity with the task. In order to account for this effect in addition to variation in annotator reading speed when
drawing comparisons across different cited generation types, we propose normalizing each annotator’s time to evaluate
citation coverage by their time on a baseline citation coverage task evaluated in parallel with every cited generation
type. We present this metric as relative T2V for individual sentences in order to capture the user experience verifying
sentences with citation.

11

Conclusion

We investigated cited generations across the extractive-abstractive spectrum through the lens of human user experience
to inform the design of future information systems. Our work shows that while users perceive generations to be more
useful as they become more abstractive, citation coverage is up to 50% lower and humans take up to three times longer
to verify cited sentences. The deployed Google Gemini system is subject to these trade-offs as well; despite having high
fluency and perceived utility, only 15.0% of sentences generated by Gemini are fully supported by citations to sources.
Our trade-off results show that designing a one-size-fits-all cited LLM system requires strategically leveraging operating
points across the extractive-abstractive spectrum within a single system. Through a failure analysis of generations
across the spectrum, we recommend three implementation opportunities for future cited LLM systems: identifying
post-hoc citations on the granularity of claims within sentences, combinations of operating points within the same
system or generation, and information retrieval for specific source quotes and entailment relations. We hope our work
helps model developers situate their application area and build systems that best maximize utility and verifiability.
One limitation of our study is that our reference implementations do not meet the compute efficiency and latency
criteria required in deployed settings. Because citation generation often requires additional rounds of retrieval and
inference, we hypothesize that these deployment considerations could exacerbate the already low citation coverage
of more abstractive generations. Another limitation stems from our assumption that retrieved sources are inherently
trustworthy. Determining whether a source reflects objective truth to the best of human knowledge is a nuanced task
that can require domain expertise and examination of sources in relation to one another. Additionally, our study focuses
solely on whether citations to sources are accurate, and not on whether these citations effectively assist users in detecting
model hallucinations; especially in systems with high T2V, users may not examine citations thoroughly enough to
detect unverified content. These limitations reflect directions for future work in building low latency citation systems,
identifying trustworthy sources, and understanding the downstream impact of citations on user decision-making.
Understanding the trade-off between information system utility and verifiability is timely. Not only are LLM systems
becoming more commonplace across domains, but LLMs are also becoming increasingly capable. We hope that future
LLM developments will push the Pareto frontier across fluency, perceived utility and verifiability. While improvements
in LLM capabilities may naturally lead to more accurate and comprehensive citations, it is unclear whether generations
with such citations will also be easier for users to verify. Without deliberate efforts to control verification complexity
in LLM systems capable of addressing increasingly complex queries, verifying generations from such systems may
naturally become burdensome for users. Yet, verifiability is critical to navigate our world where disinformation abounds,
long-tail knowledge constantly evolves, and LLMs retain bias from training, even as their capabilities scale. We hope
the extractive-abstractive spectrum inspires future information systems that play upon the strengths of multiple operating
points in order to maximize both utility and verifiability for users.

Alternative measures of T2V

We investigate whether the increase in relative T2V is driven by the increase in coverage errors as generations become
more abstractive. Figure 7B shows that relative T2V increases as generations become more abstractive over sentences
that are properly covered by citation. While users evidently take longer to evaluate sentences with coverage failures, this
does not drive the differences in relative T2V across operating points observed in Figure 7A. We also report absolute
T2V in seconds in Figure 7C.
27

A P REPRINT

Figure 7: (A) Figure 3 copied for convenience. Over all sentences, relative T2V increases alongside generation abstraction. (B) Over
sentences with citation coverage, relative T2V increases as generation abstraction increases. (C) Over all sentences, absolute T2V
increases as generation abstraction increases. Absolute T2V is the wall clock time in seconds taken to verify a cited sentence.

Additionally, we examine the time that annotators take to verify entire responses. While our work considers T2V as
the time taken to evaluate the coverage of an individual sentence to emulate the user experience of verifying whether
part of a generation is correct, verifying the entire response is also a meaningful user experience. However, taking
response-level T2V across all generations introduces a confounding effect. More abstractive generations tend to have
lower coverage in part because more sentences that require citation simply have no citation at all. Because T2V is only
measured for sentences with citation, responses with sentences without citation then appear to be evaluated far faster
than responses where all sentences have citation. We report the relative time to verify entire responses where each
sentence has at least one citation, by query distribution (Figure 8). However, we note that only evaluating responses
that have associated citation potentially introduces another confounder. It is possible that generations that remain cited
at more abstractive OPs are those that are the easiest to cite (and thereby verify). This may confound T2V results
aggregated across properly covered generations by leading to lower relative T2Vs for more abstractive OPs.
Figure 8 shows that the time taken to verify entire cited responses, relative to those of the quoted OP, generally trends
upward alongside abstractiveness with the exception of the paraphrased OP. This elevated complexity of evaluating
entire paraphrased responses may be explained by paraphrased generations having similarly high word count as the
quoted generations, while lacking the convenience of inline quotation. In contrast, entailed and abstractive generations
have substantially lower word count than quoted generations (Table 5). Despite this, the verification times for entailed
and abstractive generations are either similar or greater than those of quoted generations in Figure 8.

Multiple choice options:

13.6.3

Gemini Generation Implementation Details

In our work, a Google Gemini generation was obtained by passing in an instruction (the same used for the GPT4 + Vertex generation) and the query. To obtain citations, we used the "Double-check response" feature on
https://gemini.google.com/app. Gemini citations include citations to supporting sources, highlighted in green, and to
conflicting sources, highlighted in orange. We only consider Gemini’s citations to supporting sources. Gemini provides
at most one citation per sentence in the response. Each citation corresponds to a sentence in a website; we scrape each
website and use a 1000 character span centered on the cited sentence as the citation’s source. The generations were
generated and scraped throughout August and September 2024.
