Abstract

arXiv:2509.23140v2 [cs.CL] 29 Sep 2026

Recent advancements have endowed Large Language Models with impressive general reasoning capabilities. However, these reasoning models often perform worse than non-reasoning
models on personalization tasks. While some
methods use outcome-based RL to improve personalization reasoning, they fail to supervise
the reasoning process. As a result, models may
reach correct answers through flawed reasoning chains, limiting further improvement. To
address this, we propose TagPR, a novel framework that adds semantic tags to the reasoning
process for step-by-step guidance. TagPR first
automatically generates a structured, tagged
dataset for Supervised Fine-Tuning. It then
employs a multi-stage RL process guided by
a composite reward signal, which integrates
tag-based process supervision with a novel Personalization Reward Model with User Embeddings to achieve fine-grained alignment with
user-specific logic. Extensive experiments on
public LaMP, LongLaMP, PGraphRAG, and a
self-constructed dataset demonstrate that our
approach achieves state-of-the-art results, delivering an average improvement of 32.65% over
the base model across all LaMP benchmark
tasks. Our work demonstrates that tag-guided
process supervision is an effective approach for
personalization reasoning.

Introduction

Research on large-model reasoning now spans
mathematics, coding, and multimodal domains (Guo et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026; Jin et al.,
2026b; Zhang et al., 2026), yet these advances
do not readily translate to personalization tasks.
This domain is increasingly critical, powering a
broad range of real-world applications, including
personalized recommendation, intelligent writing
assistance, targeted advertising, and user-tailored
conversational agents. Effective personalization

demands far more than generic logic. It requires
personalization reasoning: the ability to carefully
analyze a user’s historical data, infer their unique
preferences and thought patterns, and synthesize
this understanding to generate responses that are
precisely tailored to each user.
Surprisingly, even state-of-the-art reasoningcentric LLMs struggle in this domain, frequently
failing to surpass non-reasoning baselines on personalization benchmarks. This performance deficit
stems from a fundamental misalignment: models optimized for general-purpose reasoning inherently prioritize their internal, generalized logic over
the user’s profile. Consequently, they generate responses that are either overly generic or directly
contradict established user preferences. Recent pioneering works, such as R2P (Luo et al., 2025) and
RPM (Kim et al., 2026), have identified this limitation. Although these approaches mitigate the issue
by employing prompt templates or pre-constructed
reasoning paths, they function merely as external
scaffolds rather than fundamentally improving the
model’s intrinsic capacity for user-centric reasoning.
Our core motivation stems from the observation
that personalization reasoning is not a monolithic
act of intuition but a structured process with multiple steps involving the analysis of user history,
the identification of recurring patterns, and the application of those patterns to new contexts. The
opaque and unconstrained reasoning of standard
LLMs is poorly suited for this task. Furthermore,
existing RL-based approaches for eliciting personalization reasoning are often hampered by their
reliance on outcome-based rewards (Zhang et al.,
2025; Li et al., 2025). Such signals are inherently
sparse because a correct final answer does not necessarily imply a valid reasoning process, which
limits further improvement. The ability to provide
structured guidance signals within the model’s internal reasoning logic therefore becomes highly

instill the foundational grammar of structured and
personalized thought. Following this, we employ
a multi-stage RL process to refine this capability.

(PRMU), which explicitly aligns the model’s reasoning with user-specific logic. Our key contributions are threefold:
I. We formulate personalization reasoning as a
sequence of semantically tagged actions and introduce a data-driven pipeline to automatically generate and label reasoning chains with semantic tags.
II. We introduce a synergistic SFT and multistage RL training framework. This process is
guided by a unique composite reward signal that
integrates tag-based constraints and our novel Per-

sonalization Reward Model with User Embeddings
(PRMU) for fine-grained alignment with user logic.
III. We demonstrate through extensive experiments on public LaMP, LongLaMP, PGraphRAG

state-of-the-art results.

Related Work

Large Language Model Personalization LLM
personalization has evolved rapidly. A dominant approach is retrieval-augmented generation,
with innovations including feedback-optimized
retrieval (Salemi et al., 2024a) and generationcalibrated retrievers (Mysore et al., 2024).
PAG (Richardson et al., 2023) enhances retrieval by
integrating user history summarization. Beyond retrieval, research has explored core personalization
components (Wu et al., 2024). DPL (Qiu et al.,
2025) models inter-user differences to capture
unique preferences. Parameter-efficient approaches
include OPPU (Tan et al., 2024b) with user-specific
lightweight modules, PER-PCS (Tan et al., 2024a)
for collaborative PEFT sharing, plug-and-play user
embeddings (PPlug) (Liu et al., 2025), and HYDRA (Zhuang et al., 2024) for black-box personalization. Additional methods include multi-stage
decomposition (Li et al., 2023) and multi-objective
parameter merging (P-Soups) (Jang et al., 2023).
Recent work has further expanded personalization
research toward personalized user-agent simulation (Jin et al., 2025) and user-specific evaluation (Qiu et al., 2026).

approaches primarily use prompting strategies for

Methodology

This section presents the methodology for TagPR.
We formulate the task and overview the framework
in Section 3.1, detail the reasoning structure initialization in Section 3.2, and present the Tag-Guided
Reinforcement Learning in Section 3.3.
3.1

Task Formulation & Overview

We define personalized reasoning as the task of generating a user-specific response y to a query x, conu
ditioned on the user’s profile Pu = {(xi , yi )}N
i=1 ,
where Nu is the total number of historical records
available for user u, and each record (xi , yi ) is a
past input–output pair. To incorporate reasoning
into this process, the task is extended to first generate an explicit reasoning chain c before producing
the final response y. Conditioned on the query x
and a relevant profile subset pu ⊆ Pu , the objective for a model parameterized by θ is to maximize
the joint likelihood of the reasoning chain and the

response:
p(c, y|x, pu ; θ) = p(c|x, pu ; θ) · p(y|c, x, pu ; θ). (1)

The core challenge is to ensure that the reasoning
chain c is coherent and faithful to the user’s profile
pu , while the response y remains consistent with
this explicit reasoning.
The overall TagPR framework addresses this
challenge through two sequential phases. First,
a lightweight Reasoning Structure Initialization
stage injects the syntactic grammar of tagged reasoning into the model via a small amount of highquality supervised data. Second, a Tag-Guided
Reinforcement Learning stage refines the model’s
personalization reasoning through tag-based process supervision and fine-grained user-aware reward signals.

used to build training resources (Jin et al., 2026a).

randomly sample 1,000 instances from each of the

2024b), employing Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking2507 (Yang et al., 2025) to generate 16 candidate

only correctly answered samples. Second, an LLM

these reasoning chains on logic, factual accuracy,
completeness, and conciseness. Only instances
scoring >15 were retained for subsequent annotation (Prompt in Appendix E.1.1).
The filtered reasoning chains then underwent

3.3

Tag-Guided Reinforcement Learning

3.3.1 Tag-Based Process Supervision
By compelling the model to externalize its logic
into discrete, semantically tagged steps, we transform the opaque internal reasoning process into an
observable sequence that can be directly supervised.
This resolves the degeneracy of outcome-only rewards, which cannot distinguish between a model
that carefully analyzes user history and one that
ignores it but guesses correctly.
The Tag Reward Rtag enforces both the structural
and semantic correctness of the tagged reasoning
chain. It operates as a penalty-based signal that
applies a set of logical checks to the generated
chain c:

The logical checks include three verifications: (1)
format verification, ensuring that each tag is syntactically well-formed with correct opening and
closing delimiters; (2) vocabulary verification, ensuring that every tag used belongs to the predefined set of 9 primary tags established during data
construction; and (3) coverage verification, ensuring that the reasoning chain meets a minimum tag

count, preventing degenerate solutions that satisfy
format requirements with a single trivial tag. Together, these checks enforce a cognitive structure
on the model’s thought process, guiding it along a
principled chain of thought.
3.3.2 Fine-Grained Alignment via PRMU
While tag-based supervision effectively guides the
model toward a principled personalization reasoning paradigm, fine-grained alignment with individual user preferences requires an additional useraware signal. To provide this signal, we introduce
the Personalization Reward Model with User Embeddings (PRMU).
Unlike standard reward model architectures,
PRMU incorporates learnable user embeddings Eu
to capture individual preferences. This architectural modification enables it to provide a granular
reward signal that prioritizes reasoning which is
not only accurate but also highly tailored to the
user’s profile. PRMU is trained on two preference datasets (∼10k samples each). The ProfileReasoning Preference (PRP) dataset contrasts responses generated with a user profile (preferred)
against those generated without (rejected), teaching the model to value profile utilization. The
Personalized-Quality Preference (PQP) dataset contains pairs of personalized responses where preference is determined by correctness or ROUGE
score, thereby training the model to discern reasoning quality among personalized outputs.
Initialized from Skywork-Reward-V2-Qwen30.6B (Liu et al., 2026), our PRMU architecture first
maps a user ID idu to its corresponding embedding
Eu . This embedding, along with the query, profile, and reasoning chain, is processed to produce a
scalar logit. Both the base reward model parameters θr and the user embeddings E are jointly optimized by minimizing the Bradley-Terry (Bradley
and Terry, 1952) preference loss:

4.1

Experimental Setup

Implementation Details We employ Qwen3-8B
as our base model. Our training process consists

of SFT on the dataset described in Section 3.2, followed by a two-stage RL phase using data sampled
from the LaMP training set. We evaluate our model
on the LaMP benchmark, a standard for assessing
personalization, reporting results on its validation
set as the test set is not public.
Baselines We conduct a comprehensive comparison against a wide spectrum of baselines. These
include: (1) standard methodologies such as Zeroshot, RAG, PAG (Richardson et al., 2023), SFT,
SFT-Ind, and their reasoning-enhanced variants (R); (2) advanced personalization (PPlug (Liu et al.,

The results, presented in Table 1, demonstrate that

To isolate the efficacy of our framework, we
first conduct an ablation study comparing TagPR
against a RAG-R (Base) method. This baseline

reasoning capabilities.

outperforms larger proprietary models evaluated
without task-specific training. For instance, on the

4.3

Ablation Study

To dissect the contribution of each component
within our framework, we conducted a comprehensive ablation study, with results presented in
Table 1. Our analysis reveals a clear incremental
contribution from each module.
The results first highlight the critical roles of the
foundational training stages. The initial SFT phase
is essential for bootstrapping the model with our
tagged reasoning syntax, and its removal (TagPR
w/o SFT) leads to a noticeable performance drop.
Building upon this, the multi-stage RL is vital for
refining this structure into high-quality personalized logic, and the SFT-only model (TagPR w/o
RL) leaves a considerable gap to the full model.
Comparable results between TagPR w/o RL and
SFT-R indicate that tags yield no measurable gain
during SFT alone, but become effective as process
supervision during RL.
Furthermore, our novel reward signals prove effective. The PRMU reward provides a crucial useraware signal, and its removal (TagPR w/o PRMU)
leads to a consistent decline across all tasks. Regarding the tag-based reward, note that TagPR w/o

Tagging replaces the 9 personalization-specific tags
with generic unstructured reasoning, while TagPR
w/o Tag-R retains the tags but removes their associated reward. Both variants underperform the
full model, confirming that the semantic content of
the personalization-specific tags and their process
supervision together drive consistent gains. Finally,
our two-stage training design is validated as superior to a single, continuous RL stage (TagPR
w/o Exp-RL). Collectively, these findings affirm
that the synergistic integration of each carefully
designed component is the key to TagPR’s success.

4.6.1

Personalization Reward Model Design

To validate our proposed PRMU, we conducted
a comprehensive ablation study to assess the contribution of its core components. Table 3 compares four configurations: our full PRMU, PRMU
without user embeddings (w/o UE), a baseline using an untrained reward model (Untrained RM),
and a baseline with no reward model (w/o RM).
Our findings first reveal that employing an off-

the-shelf reward model offers no consistent advantage over having no reward model at all. In
fact, it proved detrimental in certain cases, yielding
a noisy and misaligned signal. This underscores
the necessity of fine-tuning the reward model on
domain-specific data. Next, training the reward
model on our personalization dataset, even without
user-specific information (PRMU w/o UE), yields
improvements across all metrics. The most significant performance gains are realized with the full
PRMU model. By integrating user embeddings to
provide a user-aware reward, PRMU consistently
outperforms all other variants.

input understanding over immediate conclusions.

5

Conclusion

In this work, we presented TagPR, a novel framework designed to bridge the fundamental gap between general reasoning and personalization reasoning in LLMs. To achieve this, we developed a
synergistic training paradigm starting with a datadriven pipeline for SFT to instill tagged reasoning
syntax. This is followed by a multi-stage RL process. The initial guided phase utilizes a composite reward that features tag-based process supervision and our novel Personalization Reward Model
with User Embeddings. A subsequent exploratory
phase is then applied to refine core performance.
Extensive evaluations across LaMP, LongLaMP,
PGraphRAG, and a self-constructed benchmark

demonstrate that TagPR achieves state-of-the-art
performance. Ultimately, our work proves that explicit tag-guided process supervision is an effective
strategy for eliciting genuine personalization reasoning in large language models.

6

Limitations & Potential Risks

Despite TagPR’s promising results, several limitations and risks remain. Our nine-tag vocabulary is
derived from reasoning chains in LaMP and may
therefore reflect benchmark-specific reasoning patterns. Although it generalizes in our experiments,
its fixed scope may limit the model’s flexibility in
handling highly complex or open-ended reasoning
scenarios that require dynamic structure adaptation.
The use of learnable user embeddings and deep personalization introduces potential privacy concerns
regarding user data retention and the risk of reinforcing existing user biases or creating echo chambers, necessitating further research into privacypreserving and alignment-focused personalization.

A.1

Implementation Details

Backbone Model We use Qwen3-8B (Yang et al.,
2025) as our base model for all experiments unless
otherwise specified.
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) The SFT stage
was conducted on 8 A100 GPUs. We used a learning rate of 1e-5 and a global batch size of 64. The
model was trained for 2 epochs on the dataset described in Section 3.2.
Reinforcement Learning (RL) Data Sampling:
We sampled data from the LaMP training set for
RL. Specifically, we randomly sampled 1,024 examples for each of the LaMP-1, LaMP-3, LaMP-4,
LaMP-5, and LaMP-7 tasks. For the more challenging LaMP-2 task, we sampled 3,200 examples.
Training Parameters: The first RL stage was trained
for 13 epochs, and the second stage was trained for
2 epochs. Both stages were conducted on 8 A100
GPUs with a global batch size of 128 and a learning
rate of 1e-6. Policy Rollout: During the policy rollout stage, we set the temperature to 1.0 and top-p to
1.0, generating 5 responses for each prompt. Other
Hyperparameters: The low and high clip ratios for
the GSPO algorithm were set to 0.0003 and 0.0004,
respectively. For the repetition penalty reward, we
used n-grams of size 4. For the tag reward, the
minimum required number of tags was set to 3.
A.2

Benchmark Details

Dataset We use the LaMP benchmark, a widelyadopted benchmark for evaluating the personalization capabilities of LLMs. It requires models to
analyze user historical profiles to answer current
queries. Since the official test set is not publicly
available, all our evaluations are conducted on the
official validation set. LaMP-6 was excluded from
our evaluation due to its unavailability. We evaluated on the complete validation dataset for all
other tasks. The detailed data statistics of LaMP
are shown in Table 4.
Evaluation Metrics Following the original LaMP
benchmark, we employ the following metrics:
Classification Tasks (LaMP-1 & LaMP-2). Task
Descriptions: LaMP-1 (Personalized Citation): A
binary classification task where the model deter-

mines which of two candidate references an author is likely to cite based on their profile. LaMP2 (Personalized Movie Tagging): A multi-class
classification task requiring the model to select
the most appropriate tag from 15 candidates for a
movie, conditioned on the user’s tagging history.
Metrics & Calculation: We report Accuracy and
F1-Score. For Accuracy, we calculate the ratio
of correctly predicted samples to the total samples. For F1-Score: In LaMP-1 (binary), we report
the standard F1 for the positive class. In LaMP-2
(multi-class), we follow the benchmark standard
and report Macro-F1.
Rating Task (LaMP-3). Task Description:
LaMP-3 (Personalized Product Rating) is an ordinal classification task. The model predicts a
specific integer rating (1–5 stars) for a product
based on the user’s historical reviews. Metrics: We
employ MAE (Mean Absolute Error) and RMSE
(Root Mean Square Error). These metrics are standard for quantifying the deviation between predicted scores and ground-truth user ratings.
Text Generation Tasks (LaMP-4, 5, 7). Task
Descriptions: These tasks involve generating text
tailored to a user’s specific style: LaMP-4: Personalized news headline generation. LaMP-5: Personalized scholarly title generation. LaMP-7: Personalized tweet paraphrasing. Metrics: We utilize
ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L. ROUGE-1: measures
the overlap of unigrams to assess content adequacy.
ROUGE-L: is based on the Longest Common Subsequence, evaluating fluency and structural similarity between the generated text and the reference.
A.3

Baselines and Comparison Setup

To rigorously evaluate our proposed method, we
benchmark it against a wide spectrum of baselines.
For a fair comparison, all methods are built upon
the Qwen3-8B base model and utilize the user’s
8 most recent profiles as input, unless specified
otherwise (e.g., proprietary models like GPT-4o).
The baselines are categorized as follows. Standard methodologies include Zero-shot, which gen-

erates responses without user profiles as a nonpersonalized lower bound; standard RetrievalAugmented Generation (RAG); PersonalizationAugmented Generation (PAG) (Richardson et al.,
2023), which enhances RAG with user history summaries; Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on the full
dataset; and SFT-Ind, which is fine-tuned only on
individual task data. Reasoning-enhanced variants of these methods, denoted with a ‘-R‘ suffix,
are also included. We further compare against advanced techniques. Personalization-focused methods include PPlug (Liu et al., 2025), a plug-andplay approach using specialized user embeddings,
and HYDRA-Adapter (Zhuang et al., 2024), for
which we use only its adapter version to maintain a
consistent retrieval method for fairness. Reasoningfocused baselines include R2P (Luo et al., 2025),
which employs hierarchical reasoning templates,
and PrLM (Zhang et al., 2025), which uses a contrastive reward model with reinforcement learning.
To situate our method’s performance against the
frontier of language models, we also include several leading state-of-the-art LLMs: GPT-4o (Hurst

B.1

Model Training Cost

The additional training cost relative to SFT-R arises
primarily from multi-stage RL optimization. As
shown in Table 5, TagPR requires 169.7 A100
GPU-hours, compared with 177.7 A100 GPUhours for the untagged RL baseline and 8.0 A100
GPU-hours for SFT-R. Thus, TagPR requires more
computation than SFT alone but remains comparable in cost to an otherwise equivalent RL-based
method.
B.2

PRMU Reward Model Evaluation

We evaluate the standalone preference prediction
accuracy of PRMU variants on a held-out set. As

shown in Table 6, the full PRMU with user embeddings achieves the best accuracy (80.45%), substantially outperforming an off-the-shelf reward model

Reward Function

We conducted a sensitivity analysis on the reward
function hyperparameters by varying the weights

within a reasonable range of parameter variations,

F

New Constructed Personalization
Benchmark

To evaluate zero-shot, cross-lingual generalization,
we built a benchmark from Dianping, a prominent
Chinese user-generated content platform. This appendix details its construction.
F.1

Data and User Profile Creation

We collected public posts from Dianping and applied rigorous filtering to retain high-quality content, removing short posts, duplicates, and advertisements. From this cleaned dataset, we selected
1,000 users with extensive post histories.
For each user, a profile representing their personal writing style was constructed from their 8
most recent posts (title and content). The 9th most
recent post was held out as the ground truth for our
evaluation tasks, ensuring a strict zero-shot setting
where the test data is unseen.
F.2

Task Formulation

The benchmark consists of three distinct tasks, with
one instance per user for each task, totaling 3,000
evaluation instances. All tasks are conditioned on
the user’s 8-post profile. As in the LaMP dataset,
we use the ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L metrics for
evaluation.
Dianping-Content (Title → Content): Given
the title of the held-out post, the model must generate the full post content in the user’s specific style.
Dianping-Title (Content → Title): The inverse
task, where the model generates a stylistically appropriate title from the held-out post’s content.
Dianping-Paraph (Generic → Stylized Post):
This task measures stylistic transfer. For each
user’s held-out post, we first used a general-purpose
LLM (GPT-4o) to generate a neutral, generic version based on the original content. The model’s
task is to rewrite this generic text to match the
user’s unique style, with the user’s original post as
the target.
