# Failure-mode taxonomy

A single score says how good an agent's report is, not where it went wrong. A failure-mode taxonomy answers the second question. It is a codebook of error categories drawn from the agent's own execution records, grouped so that each error can be placed at a stage of the work. The paper calls its instance DEFT and presents it as "the first failure taxonomy developed" for deep research agents.[^c1]

The construction is a sequence of coding passes, borrowed from grounded theory. The paper says it constructs "a comprehensive failure taxonomy to systematically identify, categorize, and interpret the underlying" causes of agent errors, and that it is built through "a human-AI collaborative" process.[^c2] Open coding has five LLM coders propose concept names into a shared codebook; near-duplicates, flagged by embedding similarity, are "merged where" appropriate.[^c3] Axial coding relates the concepts and is checked by inter-coder reliability rounds, after which "we finalized 14 axial categories."[^c4] Selective coding then collapses them into three core categories, "ultimately distilling three core categories" of Reasoning, Retrieval and Generation.[^c5] Those three are presented as a loop: "these three core categories form a complete closed-loop for agent" task execution.[^c6]

The taxonomy gives a profile per stage. In the paper's counts, the generation stage holds the largest share, with strategic content fabrication at 18.95% of coded failures, a retrieval category "External Information Acquisition (2-1-IIA, 16.30%)," and a reasoning category "Lack of Analytical Depth (1-2-LAD, 11.09%)."[^c7][^c8][^c9] To make the categories comparable across models, the paper introduces "a positive performance metric that transforms model error counts in each category into a bounded, interpretable" score.[^c10] Its exact form is not legible in the text I read, so I do not state it. The point is that a model is then compared stage by stage. Here the paper reports that Kimi K2 and O4-Mini are strong on reasoning but weaker on generation, a pattern a single report score would hide.[^c15] This is discussed in [[reasoning-resilience]], and the generation stage's largest category is [[strategic-content-fabrication]].

The taxonomy sits beside other process-level scoring. [[process-report-alignment-evaluation]] scores the research process and checks whether the report reflects it, which asks a related question from another side. The paper also reports a checklist measure beside the taxonomy, described as "meta-reasoning and procedural adherence to the intended research" workflow.[^c11] A checklist counts what the report contains, and the taxonomy says what kind of failure produced what is missing; [[threshold-gated-rubric-coverage]] shows how a checklist-style count can be gated.

## Limits

- The codebook is generated by LLM coders, and human checks are on small samples. The inter-coder check used "a randomly sampled subset" of 24 or 54 records per round, with three domain experts.[^c12] The taxonomy's saturation check used "36 execution records" from two agents not involved in building it.[^c13]
- The taxonomy identifies failure modes and does not localize causes. The paper states the aim as "identifying and labeling failure modes rather than conducting deep causal analysis."[^c14] A category says what went wrong, not why.
- The percentages are shares of coded errors within one benchmark of 100 tasks and about 1,000 reports. They are not error rates per report, and they depend on which agents were sampled.
- The taxonomy is a classification. The paper's advice that constraints and verifications should extend into generation is a recommendation drawn from it, and the text does not show that acting on a category improves an agent (my inference).
- Category boundaries are inherited from the coders' prompts and seed concepts. A different codebook from a different coder set would likely cut the same failures differently; the paper does not test this.
