Abstract

We use a two-agent debate [12, 24] as an environment to study confidence composition. Debate creates a setting with multiple correlated answer streams and confidence signals, in which
agents may revise their answers through interactions. Prior work on multiagent debate has used
confidence to trigger debate or resolve disagreements [26, 14, 4, 13], but has not formulated a final
confidence for the multiagent system. They do not
specify how confidence should be assigned in cases
such as when agents agree, disagree, revise their
answers after interaction, or produce several intermediate outputs from which the system selects its
final answer. Also, reporting the confidence of a
single agent ignores evidence from the rest of the
multiagent process.
We study two methods to compose confidence.
The first is Weighted Stream Voting (WSV), a
soft-aggregation protocol that lets all answerconfidence streams vote for candidate labels after
validation-fitted calibration and weighting, making the confidences more comparable. The second is Confidence-Gated Fusion (CGF), which
first determines, for each agent, whether its postdebate answer is sufficiently confident to replace
its zero-shot answer, and then combines the surviving streams in log-odds space. We also consider an asymmetric variant, A-CGF, which triggers one-sided persuasion when the agents’ initial
confidences are highly imbalanced. All calibration functions and the aggregation parameters are
optimized on a validation set.
We evaluate across five benchmarks, 30 homogeneous and heterogeneous model pairs, and two
qualitatively different confidence estimators, sequence probability and verbalized self-report. CGF
and A-CGF improve system-level confidence discrimination over zero-shot, standard debate, and
debate-on-disagreement baselines in all twelve
comparisons (pHolm ≤ .0016). WSV is also competitive, particularly on MMLU. The gains hold

Multiagent language model systems, such as
collaborative reasoning and debate, produce
multiple correlated candidate answers and confidence signals. However, these signals are
usually calibrated only at the individual agent
level, and provide no principled confidence estimate for the system’s final answer. We formulate this as a confidence composition problem:
combining confidence across agents and reasoning stages while preserving both selective
utility and probabilistic reliability. We study
confidence-aware routing and log-odds pooling
protocols that select among candidate answers
and output a system-level confidence. Across
five benchmarks, 30 heterogeneous and homogeneous model pairs, and two confidence estimators, our gated-fusion methods improve
AUARC and reduce Brier score over single
agent, standard debate, and selective debate
baselines, while retaining competitive weighted
F1-score as a correctness metric. We further
show that our log-odds fusion is overconfident
due to correlated intermediate signals. We propose a shared dependence discount that substantially improves reliability while preserving
predictions.

Introduction

Confidence estimation has become prominent
throughout NLP tasks by facilitating selective prediction, human oversight, self-correction [44], selfconsistency [36], routing [26], and reasoning [35].
However, existing confidence estimators are designed for a single model and a single response,
and not for the final response of a collaborative
process [27, 38, 29]. This limitation is increasingly
important as language model systems rely on multiple models and reasoning stages, producing several
intermediate answers and confidence signals before aggregating them into a final response. In this
work, we study the composition of confidence in
multiagent settings.

Latent-information methods use quantities such as
token log-probabilities, sequence likelihood, entropy, or other distributional statistics [27, 5]. Verbalization methods ask the model to report a numerical confidence or likelihood in natural language
[38, 42]. Consistency-based methods estimate uncertainty from agreement across multiple sampled
generations [29]. These methods are defined and
studied for a single model output.
A confidence signal is useful when it matches
empirical correctness (Calibrated) and discriminates between correct and incorrect samples. A
predictor is calibrated if predictions assigned confidence p are correct with frequency p, and Expected
Calibration Error (ECE) estimates this mismatch
by binning examples by confidence and measuring
the weighted gap between bin accuracy and bin
confidence [18]. Discrimination asks whether the
confidence ranking separates correct from incorrect predictions, which is the property needed for
selective prediction. We also report the Brier score,
a proper scoring rule defined as the mean squared
error between confidence and correctness.

Related Work
2.2

Combining several probability estimates into one
prediction is a classical problem in statistics. Opinion pooling studies how beliefs from multiple
sources should be aggregated into a single probability distribution, with common approaches including linear pooling, which averages probabilities,
and logarithmic pooling, which combines probabilities in log space [16, 10]. Similar ideas appear
in ensemble learning, where model predictions are
combined through voting, probability averaging,
stacking, or product-of-experts rules to improve
robustness or predictive quality [39, 20]. These
methods differ in the assumptions they make about
the sources being combined. Simple averaging returns a compromise between the input probabilities,
while logarithmic or product-based rules increase
confidence when multiple sources support the same
answer.
2.1

Confidence Driven Multiagent Debate

Multiagent debate is a common collaborative reasoning framework in which multiple LLM agents
exchange arguments before producing final answers [12, 24]. Debate is often motivated by the
possibility that interaction can expose errors, diversify reasoning, and reduce premature commitment
to an incorrect answer. Recent work suggests that
the gains from multiagent debate are unstable and
often come more from aggregation than from the
debate’s information exchange itself [7, 41, 48, 28].
Several recent methods bring confidence into debate. Some expose each agent’s confidence, allowing other agents or an aggregator to weight claims
by reliability [26, 45, 4]. Others use confidence as
a routing signal to decide whether debate should
be triggered [14, 13], or study whether deliberation improves the calibration of individual agents
[43, 34].

Dependence Correction. While naive fusion
treats the two surviving streams as conditionally
independent, multiagent interaction inevitably introduces information redundancy and correlated
errors. To account for this dependence, we introduce a shared discount parameter w ∈ (0, 1] that
scales the total log-odds evidence: cdiscounted =
σ (w(ℓ1 + ℓ2 )) for agreement, and cdiscounted =
σ (w|ℓ1 − ℓ2 |) for disagreement.
w < 1 indicates that the two agents partly repeat
the same evidence, so they do not overcount their
combined confidence excessively. For CGF, using
the same positive w for both streams also preserves
ordering. If example A had more fused evidence
than example B before discounting, it still has more
after multiplying both by w. That is why we expect no change in discrimination (AUARC), while
calibration (ECE and Brier) improves. Mathematically, this can be viewed as a post hoc temperature
calibration, with T = 1/w.

Confidence-Gated Fusion (CGF)

CGF is a two step setup. The first step is confidence gating, in which each agent independently
decides whether to retain its zero-shot prediction or
switch to its debated prediction. Then the protocol
performs a probability fusion on the two surviving
streams.
I. Confidence Gating. In the first step, the transition to the debated output is allowed if the debate process increases that agent’s confidence by
more than a learned threshold τi in logit space:
logit(ci,1 ) − logit(ci,0 ) > τi . Upon a significant increase in confidence, the model will report
{yi,1 , ci,1 }; otherwise, {yi,0 , ci,0 }.
Asymmetric-CGF (A-CGF) is another variant of
this setup; rather than conducting a full symmetric debate across all cases, we initiate a one-sided
debate (or persuasion step) whenever a large initial confidence imbalance exists between agents:
|logit(c1,0 ) − logit(c2,0 )| > γ. In this regime, the
significantly more confident agent presents its rationale to convince the less confident agent, while
symmetric debate is reserved for cases with initially
balanced confidence. Following this interaction, ACGF applies the exact same confidence gating.

another family of confidence composition with our
CGF method. WSV treats the four (agent, stream)
outputs as independent pieces of evidence about
the true label. Each piece contributes a learned

the protocol computes a score by summing the
weighted logit-confidences (wi,s · logit(pi,s )) of
all streams that predicted yk . The final prediction is the candidate with the highest score, and
the routed confidence is softmax-normalized to
prevent inflated confidence. w1,0 , w2,0 , w1,1 , w2,1

We explore the self-report and sequence probability
families of confidence estimators. Our self-report
confidence estimator elicits a verbalized score by
prompting the model to rate its certainty after producing its answer, and then averaging the results
over three repetitions with a slightly higher model
temperature (0.3) [38, 42]. The sequence probability estimator computes the mean log-likelihood
over the generated token sequence, normalized by
length to control for output length bias [25], followed by a token-bias correction. Bias correction
step adjusts sequence probability by subtracting
the model’s content-free prior log Pθ (ŷ | xnull )
from the conditional score: C(x, ŷ) = log Pθ (ŷ |
x) − log Pθ (ŷ | xnull ), measuring how much input
x increases the likelihood of ŷ beyond baseline,
attempting to measure the abstract meaning of the
answer rather than the tokens probability [47, 22].

We fix λBrier = λanchor = 0.1 across all experiments. The Brier term discourages routing solutions that improve correctness but produce miscalibrated outputs, and the anchor term shrinks thresholds toward neutral defaults to prevent overfitting
on the small validation split. We use separate validation data for the initial calibration and parameter
optimization.

Experimental Setup

Models and Debater Pairs. We evaluate fourteen open-source models across five backbone
families: LLaMA [17] (3.2-3B, 3.1-8B), Mistral
(Mistral-7B, Ministral-8B), Phi [1] (3-Medium14B, 4-Mini-3.8B, 4-14B), Gemma [37] (2-9B, 34B, 3-12B), and Qwen [3] (2.5-7B, 2.5-14B, 2.532B, 3-4B), all instruction-tuned variants. We first
evaluate each model individually on all five benchmarks and record zero-shot performance on the
validation split. For each benchmark, we select homogeneous and heterogeneous pairs that differ in
capability. We chose both pairs with similar zeroshot accuracy and pairs with one high and one low
accuracy to ensure our results are not tied to any
particular capability gap or size regime. Selected
pairs and their initial performances can be found in
Appendix Table A.1.

Confidence Calibration For all confidencecomposition methods and baselines, each model’s
raw confidence is first passed through a beta calibration step. The calibrator is fitted to a small
validation. Its role is to transform raw confidence
scores into probabilities that better match empirical
correctness before the protocol combines them.
Beta calibration is a post-hoc parametric
method that applies an affine transformation to the
log-odds of the raw probabilities [23]. For binary
probability inputs p, the calibrated score is formulated as: c̃ = σ(a ln p − b ln(1 − p) + c) where σ
is the Sigmoid function, a, b ≥ 0, and c are scalar
parameters. The transformation learns to minimize
the negative log-likelihood (NLL) to predict the
sample’s correctness.

spanning four task types to cover a variety of tasks
with varying levels of ambiguity. For scientific

4.1

Statistical Significance. We assess whether a
protocol consistently improves over each baseline using exact one-sided paired sign tests. Each
model-pair and dataset setting provides one paired
observation. The sign test is a nonparametric
test designed for paired comparisons in which improvement magnitudes are not directly comparable
across settings [11]. It uses only the direction of
the difference, which is appropriate here because
a gain on one benchmark need not have the same
numerical meaning as an equally sized gain on another benchmark.
Within each of the 30 settings, we evaluate the
protocol and baseline on the same held-out test examples and compute the difference in their metrics.
A setting favors the protocol when this difference
is positive. The sign test then asks whether positive differences occur more often than expected
by chance across the 30 settings. We run the analysis separately for sequence probability and selfreported confidence. For each metric, we test CGF
and A-CGF against ZS, MAD, and MAD-D under
both confidence estimators, giving 12 comparisons.
We control the family-wise error rate across these
comparisons using Holm’s procedure [21].

Evaluation

Our primary confidence-quality metric is AUARC,
the area under the accuracy–rejection curve.
AUARC measures whether a system ranks correct
predictions above incorrect ones. It is computed by
progressively rejecting the least confident predictions and measuring the accuracy of the retained
predictions at each coverage level. Higher AUARC
indicates a more useful confidence signal for selective prediction.
We measure calibration using expected calibration error (ECE) and Brier score. ECE partitions
predictions into equal-width confidence bins and
computes the bin-size-weighted absolute difference
between the mean confidence and empirical accuracy in each bin [18]. Lower ECE indicates that
reported confidence more closely matches observed
accuracy.
Unlike ECE, which measures the mismatch between confidence and empirical accuracy, Brier[6]
is a proper scoring rule that reflects both calibration
and the useful variation in confidence across correct
and incorrect answers[31]. Brier score is the mean
squared difference between each confidence value
and its binary correctness label, so lower values
indicate more accurate probabilistic predictions.
We report both raw metrics and their
temperature-calibrated versions, t-ECE and
t-Brier. We fit a temperature tuning to each
system’s output confidences on the validation split
by minimizing the negative log-likelihood, and
apply it unchanged to the test split. Temperature

Calibration and Correction for Dependence Assumption Table C.1 shows that CGF and A-CGF
have higher raw ECE than MAD and MAD-D, even
though all input streams are beta-calibrated. The
reason is that MAD and MAD-D simply select one
calibrated stream, while WSV, CGF, and A-CGF
transform calibrated inputs through aggregation or
fusion. After one-parameter output temperature
scaling, t-ECE and t-Brier return to baseline-level
or better values in most settings.
Naive log-odds fusion treats the surviving confidence streams as conditionally independent, even
though interactions make them correlated. We correct this scale error by fitting a shared discount
factor w on validation data and applying it to the
pooled log-odds evidence. Across 30 model pairs,
two confidence estimators, and two fused protocols,
the fitted value was below 1 in 118 of 120 settings,
with a median w∗ of 0.65. Thus, the validation data
consistently prefer counting the fused evidence at
roughly two-thirds of the strength implied by independence.
The correction improves calibration without
changing the final decisions. For CGF, it also preserves the confidence ranking almost exactly, with
maximum absolute AUARC change of 0.00148,
while raw ECE drops from 0.100 to 0.055 under
sequence probability and from 0.112 to 0.063 under self-report. Raw Brier improves as well, and
Figure 2 shows the same pattern visually: CGF remains on the AUARC identity line, while raw ECE
and Brier move below it after discounting. Because
this discount is equivalent to an output temperature
with T = 1/w, t-ECE and t-Brier change little after
discounting; the main cost of the violated independence assumption is a one-scalar confidence-scale
error.

Conclusion

Multiagent, multi-round language model systems
produce intermediate responses and confidence signals at different stages of the process. Using a
two-agent debate as a controlled environment, we
studied how these correlated signals can be combined into a single confidence estimate for the final
answer. We evaluated two families of solutions:
weighted voting over agent outputs across rounds
of debate (WSV), and log-odds probability fusion
applied after each model revises its belief under a
confidence condition (CGF and A-CGF).
We provided evidence that reporting the confidence of the debate’s winning stream is miscalibrated and substantially less discriminative between correct and incorrect samples because it ignores evidence from the other model and the earlier debate round. Our confidence composition
methods showed significantly higher discrimination (AUARC) and better probability quality (tBrier). WSV is strongest on the least ambiguous
task but less consistent across settings. These gains
do not come at the cost of task accuracy; our protocols avoid the large F1 losses that unrestricted debate incurs on the more ambiguous labeling tasks.

Limitations

This work is limited to the two-agent, one-round
setting, as it is the most controlled setting for studying confidence composition. Two-agent debate is
the common minimal form of multiagent interaction and has a relatively low inference cost. We
therefore analyze this setting in depth, using 30
model pairs and tasks with varying degrees of ambiguity. Additionally, the main components of
our approach can extend beyond this setup. Confidence gating can be applied at later rounds by
deciding whether each new response should replace the previous one, and the log-odds fusion
rule can extend to more streams (e.g. σ(logit(c1 ) +
logit(c2 ) + logit(c3 ))). However, moving to three
or more agents also introduces many additional
design choices in the debate topology, such as
whether all agents interact symmetrically, whether
one agent acts as a judge or sub-agent, and how
information flows across rounds. These choices
substantially expand the experimental space, so we
leave a systematic study of larger and multi-round
systems to future work.

A.1

Computational Budget and
Infrastructure

We evaluate on five publicly available English
benchmarks spanning four task types. Table A.2
reports the task and split sizes used in our experiments. For every dataset, we use a held-out validation split to fit calibrators and learn routing thresholds, and report all results on the test split; the
validation split is never used for evaluation. The
training set is never used in general. BoolQ and
HatEval do not have a validation set; we used a random sample of 1000 from the test set as validation
(random state=42). Additionally, MMLU was used
with its college samples.
All datasets are standard research benchmarks
used in accordance with their intended research
use.
The code repository will be public upon publication.

All of our experiments are inference-only; we
do not perform any model training or fine-tuning.
The only learning step is the lightweight optimization of per-stream calibrators and routing thresholds, which runs on CPU and adds negligible
cost. Model inference was conducted on a single NVIDIA H200 NVL (141GB) GPU. The full
set of experiments (fourteen models across five
benchmarks, six debater pairs per benchmark, with
self-consistency sampling of three generations per
query) can be reproduced on a single NVIDIA
H200 GPU in 15 days. Parameter counts for all
models follow their officially released sizes (3B–
32B), listed in the Experimental setup section.
