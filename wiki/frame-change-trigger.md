# Frame change trigger

> On Kelly & Gero's hypothesis a frame changes when something **occasions**
> it rather than when its holder schedules it — four occasions, of which only
> one is produced by the inquiry's own bookkeeping. None of the four is
> tested.

## The hypothesis

Kelly & Gero (*Design Science* 8:e30, 2022), arguing that design research[^s1]
lacks a cognitive model of framing, set out what such a model would have to
explain: why and how a design frame changes during activity. They offer four
occasions, in their own words "four examples of how this may occur can be
hypothesised as follows":

1. **Endogenous to the frame** — the frame, as part of the action it calls
   for, requires changes to the situation.
2. **Endogenous to the situation** — the situation, being a complex
   conceptual structure, becomes incoherent in some way (they give
   self-contradiction as the example), and the conceptual system changes its
   structure in response.
3. **Endogenous to the person** — a hedonic reward for a level of novelty
   makes a static situation unlikely (they cite Saunders & Gero's 2004 curious
   agents); other embodied responses, such as disgust, may also force a
   change.
4. **Exogenous** — perceived changes in the environment introduce new
   concepts into the situation: interaction with other beings, interpretation
   of a representation, or any other environmental trigger.

These are hypotheses inside a call for a grounded cognitive model. The review
tests none of them and claims no exhaustiveness.

## Why it matters

Axes holds generators that *produce* candidate axes — from the
informant ([[grand-tour-question]]), from mutating what is held
([[metamorphic-probes]]), from conditioning the generator ([[premortem]]) —
and nothing that says *when* a working axis set is replaced. This taxonomy is
a candidate answer, and it has two consequences for a questioning procedure.

**Incoherence may be an occasion for restructuring the space, not only for
repairing its contents.** Every consistency note the wiki carries —
[[agm-belief-revision]], [[kernel-contraction]], [[truth-maintenance-system]]
— repairs a belief set inside a fixed language: something is retracted, and
the axes stay where they were. Occasion (2) says a self-contradiction can
instead be what makes the structure change. Nothing in the review says when to
do which, and nothing in the belief-revision literature the wiki holds
contemplates the second option at all; *reading occasion (2) as a clash among
received answers is this wiki's substitution* and it is what puts the two in
competition.

**Only part of the taxonomy is schedulable.** Occasion (1) is generated by the
inquiry's own commitments and is the one [[problem-solution-coevolution]]
already documents; (2) is generated by the answers already held; (3) and (4)
are properties of the asker and its environment that no questioning procedure
schedules. If the taxonomy holds, a procedure that treats reframing as a
planned step covers at most the first of four hypothesised occasions.

## Transfer

| | |
|---|---|
| **In its home field** | A designer's understanding of the problem — which concepts are in play and under what working principle — shifts during design activity; what changes is a situated structure held by a person acting on an artefact. |
| **Here** | The asker's axis set for $W$ is replaced mid-inquiry, and the four occasions become four places a questioning procedure could detect that its current dimensions are the wrong ones. |
| **What survives** | The partition by *origin* — the frame's own action requirements, an incoherence in what is already held, the holder's disposition, an intrusion from outside — and the consequence that only the first two are visible in the inquiry's record. |
| **What does not** | The situated-cognition substrate the taxonomy assumes. Occasion (3) rests on a hedonic novelty reward and on embodied affect, for which an asking procedure has no counterpart unless an exploration term is stipulated; and the "situation" that becomes incoherent in (2) is a grounded conceptual structure including perception and emotion, not a set of received answers, so the identification this note makes between the two is an import and not Kelly & Gero's claim. |

## Limits

- The four occasions are **explicitly hypothesised**, inside a call for a
  cognitive model that the paper says does not yet exist. There is no test, no
  exhaustiveness claim, and no way given to tell which occasion is acting in
  an observed episode.
- Occasion (3) rests on Saunders & Gero (2004), a computational model of
  novelty-seeking agents, cited in the review as an illustration and not read
  here [?]; it is not evidence about people.
- The *frame experiment* row in ## Known as reaches this note only through
  kelly-2022's report of Schön (1984), which was not read here [?]; if Schön's
  own text makes the frame experiment a response to a trigger rather than a
  component, that row's contrast weakens.
- The taxonomy presupposes the internal reading of a frame ([[design-frame]],
  reading (i)). Under the externalised reading a "change" is an edit to a
  document, and the four occasions do not partition it.
- **This note's own load-bearing claim** — that occasion (2) puts frame change
  in competition with belief repair — rests on identifying an incoherent
  situation with an inconsistent answer set. Kelly & Gero make no such
  identification; if the two are different objects, the competition
  disappears and only the schedulability point survives.
