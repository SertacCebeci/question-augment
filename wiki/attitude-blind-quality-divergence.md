# Attitude–blind-quality divergence

A judge of AI answers can be asked two different things: what they think of the source, or how good the answers are when the source is hidden. Those two measurements need not move together. A three-year mixed-methods study of philosophy scholars and students ran both on the same people: "We conducted a three-year (2023-2025) mixed-methods longitudinal study with 16".[^c1] The interviews traced a path from resistance toward a deeper objection: "to instrumental acceptance of the IUI as a tool, and finally to a deep, principled questioning of the".[^c2]

The blind side was built to keep the two apart. Each year, scholars rated anonymized answers, some written by human PhDs and professors and some generated by a language model, on a six-dimension rubric. The design aim is stated: "This blind rating process was designed to re-examine their attitudes objectively and prevent cross-contamination bias from knowing the information source."[^c3] Hiding the source is a way to keep a label such as "AI" from setting the score, which is the effect that [[disclosed-label-trust-effect]] reports for trust in health information.

The two series diverge. In 2023 the model's mean overall score was "lagging behind the human average of 3.45" (2.85 against 3.45).[^c4] By 2025 it was "achieving a mean overall score of 4.35 compared to the humans" (against 3.91).[^c5] Over the same years the attitude side moved the other way: "as the philosophers grew more familiar with AI and more critical of its capacity for genuine thought in principle" describes the interview trend.[^c6] The attitude scale was not monotone; it rose from 2023 to 2024 and then "dipping in 2025 as principled objections began to surface."[^c7] The rated quality did not dip. "the quality of AI-generated philosophical texts in blind-review experiments improved" steadily across the period.[^c8]

The gap is sharpest on the dimension the interviews called most human. The model "even surpassed the human average in Originality of Thought" in 2025.[^c9] So the question a judge answers when asked about an AI ("can it think?") and the question answered when rating a text blind ("is this text good?") can return opposite results on the same person.

What the divergence does to evaluation: a judge's stated view of a source is a poor guide to the quality of that source's output, and a blind rating is a separate measurement, not a confirmation of the view. Whoever reports only the attitude, or only the blind score, reports half of the relation. The paper names the tension itself: "This disconnect between the philosophers’ subjective, principled critique and the objective, demonstrated performance of the AI forms the central paradox" — the phrase is from the text, and the authors' interpretation of it is the next section's concern.

A second study has the source recognised rather than hidden, and finds a belief-linked gap anyway. Participants read two texts on one topic, one human-authored and one AI-generated, "without knowing whether they were written by a human or generated by AI."[^c17] Blinding did not keep the source out of view: about 90% of them named the AI text, and the authors conclude that "This means it is not difficult to identify AI writing."[^c18] The rating gap then tracked the rater's prior view. The authors report that people's evaluations of AI-generated texts "are significantly influenced by their preconceived views on the logical" reasoning ability of AI, and that "Their evaluations are not grounded in objective criteria; instead, they reflect" pre-existing beliefs about machine thought.[^c19][^c20] Style did not carry the judgment: "This is indicated by the result that style does not influence their judgment of AI's" reasoning.[^c21]

## Limits

- Small and shrinking panel. Group 1 began with 16 people, and "We experienced an attrition of two participants in 2024 and two additional participants in 2025."[^c10] The human comparison group is 11 PhDs and professors.
- No statistics in the text read. The authors write "Data analysis is ongoing."[^c11] The gaps (3.82 against 3.75 in 2024; 4.1 against 3.7 for depth in 2025) are reported as point means without tests, so the size of the divergence is not established.
- Year and model are the same index. The test model was "GPT-3.5 in 2023, GPT-4 in 2024, and GPT-4.5 in 2025."[^c12] The rising blind score is a change of model as well as of time (my inference from the year-model pairing). The design cannot separate a judge's changing view from a model's changing output, and the rise in quality is not evidence that the view was wrong.
- Question selection by the judges. Each judge "submitted at least two philosophical questions they believed could best distinguish between human and IUI responses."[^c13] Questions chosen to separate the two sources may favour one side; the text read does not say how the final set was weighted.
- The rubric is the judges' own. "participants in Group 1 collectively constructed this six-dimensional rubric."[^c14] So the standard that found the AI superior was written by the people whose attitudes were being compared with it. The authors' explanation, that "current standards for evaluating philosophical quality, even when designed by experts, may" miss the implicit values the interviews stressed,[^c15] is their argument, not something the study tests.
- Whether raters guessed the source is not reported in the text read. The blinding is asserted by design, not checked. (My inference from the text.)
- Population. Scholars were recruited through social media and had to have "fluent Chinese reading and expression skills."[^c16] Whether the divergence holds for other languages or disciplines is not tested.
- The two measurements are on different objects: a general attitude toward AI in philosophy against scores on specific anonymized texts. A divergence between them is a fact about this study's design; it does not show that attitudes are generally unreliable.
- The attitude is not a significant predictor in the second study's own regression. The headline runs through the logic dimension of the rating: "Only the logic factor had the most significant influence on score differences (P < 0.01)."[^c22] "The influence of other factors is not significant."[^c23] In the table of that regression the attitude coefficient is −0.1462 with P = 0.122 (my reading of the table). So the belief-linked gap is carried by how the rater judges the logic, not shown by the attitude scale on its own.
- The second study is one questionnaire over a convenience sample of 129 students, taught by the researchers. "While not randomized, this sample offered the advantage of consistency."[^c24] The design is cross-sectional, yet it was set "aiming to establish a causal relationship between these attitudes and evaluation"[^c25] — my inference is that the direction of the attitude–rating link is not established by it.
- The open question above (were raters guessing the source?) is partly answered there: recognition was high, so blind rating did not remove the source. Whether the gap would shrink if raters could not guess the source is not tested in either text.
