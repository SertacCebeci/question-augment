# Design frame

> One name, three objects, separated by **where the frame sits**: inside one
> head, on paper, or in the pair of representation-and-use. Each is identified
> by different evidence, so "they reframed" is not one measurement and "adopt
> a frame" is not one instruction.

## The name and what it covers

Nick Kelly and John Gero (*Design Science* 8:e30, 2022) review the design[^s1][^s2]
framing literature and report that four decades after Schön introduced the
term there is still no consensus definition of a *design frame*. Their
diagnosis: published definitions say what frames **do**, and skip the prior
question of **where they are located** relative to a designer. Three answers
are implicit in the literature; the last column says which note holds each
object.

| Reading | Where the frame is | What identifies one | Who can hold one | Member note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (i) **conceptual assemblage** | internal — part of a designer's cognitive structure | imputed by a researcher from protocol, sketches, artefacts or recall | an individual; a team only in so far as members' assemblages overlap | none yet — queued as the *internal assemblage and shared frames* gap (Hey, Joyce & Beckman 2007; Bucciarelli; Dong et al. 2013) |
| (ii) **externalised representation** | external — speech, a sketch, a problem statement, a competition brief | read off the artefact; it exists independently of any designer and must be interpreted by whoever receives it | anyone who reads it | none yet — queued as the *handed-over problem statement* gap (Silk et al. 2021, the one study that manipulates a frame as an independent variable) |
| (iii) **tool for thinking** | liminal — the representation *plus* the moves made in relation to it | recognised retrospectively, from the activity that follows | the relation between a representation and its users | none yet — queued as the *frame defined by its use* gap (Dorst 2015; Valkenburg & Dorst 1998) |

Kelly & Gero quote the readings from their proponents: Dorst (2015) takes the
third, defining frames as "tools that exist within a world of actions and
intentions" whose framehood "is completely defined by its use"; Valkenburg &
Dorst (1998) code from the activity, "a frame is mostly only recognisable
through the following activity"; Hey, Joyce & Beckman (2007) take the first,
"frames are cognitive and thus are held implicitly by individuals … no single
method has been able to reliably tease apart individual frames from team
frames".

Dorst's (2011) propositional model — a frame is the implication that adopting
a working principle will create the value sought — is on a different axis. It
says what a frame does and is compatible with all three locations; it is not a
fourth member of the table. That object is [[problem-framing]].

## Where the split shows

Kelly & Gero tabulate seven empirical studies of framing spanning 1984–2022
(their Table 1) and report that each commits, usually implicitly, to a
different one of the three. Schön (1984) imputes a frame from a protocol and a
participant's recall; Kvan & Gao (2006) count a framing event whenever a
designer identifies a new design problem or interprets further from the brief;
Valkenburg & Dorst (1998) code a context as a frame when a team frames a
(sub)problem and explores further; Silk et al. (2021) give 102 engineering and
industrial-design students controlled problem statements and treat the
statement as the frame, while observing that "individuals' interpretations of
a framing can vary considerably"; Chandrasegaran et al. (2022) speak of a
team's "primary frame" and of frames interacting, with no nomenclature for the
individual members' frames. Kelly & Gero's summary is that the studies "all
purport to be studying this same phenomenon — design framing — yet utilise
different definitions".

They propose a nomenclature to end it: *design frame* for the implicit
assemblage, *representations of a design frame* for its externalisations,
*design framing* for the conscious metacognitive strategy, and *changes to a
design frame* for what an outside observer identifies. Under it "the term
**reframing** becomes unnecessary, a construct that confuses rather than
clarifies" — because many expert designers do not consciously *do* framing but
change the frame intuitively, and one word covering both hides which of the
two a study measured.

| Design research, general | *frame* (bare) | **exact for neither note**: the bare word is used both for the located object (here) and for Dorst's propositional implication ([[problem-framing]]), and a given use rarely says which. Disambiguate before citing it |
| Design research (Dorst 2011) | frame (the propositional model) | close — what a frame *does*, orthogonal to location and compatible with all three readings; held by [[problem-framing]] |
| Design research (Schön 1983) | problem setting; naming and framing | **not this name**: the wider activity, also held by [[problem-framing]] |
| Sociology (Goffman 1974) | frame | close — the acknowledged origin of the word: as quoted in Kelly & Gero, Goffman's frames are definitions of a situation built up in accordance with principles of organization. | AI (Minsky 1974); JDM (Kahneman & Tversky 1984) | frame; framing | **not this concept**: Kelly & Gero name these as parallel uses of the word that complicate its origins, and do not reconcile them with the design use |

## Why it matters

Stall 1 asks what the dimensions of $W$ are, and [[problem-framing]] answers
that they are committed to rather than discovered. This note says the
commitment has three incompatible readings, and the difference is operational:

- Under **(i)** an axis set is a state nobody can inspect, including its
  holder; anything a checker sees is an imputation from behaviour.
- Under **(ii)** it is a string that can be written, stored and handed over —
  and Silk et al.'s finding is that the receiver's reading of the string
  varies, so handing over the representation does not hand over the frame.
- Under **(iii)** the same written frame followed by different moves is a
  different frame, so "did the axis set change?" cannot be answered from the
  statement alone.

Only (ii) and (iii) leave a trace at all, and they disagree on whether the
trace is the statement or the statement plus the moves. Every claim the wiki
carries about reframing as a detectable event — [[problem-solution-coevolution]]'s
solution-to-problem arrow, for one — is a claim under one reading and not the
others. And Hey et al.'s report that no method separates an individual's frame
from a team's means that a multi-source inquiry cannot presently be told
whether its axis set is one frame or several.

## Limits

- The paper is a **critical, non-exhaustive** review, and the three readings
  are the authors' synthesis of what is *implicit* in the literature; none of
  the seven studies self-identifies with one. If the synthesis is wrong, the
  studies may be comparable after all.
- **Unlike [[saturation]], this split is not measured.** No one has run two
  readings over the same protocol and reported how far apart the frame count
  or the frame-change points land. What is supported is that the studies are
  not commensurable, not that they disagree by a known amount.
- Every quotation and attributed definition above — the seven studies, and the
  Goffman, Minsky and Kahneman & Tversky rows of *Known as* — is taken from
  kelly-2022's text and table; none of those primary texts was read here [?].
- No member note exists for any of the three readings yet, so the table's last
  column is a queue, not a partition of the wiki: the objects are named here
  before anything measures them separately.
- The nomenclature proposal is a proposal. The paper's own conclusion is that
  no cognitive model of design frames exists to adjudicate between the
  readings — that absence is what its title points at.
- Reading a "frame" as an axis set over $W$ is this wiki's move. Kelly & Gero
  describe designers, not askers; nothing in the review concerns questions.
