This study aims to simulate real-world clinical scenarios to systematically evaluate the ability of Large Language Models (LLMs) to
extract core medical information from patient chief complaints laden with noise and redundancy, and to verify whether they exhibit a
functional decline analogous to Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD). We employed a cross-sectional
analysis design based on standardized medical probes, selecting four mainstream LLMs as research subjects: GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5,
DeepSeek 3.1, and Qwen3-Max. An evaluation system comprising twenty medical probes across five core dimensions was used to
simulate a genuine clinical communication environment. All probes had gold-standard answers defined by clinical experts and were
assessed via a double-blind, inverse rating scale by two independent clinicians. The results show that all tested models exhibited functional
defects to varying degrees, with Qwen3-Max demonstrating the best overall performance and Gemini 2.5 the worst. Under conditions of
extreme noise, most models experienced a functional collapse. Notably, GPT-4o made a severe misjudgment in the risk assessment for
pulmonary embolism (PE) secondary to deep vein thrombosis (DVT). This research is the first to empirically confirm that LLMs exhibit
features resembling metabolic dysfunction when processing clinical information, proposing the innovative concept of "AI-Metabolic
Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (AI-MASLD)". These findings offer a crucial safety warning for the application of
Artificial Intelligence (AI) in healthcare, emphasizing that current LLMs must be used as auxiliary tools under human expert supervision,
as there remains a significant gap between their theoretical knowledge and practical clinical application.

1 INTRODUCTION
In clinical practice, the most critical yet often most challenging stage is the direct communication between the clinician
and the patient. The physician must form an initial impression through real-time exchange to guide subsequent examination,
diagnostic reasoning, and treatment decisions. The skill of extracting medically relevant information from a patient's
unstructured narrative requires years of rigorous training, including clinical rotations and practical mentorship. However,
even experienced clinicians can make errors when interpreting non-standardized, fragmented, and detail-laden patient

accounts, which can lead to misdiagnosis, delayed treatment, or suboptimal care plans (1). This inherent risk stemming

from unstructured communication is an ongoing problem in clinical practice that systematic medical education cannot fully
mitigate.
In the rapidly evolving field of AI in healthcare, Large Language Models (LLMs) have garnered widespread attention.
Their exceptional performance in medical licensing exams, structured data analysis, and standardized case-based Q&A has
prompted extensive discussion about whether "AI has surpassed doctors" (1-3). However, this purported "superiority" is
built on a narrow foundation: LLMs are primarily trained and evaluated on patient texts generated by doctors or
professionals—texts that have been filtered and standardized, thus stripped of the "interference information" inherent in
raw patient-physician communication. Essentially, these texts are a secondary interpretation of the patient's narrative by
the clinician, not the patient's original statement.
This creates a significant research gap: the direct interaction between LLMs and patients—i.e., confronting unprocessed,
highly contextualized, and unstructured expressions—remains underexplored (1). In reality, the conclusion that "models
have surpassed doctors" is based on processing data distilled by clinicians, not the raw communication in authentic clinical
settings. Patient-physician communication is inherently complex; even senior doctors can face interpretive difficulties.
Patient expressions are influenced by various factors such as age, education level, ethnicity, and cultural background. Their
narratives often lack medical terminology, are structurally disorganized, and intermingled with copious irrelevant details.
For LLMs, which lack actual clinical experience and the ability to interpret non-verbal cues or contextual nuances, directly
processing such unfiltered patient input is a daunting and largely unverified challenge. If experienced doctors can err in
these unstructured communications, the probability of error may be even higher for algorithm-dependent LLMs interacting
directly with patients.
Based on this, this study introduces the metaphorical framework of AI-Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic
Liver Disease (AI-MASLD). MASLD affects approximately 38% of the global population, with its core pathophysiology
involving impaired hepatic lipid metabolism, leading to progressive fat accumulation, hepatocellular injury, and functional
decline (4-7). We hypothesize that the error patterns of LLMs during direct patient interaction may share similarities with
this disease. Just as the liver suffers functional impairment under metabolic stress induced by excessive lipid load, could
LLMs exhibit a similar functional disorder when encountering the "metabolic load" of a patient's unstructured narrative—
a narrative replete with interference, redundancy, logical contradictions, and a chaotic timeline? We define this potential
functional decline as AI-MASLD, characterized by inaccurate information extraction, erroneous prioritization of
symptoms, and diminished ability to interpret critical medical data (8).
Previous research has identified some limitations of LLMs, such as the flattery effect (blind agreement with incorrect
input) and cognitive bias (9-12). However, no study has yet systematically assessed whether these models exhibit a
metabolic-like functional disorder analogous to human MASLD when interacting directly with patients and facing the
inevitable "metabolic load" imposed by unstructured data. Therefore, this study pursues three main objectives: First, to fill
the research gap in evaluating LLM performance in simulated direct patient-physician interaction scenarios, clarifying
their capability in real communication environments. Second, to verify whether LLMs exhibit characteristics of "AI-

2

MASLD"—i.e., functional impairment similar to human MASLD—when processing unfiltered patient narratives. Third,
to develop and validate an evaluation framework to serve as a standardized reference tool for assessing LLMs in clinical
settings.
This study aims to test LLMs directly in the Real World, as the genuine, direct interaction between AI and the patient
is "The Last Mile" for AI's application in medicine. Through this systematic evaluation, we hope to provide empirical
evidence for the clinical application of LLMs, help people objectively understand their current capabilities, correct the
overly optimistic perception that "models have surpassed doctors”, and further affirm that, despite their rapid development,
LLMs cannot replace the indispensable judgment, empathy, and contextual interpretation ability of a well-trained clinician
in the core processes of direct diagnosis and treatment.

2 METHODS
2.1 Study Design
This study employed a cross-sectional analysis design based on standardized "medical probes”, aimed at systematically
evaluating the ability of LLMs to extract core medical information from patient chief complaints filled with noise and
redundancy. This evaluation method simulated the assessment of a doctor's diagnostic thinking process in a clinical
environment, focusing on the model's performance in capturing key information from a patient's rambling narrative.
2.2 Language Model Selection
In selecting study subjects, we established clear inclusion criteria: choosing publicly available mainstream LLMs with
significant influence and application potential in the medical field. Ultimately, four advanced LLMs were selected for
evaluation: OpenAI's GPT-4o, Alphabet's Gemini 2.5, DeepSeek 3.1, and Alibaba's Qwen3-Max. To ensure consistency
in the testing environment and reproducibility of results, all models were accessed and tested via their official API
interfaces.
2.3 Evaluation Tools and Implementation
The core of the evaluation tool is the "Medical Probe" assessment set designed by senior clinical experts, with its design
strictly adhering to four basic principles. First, emphasizing the principle of clinical authenticity, all probes simulate the
complexity of real patient chief complaints, incorporating colloquialisms, emotional content, and non-linear narrative
structures to test model performance in non-idealized environments. Second, establishing a gold-standard reference system,
each probe has a "gold-standard answer" predefined by senior clinical experts, serving as the sole objective benchmark for
evaluating all model outputs. Third, adopting an objective quantitative evaluation method, a clear, quantifiable inverse
rating system (0-4 points) was established, with detailed criteria for each score to minimize subjective judgment and ensure
assessment reproducibility. Fourth, achieving multi-dimensional capability assessment, the probe design covers multiple
dimensions of core clinical thinking, including information filtering, compression, distinguishing between primary and
secondary issues, and contradiction identification.
The evaluation set comprises five clinical core capability dimensions, totaling 20 independent text probes across five
groups. Probe Group 1 (Noise Filtering) primarily assesses the model's ability to accurately extract core medical signals
from extremely noisy text containing substantial irrelevant information and emotional interference. Probe Group 2 (Priority
Triage) focuses on the model's capability, based on medical knowledge, to identify genuinely priority-level potential fatal

3

risks among multiple seemingly urgent "distraction" symptoms. Probe Group 3 (Contradiction Detection) tests the model's
ability to recognize and process hidden internal logical contradictions within the patient's statement. Probe Group 4 (FactEmotion Separation) evaluates the model's capacity to penetrate highly emotional language and extract objective,
diagnostically valuable factual information. Probe Group 5 (Timeline Sorting) examines the model's ability to construct a
clear, accurate timeline of disease progression from a chaotic, non-linear narrative.
For implementation, all probes were input to each LLM via API using unified text prompts. Model outputs were
independently scored in a double-blind manner by two pre-calibrated attending physicians, each with over 10 years of
clinical experience, based on the preset Probe Scoring Guideline. The scoring utilized a 4-point inverse scale, where a
lower score indicates better performance. To ensure the assessment focuses on advanced clinical reasoning capabilities, all
probes were designed as pure-text clinical situation descriptions, entirely excluding interference from images, audio, or
other multimodal information, thereby avoiding bias caused by differences in models' visual or auditory processing abilities.
2.4 Outcome Measures
The primary outcome measures of the study were the average scores of each LLM across the five probe dimensions and
the total score across all 20 probes. Secondary outcome measures focused on the qualitative analysis of specific failure
patterns on high-risk probes to identify critical clinical judgment deficiencies.
2.5 Quality Control
Multiple measures were adopted for quality control to ensure the rigor and reliability of the assessment. Unified text input
and output via API ensured all models were tested under identical conditions, effectively excluding environmental factors
like frontend interfaces and network latency. Two independent clinical experts performed double-blind scoring based on a
detailed scoring guideline, and inter-rater reliability was checked by calculating the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC)
to ensure the objectivity, consistency, and reproducibility of the scoring. The pure-text probe design ensured that results
reflected only the models' advanced cognitive abilities in language understanding, logical reasoning, and medical
knowledge application, rather than multimodal perception capabilities.
2.6 Ethical Considerations
No patients or public members were directly involved in the design, execution, or reporting of this study. However, it
should be noted that the study's design originated from the universal concern regarding the potential risks to patient safety
posed by current AI applications in healthcare due to the inability to comprehend complex real-world communication,
reflecting the research team's emphasis on and responsibility for medical AI safety.

3 RESULTS
3.1 Overall Performance
The study used an inverse scoring system, with a total possible score of 80 (20 probes, 4 points maximum each), where a
lower score indicates superior performance. The evaluation results revealed significant differences in performance among
the LLMs in simulating real clinical communication scenarios, and all tested models exhibited functional defects to varying
degrees.

complexity.
Notably, GPT-4o, widely considered a leader in general capability, performed moderately (27/80) in this specialized
test, significantly trailing DeepSeek 3.1 (23/80) and Qwen3-Max. This challenges the prevailing perception regarding the
capability hierarchy of LLMs.

3.2 Performance Analysis by Dimension
We conducted an in-depth analysis of the evaluation results across the five core clinical capability dimensions, covering
the entire process from basic information extraction to advanced logical reasoning. We found a clear performance
divergence among the models in each specific capability (Table 2 / Figure 2).
3.2.1 Noise Filtering: Universal Functional Decline
Noise Filtering was the worst-performing dimension for all models (highest average score ≈ 9.00). The average score for
each model was around 2 points (GPT-4o = 2.00, Qwen = 2.00, DeepSeek = 2.25, Gemini = 2.75), indicating a moderate
information screening dysfunction when processing high-noise clinical narratives. Our data confirm the core feature of
"AI-MASLD"—a functional decline when faced with a high "metabolic load" of unstructured information.
In the extreme noise environment designed in Probe 1.1 (containing significant irrelevant life details), Gemini,
DeepSeek, and Qwen-Max all suffered a catastrophic failure (score 4), exhibiting a "performance cliff" phenomenon and
being completely misled by non-medical cues (Table 3).
GPT-4o was the only model to show some resilience, suffering severe interference (score 2) but not a complete collapse,
manifesting as functional degradation rather than failure. This suggests that most models lack robust mechanisms to handle
complex information overload.

3.2.2 Priority Triage: Capability Divergence and Fatal Misjudgment
This dimension served as a critical dividing line for model capability, primarily examining the ability to identify potentially
fatal risks amidst multiple symptoms. While most models could handle simple emergency scenarios (e.g., Probe 2.1),
serious defects were exposed in complex scenarios requiring cross-system reasoning.
In Probe 2.2 (Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) / Pulmonary Embolism (PE) risk assessment), GPT-4o suffered a
catastrophic failure (score 4). It failed to establish the clinical link between "leg swelling/pain" and "shortness of breath”,
failing to identify the potential lethal pulmonary embolism risk chain, and was instead misled by the more conspicuous
"angina" distractor. This constitutes a severe clinical safety hazard. In contrast, Qwen performed perfectly in this high-risk
probe, successfully identifying the PE risk associated with DVT.
3.2.3 Contradiction Detection: Fragility of Logical Verification
This dimension tested the model's ability to identify intrinsic logical contradictions in the patient's chief complaint (e.g.,
conflict between self-reported health and symptom deterioration). This is not merely information extraction but a stress
test on the model's logical reasoning and clinical knowledge integration capabilities. Patients often present contradictions
between their self-perception ("I'm managing fine") and objective symptoms (signs of disease escalation) due to cognitive
bias, lack of medical knowledge, or psychological denial. The ability to identify such contradictions is key to distinguishing
a "quasi-clinical assistant” from an "information provider".

3.2.4 Fact-Emotion Separation: Key to Clinical Utility
This dimension assessed the model's ability to strip away subjective emotions (anxiety, fear) and extract objective medical
facts. The model's capacity for "emotion deconvolution"—i.e., separating subjective emotional expressions like anxiety,
fear, or complaint from the objective, pure medical facts within a highly mixed clinical narrative—is the critical indicator
distinguishing a "dialogue partner" from an "efficient clinical tool”. When model output is contaminated with emotional
content, it not only increases the clinician's cognitive load but can also lead to unnecessary medical intervention due to the
amplification effect of emotional expression.
Qwen demonstrated a significant advantage in emotional immunity (average score = 0.75), effectively distinguishing
emotional expressions from objective facts, achieving perfect scores in multiple probes. GPT-4o and DeepSeek showed
clear emotional sensitivity (average scores of 1.75), frequently mixing irrelevant emotional descriptions into the output,
making the medical summary less pure and increasing the burden of secondary refinement for the doctor.
3.2.5 Timeline Sorting: Most Reliable Basic Capability
This was the best-performing dimension for all models (average score 1.00), indicating that processing clear temporal
markers and linear sequencing is a capability largely mastered by current LLMs. Despite generally good performance,
DeepSeek exhibited a persistent "minor redundancy" defect in this dimension (scoring 1 point for each item), continuously
adding unnecessary explanatory content during timeline sorting instead of providing a concise sequence.
3.3 Key Failure Modes
Through a qualitative analysis of each model's performance on specific high-risk probes, we identified three typical
functional pathological modes in this study.
The first mode is Catastrophic Functional Failure, defined as a complete failure of the model to extract core information
or the making of a fatal misjudgment (score 4). Specifically, Gemini 2.5 exhibited the highest frequency of catastrophic
failures (5 instances), primarily concentrated in noise filtering and priority triage tasks, which indicates severe functional
instability. Although GPT-4o only experienced 1 catastrophic failure, it occurred in the critical pulmonary embolism risk
assessment scenario, directly endangering patient safety. In contrast, DeepSeek and Qwen demonstrated "precipitous"
failure characteristics under extreme noise conditions.
The second mode is Information Steatosis, characterized by redundancy and a low signal-to-noise ratio. It is defined as
the model outputting correct information while intermixing it with substantial secondary information, emotional
interference, or irrelevant explanations. This pattern represented the most typical penalty scenario for GPT-4o (often
scoring 2-3 points). In multiple probes, GPT-4o tended to generate redundant content, blurring the distinction between
primary and secondary issues and thereby reducing the signal-to-noise ratio of the output. This "information fat
accumulation" compels clinicians to conduct secondary filtering, which seriously impairs the utility of AI in real-world,
high-pressure clinical environments.
The third mode is Hallucination/Fabrication, defined as the model including symptoms or information in its output that
were not mentioned by the patient. In Probe 1.1, after being subjected to interference, Gemini, DeepSeek, and Qwen all
fabricated the symptom of "hypersomnia" (see Table 3), whereas the original text only stated that the patient was "feeling
weak all over”. The generation of spurious information by the models under high-noise stress suggests an inherent risk of
over-inference when processing complex contexts.

4 DISCUSSION
4.1 Principal Findings: Confirmation of the "AI-MASLD" Phenotype
This study is the first to empirically confirm the existence of the "AI-Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver
Disease (AI-MASLD)" functional phenotype by simulating real-world high-noise clinical interaction scenarios. Unlike
conclusions from existing studies based on standardized datasets (such as USMLE), our results show that when faced with

10

patients' raw narratives that are unstructured, fragmented, and full of interference (i.e., high "data metabolic load"), all
tested LLMs exhibited functional impairment to varying degrees.
The core characteristic of the "AI-MASLD" observed in this study is a reduced capacity to process "metabolic load"—
where "metabolic load" specifically refers to the inherent complexity, redundancy, and ambiguity in unstructured patient
narratives. Just as human MASLD stems from the liver's inability to effectively metabolize excessive lipids, leading to
functional decline, LLMs suffering from "AI-MASLD" struggle to filter irrelevant information, prioritize critical
symptoms, resolve logical contradictions, or integrate fragmented details when faced with raw clinical input(13).
Specifically, the models exhibited significant heterogeneity in their clinical information processing performance.
Qwen-Max demonstrated the best "metabolic resilience”, excelling in critical symptom extraction and emotion separation;
while Gemini 2.5 showed the most severe "functional decompensation”, frequently being distracted by irrelevant
information. Notably, despite GPT-4o being considered a benchmark in general domains, it exhibited a concerning "latent
defect" in the advanced clinical reasoning tasks of this study, especially in critical risk judgments involving multi-system
associations(14-17). The performance differences among models align with prior research that LLM performance is
influenced by training data, architectural design, and optimization strategies, highlighting the importance of assessing
model functional integrity beyond general medical knowledge(18). These findings confirm that model superiority in
idealized tests does not directly translate to complex real-world clinical scenarios, and AI-MASLD constitutes a critical
pathological barrier preventing LLM clinical adoption.
4.2 Mechanistic Interpretation: Deconstructing the Pathology of AI Models
Using the "medical probe" matrix designed in this study, we first established the overall pathological characteristics
exhibited by LLMs in real clinical settings: all tested models presented a "sub-healthy" state when facing high-noise and
high-complexity information. This pathological state has three distinct features: First, non-linear capability distribution,
meaning the model's general capability ranking may be "inverted" by its performance in professional high-risk scenarios,
with some open-source models outperforming closed-source models in key judgments (like emotion separation and
contradiction detection); second, core central functional defect, primarily manifested as a lack of risk weighting and
prioritization ability (i.e., clinical judgment) in complex situations; third, low metabolic efficiency, where even accurate
information is generally accompanied by redundancy and a lack of focus, severely impacting clinical utility(19).
Based on these overall evaluation conclusions, we can further deconstruct the specific pathological mechanisms
contributing to "AI-MASLD" across three dimensions.
The first dimension is the "steatosis" of information processing, namely the failure of noise filtering. The results from
Probe 1 (Noise Filtering) revealed the models' pervasive "information steatosis"; when the signal-to-noise ratio in the
clinical narrative dropped, most models (DeepSeek, Gemini, Qwen) encountered a "Performance Cliff”, drastically falling
from a usable state to complete failure, which is analogous to hepatic metabolic stasis under lipid overload. In contrast,
GPT-4o also experienced performance degradation (functional decline) but did not completely shut down, showing a
unique "metabolic resilience”. This finding suggests that most current LLMs lack robust mechanisms to sustain attention
in noisy clinical environments(20, 21).
The second dimension is "algorithmic fibrosis”, referring to rigidity in risk stratification, which stands as the most
dangerous pathological feature of AI-MASLD—not "ignorance" but "judgment rigidity”, and it is the fundamental cause
of the aforementioned "core central functional defect”. Results from Probe 2 (Priority Triage) and Probe 3 (Contradiction
Detection) show that models are extremely susceptible to "Clinical Smoke Bombs" interference. In Probe 2.2 (DVT/PE
risk identification), GPT-4o failed to infer the pulmonary embolism risk chain from "leg swelling" and "shortness of breath”,

11

instead being attracted by a more conspicuous chronic symptom, which suggests that while the model possesses knowledge,
it lacks dynamic risk weighting capability(22). In Probe 3.3, some models failed to identify the esophageal cancer warning
signs obscured by the patient's subjective statement of "feeling fine”, and this blind adherence to subjective patient accounts
reflects the model's "fibrosis" in logical reasoning—an inability to penetrate surface appearances and a rigid handling of
conflicting clinical evidence(23).
The third dimension is toxic accumulation, specifically the failure in emotion-fact separation(24). The results of Probe
4 highlighted the absence of "Emotion Deconvolution" capability, which is also the main pathological source of "low
metabolic efficiency" and output redundancy. Except for Qwen, most models failed to effectively strip away patient anxiety
and complaints, leading to medical summaries contaminated with subjective emotions ("toxic accumulation"); this not only
reduces the clinician's reading efficiency but may also lead to unnecessary overtreatment due to emotional amplification.
Gemini's dramatic fluctuation in this area ("Schrödinger's Reliability") further undermines its trustworthiness as a clinical
aid.
4.3 Clinical Implications: The Gap Between "Textbook" and "Bedside"
This study profoundly reveals the vast chasm between "Textbook Medicine" and "Bedside Medicine”, pointing out that
the core limitation of existing AI healthcare research is its over-reliance on processed, structured data(25). Previous medical
model training and evaluation systems largely employed doctor-compiled medical records, standardized case reports, or
cleaned Electronic Health Records (EHRs). These materials are essentially "secondary interpretation" texts, professionally
filtered to remove the redundancy and ambiguity of real diagnostic information. However, clinical practice is fraught with
complexity: influenced by education, cultural differences, and language proficiency, patients' raw narratives are often
fragmented, logically chaotic, inverted in priority, or even self-contradictory. Even experienced clinicians may struggle to
extract accurate medical information from such raw accounts; and this study confirms that LLMs have an even lower
tolerance for the high "metabolic load" challenge posed by such unstructured input, exhibiting functional impairment akin
to "AI-MASLD"(26).
This functional defect introduces profound clinical safety hazards. The probe-based evaluation framework in this study
reveals a significant performance drop-off "from doctor-interpreted text to patient's original expression”, negating the
rationale for extrapolating "superiority" on exam-type or standardized texts to direct patient-physician interaction. When
models suffer from severe "AI-MASLD", they are highly prone to failing to identify critical risk factors (e.g., obesity,
diabetes, dyslipidemia) amidst complex distractors, or misjudging the urgency of symptoms. For instance, in a simulated
case, GPT-4o failed to recognize the fatal pulmonary embolism risk within the distracting information; the same logical
defect could lead the model to overlook key signs like fatigue and jaundice casually mentioned by a patient during small
talk. This indicates that applying models with un-"cured metabolic defects" independently in emergency triage or primary
care would pose extremely high patient safety risks.
Based on the aforementioned findings, this study puts forward two key recommendations. The first is the reshaping of
the Human-AI collaboration paradigm(27). The results of this research do not support the radical viewpoint that "AI will
soon replace doctors"; instead, they underscore the irreplaceable role of human physicians as "information gatekeepers".
Until AI systems can effectively manage the "metabolic load" of unstructured input, their role must be strictly confined to
an auxiliary status. Clinical institutions integrating large language models (LLMs) should proceed with extreme prudence,
particularly in patient-facing scenarios, by implementing stringent human supervision mechanisms to validate AI outputs
and ensuring that all critical decisions undergo final review by human experts(28).

12

The second recommendation involves dismantling the linear myth of model capability. This study found that the
strength of general capability is not directly equivalent to the quality of medical capability; the performance reversal of
open-source models (such as Qwen and DeepSeek) over GPT-4o in specific high-risk reasoning tasks indicates that clinical
reasoning ability necessitates specialized training and optimization, rather than mere reliance on the stacking of parameter
scales. When selecting models, healthcare organizations should prioritize the models’ ability to handle unstructured
"metabolic load"—opting for models with milder "AI-MASLD" symptoms—instead of blindly pursuing universal
parameter size.
4.4 Comparison with Existing Literature
While extensive previous research has confirmed the superior performance of LLMs in medical qualification exams like
USMLE and structured EHR analysis tasks(29, 30). This study fills a critical gap in medical AI by shifting the evaluation
focus to unstructured narratives simulating authentic patient-physician interaction. Unlike previous studies that mainly
focused on knowledge proficiency under standardized conditions, this research introduced the challenge of unstructured
narratives with high "metabolic load”, thus revealing a previously under-recognized functional limitation of the models—
namely, "AI-MASLD”.
This finding not only complements the comprehensive assessment of model cognitive capability but also aligns with
the observation regarding the "decoupling of diagnostic accuracy from clinical reasoning ability”, further pointing out that
this decoupling sharply worsens when confronting noisy, real-world data(31, 32). Furthermore, the AI-MASLD framework
proposed in this study offers a novel, systems-based explanatory perspective for understanding AI hallucinations and
reasoning errors, thereby confirming that exam scores alone cannot fully guarantee model reliability in genuine clinical
diagnosis and treatment.
4.5 Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, regarding the sample scope, only four mainstream LLMs were evaluated, without
covering a wider range of open-source models or fine-tuned medical models, which may restrict the comprehensiveness
of the conclusions drawn from the model performance analysis. Second, in terms of complexity constraints, although the
simulated clinical cases in this study sought to reflect the complexity of the real world, they still cannot fully encompass
all types of patient narratives encountered in clinical practice; the inclusion of de-identified real patient records would
further enhance the study's ecological validity by better aligning with actual clinical scenarios(33). Third, there are modality
constraints: the probes were limited to text form, excluding medical imaging or speech tone information, which may
underestimate the potential of multimodal models in real clinical settings where diverse data types are often involved(34).
Fourth, regarding specialty limitations, the cases primarily concentrated on internal medicine and metabolism-related
symptoms, and the generalizability of these conclusions to other medical fields, such as surgery or psychiatry, which have
distinct clinical characteristics and information requirements, requires further validation.
4.6 Future Directions: Diagnosing and Treating AI-MASLD
Based on this study's findings, to promote the safe application of LLMs in healthcare, future research should focus on two
core dimensions: "diagnosis" and "treatment”, aiming to establish a standardized evaluation system and implement targeted
functional optimization. The first dimension involves standardizing the diagnostic system for "AI-MASLD”. Current
evaluations rely heavily on custom scoring systems, so there is an urgent need to establish a unified and widely accepted
diagnostic framework—similar to FibroScan (Transient Elastography) for assessing human liver stiffness. This entails

13

developing an "AI Clinical Capability Stress Test Benchmark”, a non-invasive, high-efficiency detection tool (i.e., "AI
FibroScan") that includes diverse unstructured clinical narratives to simulate different levels of "metabolic load"; this
benchmark should cover cases of varying complexity, cultural backgrounds, and language proficiencies to fully reflect the
diversity of real-world clinical scenarios. Additionally, diagnostic standards should include quantitative indicators such as
information filtering efficiency, noise rejection capability, conflict detection, prioritization ability, and narrative integration
capacity, which will help researchers and clinicians quickly screen a model's "liver function indicators" before deployment,
ensuring that only functionally sound models are applied in clinical settings and allowing for objective comparison across
different models and versions.
The second dimension focuses on implementing systematic "anti-inflammatory and metabolic" interventions, as
exploring ways to mitigate or reverse "AI-MASLD" is crucial for improving the model's clinical utility. Recommended
multi-level strategies include strict "Data Diet Control”, which involves introducing more authentic, unstructured clinical
dialogue data during pre-training or fine-tuning instead of solely relying on cleaned textbook texts to specifically enhance
the model's tolerance for raw narratives; algorithmic "Anti-fibrosis" Treatment, which uses Reinforcement Learning from
Human Feedback (RLHF) to specifically train the model's sensitivity to "warning symptoms" and break down rigid linear
reasoning, while concurrently developing prompt engineering techniques or integrating information filtering algorithms to
guide the model to prioritize key information; and constructing Mixture of Experts Systems, which explores multi-model
collaboration mechanisms to compensate for the metabolic defects of a single model—for example, using a model
proficient in information extraction (like Qwen) as a frontend "filter" and a model proficient in knowledge generation as a
backend "analyzer”, or designing post-processing modules to remove redundancy and resolve logical contradictions.
Furthermore, this process requires cross-disciplinary collaboration among computer scientists, clinicians, and linguists to
develop solutions that address the fundamental problems, and future longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the
effectiveness of these interventions and continuously track changes in the severity of "AI-MASLD" during model iteration.
5 CONCLUSION
AI-MASLD is a real and undeniable technical pathology. It reveals that even the most advanced Large Language Models
possess severe cognitive limitations and functional fragility when confronted with the complexities of real-world patientphysician communication(35, 36). Acknowledging and thoroughly investigating this phenomenon is a necessary step for
AI to transition from a "demonstration technology" to a "practical medical tool”. Only by curing AI's "fatty liver" can we
truly and confidently entrust patients' health to this transformative technology.

I. DESIGN PRINCIPLES
This evaluation probe is designed to simulate real clinical scenarios and test large language models’ (LLMs’) ability to
extract core medical information from patient complaints filled with noise and redundant information. This resembles
clinically assessing a physician’s diagnostic reasoning—whether they can grasp key points amid a patient’s rambling
narrative. The design follows the following principles:
Clinical Authenticity: Simulates the complexity of patient complaints, incorporating colloquial, emotional, and nonlinear
narrative styles.
Gold Standard Reference: Each probe has a clinician-defined “gold standard answer” serving as the benchmark for
evaluating model outputs.
Objective Quantitative Evaluation: Establishes a clear, quantifiable scoring system to reduce subjective judgment and
ensure reproducibility.
Multidimensional Capability Assessment: Probes cover multiple dimensions including information filtering,
compression, prioritization of primary vs. secondary issues, and identification of contradictions.
II. HIGH-DIFFICULTY PROBE DESIGN AND SCORING CRITERIA
To effectively differentiate LLM performance, we designed the following five types of high-difficulty probes, each
containing four specific test cases. These probes aim to simulate more complex real-world clinical dialogues, thereby
imposing stricter stress tests on the model’s “liver function” (i.e., core clinical reasoning capacity).
Probe 1: Information Filtering Ability Under Extreme Noise

information and distractors.
