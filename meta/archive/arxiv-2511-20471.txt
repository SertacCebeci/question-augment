A BSTRACT
Recent advances in Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning have improved conventional problem solving, but creative reasoning remains comparatively underexplored. Inspired by cognitive science, we formalize combinational, exploratory, and
transformational creativity as executable computational operators over structured
problem and solution spaces, specifying how each mode combines, explores, or
transforms those spaces, respectively. Combinational reasoning transfers ideas
across domains to form unfamiliar combinations; exploratory reasoning searches
for new solutions within an existing conceptual space; and transformational reasoning modifies the rules or constraints that define that space. This formalization
yields distinct algorithmic procedures, which we directly instantiate in Universe of
Thoughts (UoT), an LLM reasoning framework. Existing creativity benchmarks
emphasize either open-ended ideation or highly constrained problem solving. We
therefore introduce three novel creative-reasoning tasks requiring concrete solutions
in low-constraint settings. Across 10 generations per method and task, T-UoT with
GPT-4o performs strongest on the low-constraint, high-objective-specificity Bridge
and Electricity tasks, while C-UoT shows its strongest relative performance on the
low-constraint, lower-objective-specificity Society task. In addition, we evaluate
UoT on HypoArena, an independent scientific hypothesis-generation benchmark
with 100 tasks across biomedical, machine-learning, and social-science domains.
With Qwen3-14B, Exploratory UoT ranks first among seven reasoning methods,
achieving a 32.7% pairwise win rate compared with 25.5% for the next-best method.
Our results suggest distinct performance patterns across task structures: T-UoT is
strongest in low-constraint, high-specificity settings, E-UoT in more constrained,
high-specificity settings, and C-UoT in low-constraint, lower-specificity settings.

I NTRODUCTION

Large Language Models (LLMs) have made rapid progress on complex reasoning, supported by
methods such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022) and Tree of Thoughts (ToT) (Yao
et al., 2023), which structure intermediate reasoning and search (Achiam et al., 2023; Chowdhery
et al., 2023). These advances have improved performance on problems with well-defined objectives
and verifiable solutions. Creative reasoning remains less explored, particularly for open-ended or
ill-defined problems where success requires solutions that are not only valid but also novel and useful
(Simon, 1973). Such settings include scientific hypothesis generation, drug discovery, and strategic
decision making, where progress may require moving beyond familiar solution patterns (Singh et al.,
2024; Gangwal et al., 2024).
Recent work has begun to move LLM reasoning toward open-ended scientific discovery. Agentic
systems such as The AI Scientist (Lu et al., 2024) automate broad scientific workflows through
iterative idea generation, experimentation, and refinement, while methods such as MOOSE-Chem
(Yang et al., 2024) more explicitly structure hypothesis generation around retrieving inspirations and
composing them into new scientific ideas. Related work has further connected LLM generation to
1

Preprint

computational creativity, particularly through analogical or combinational mechanisms that bring
together knowledge from different contexts (Gu et al., 2024; Schapiro et al., 2025). These advances
demonstrate that structured reasoning can support creative discovery. However, existing approaches
typically instantiate a particular discovery workflow or a single creative mechanism; they do not
provide a unified computational account of the qualitatively different ways in which creative reasoning
can search for new solutions.
We address this gap with Universe of Thoughts (UoT), a reasoning framework grounded in Margaret
Boden’s three modes of creativity: combinational, exploratory, and transformational (Boden, 2004;
2007; 2009). UoT operationalizes these modes as distinct search programs: combinational reasoning
transfers useful structure from unfamiliar contexts, exploratory reasoning expands into previously
unvisited possibilities while preserving the current problem rules, and transformational reasoning
modifies the assumptions that define which solutions can be considered. This unified formulation
allows the three modes to be instantiated within the same LLM reasoning framework and, importantly,
directly compared to characterize when different forms of creative search succeed or fail.
Evaluating creative reasoning remains difficult because existing benchmarks often fall into two
extremes. Open-ended idea-generation tasks permit novelty but make utility difficult to assess,
while grounded settings such as scientific discovery provide clearer evaluation but impose stronger
constraints that narrow the solution space. We therefore characterize tasks by degree of constraint
and objective specificity. We introduce three low-constraint problem-solving tasks that allow both
novelty and utility to be evaluated, and additionally use HypoArena (Zhong et al., 2026), which
permits multiple plausible hypotheses and pairwise comparison rather than requiring reproduction
of a single reference hypothesis. Together, these settings let us study how creative search behavior
changes with task structure.
The results reveal distinct performance patterns across task regimes. On the novel creative-reasoning
tasks, T-UoT performs strongest on the low-constraint, high-objective-specificity Bridge and Electricity tasks, where flexible solution spaces permit viable rule transformations and clear objectives
support their evaluation. C-UoT shows its strongest relative performance on Society, where lower objective specificity broadens analogue retrieval and low constraint facilitates cross-domain mechanism
transfer. On HypoArena, E-UoT performs best overall with Qwen3-14B, particularly in the more
constrained, higher-specificity biomedical and machine-learning settings, where local search is more
strongly guided by the problem context. Our failure analysis localizes an additional bottleneck beyond
creative search itself: C-UoT often identifies useful analogies but fails to preserve their mechanisms
during final synthesis, while T-UoT can identify meaningful rule transformations whose consequences
are not fully carried into the final hypothesis. Targeted interventions that preserve the transferred
mechanism or explicitly reason through the consequences of transformed rules substantially improve
these failure cases, suggesting that stronger downstream reasoning and integration could allow these
creative search processes to realize their potential more reliably.
We summarize our main contributions as follows:
• We formalize and operationalize three cognitive-science creativity modes (combinational, exploratory, and transformational) as executable LLM search procedures in the Universe of Thoughts
(UoT) framework.
• We evaluate UoT on novel creative-reasoning tasks in low-constraint settings and on 100 scientific
hypothesis-generation tasks from HypoArena.
• We identify task-dependent strengths across creativity modes: transformational reasoning is
strongest in low-constraint, high-objective-specificity settings, exploratory reasoning performs best
in more constrained, higher-specificity settings, and combinational reasoning shows its strongest
relative performance in low-constraint, lower-specificity settings. Ablation and failure analyses
further reveal distinct contributions and bottlenecks across the three modes.

R ELATED W ORK

2.1

LLM R EASONING F RAMEWORKS

LLM reasoning methods increasingly structure search over intermediate thoughts. Chain-of-Thought
(CoT) elicits sequential reasoning (Wei et al., 2022), Tree of Thoughts (ToT) introduces branching
2

Preprint

search with evaluation and backtracking (Yao et al., 2023), and Graph of Thoughts (GoT) generalizes
this process to arbitrary graph structures (Besta et al., 2024). More recent methods enrich these search
procedures through refinement, task-specific reasoning structures, self-reflection, and environment
feedback, including Self-Discover (Zhou et al., 2024) and Language Agent Tree Search (Zhou
et al., 2023), as well as related refinement-oriented approaches (Shin & Kim, 2025). Although
such frameworks can support creative outputs, their mechanisms are primarily designed for generic
reasoning, planning, and search rather than explicitly modeling distinct forms of creative reasoning.
UoT instead operationalizes creativity-specific search procedures derived from cognitive science.
2.2

C REATIVE R EASONING IN LLM S

Prior work has explored individual mechanisms related to creative reasoning, including analogical
reasoning (Yu et al., 2023; Yasunaga et al., 2023) and inductive reasoning (Cheng et al., 2024;
Wang et al., 2023), as well as agentic systems for scientific discovery such as MOOSE-Chem, The
AI Scientist, and AlphaEvolve (Yang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024; Novikov et al., 2025). These
systems show that iterative retrieval, recombination, refinement, and search can improve candidate
solutions and sometimes produce novel outputs. However, they generally do not explicitly specify
creativity-oriented search mechanisms for deliberately reaching unfamiliar solutions or changing the
space of possibilities being explored.
More recent work explicitly connects LLMs with computational creativity, particularly combinational
creativity (Schapiro et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2024). In contrast, UoT provides a unified operationalization of combinational, exploratory, and transformational creativity as distinct search regimes:
transferring structure across contexts, exploring new solutions within a fixed space, and modifying
the rules that define the space itself. This common formulation enables direct comparison of the three
modes and analysis of their distinct strengths and failure modes.
2.3

B ENCHMARKING C REATIVITY IN LLM S

Creativity benchmarks span a spectrum from open-ended generation to more constrained problem
solving. Open-ended evaluations cover divergent thinking (Hou et al., 2025), story generation
(Li et al., 2025), scientific idea generation (Ruan et al., 2026; Qiu et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025),
and creative writing (Sen et al., 2026). These settings allow substantial novelty and diversity, but
functional effectiveness can be difficult to assess objectively.
More constrained benchmarks provide clearer evaluation criteria, including MacGyver-style physical
problem solving (Tian et al., 2024) and scientific-discovery tasks involving law discovery (Zheng
et al., 2026), multimodal discovery (Liu et al., 2026a; Panigrahi et al., 2026), AI model discovery
(Wu et al., 2026), end-to-end research (Zhang et al., 2026), and scientific hypothesis generation (Liu
et al., 2026b; Lew et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2026). Their stronger
grounding makes utility easier to evaluate, but also narrows the admissible solution space.
This leaves an important evaluation gap. Existing benchmarks often either support open-ended idea
generation, where utility is difficult to assess concretely, or impose stronger constraints that narrow
the admissible solution space. We therefore introduce three low-constraint creative-reasoning tasks
that allow both novelty and utility to be evaluated while preserving multiple valid solution strategies.
Because our primary contribution is the reasoning framework rather than benchmark construction, we
also evaluate UoT on HypoArena (Zhong et al., 2026), a scientific hypothesis-generation benchmark
that permits multiple plausible hypotheses and pairwise comparison rather than requiring reproduction
of a single reference hypothesis.

reasoning into analogue → mechanism abstraction → target transfer.
Operationally, C-UoT proposes distant analogues, solves the source problem, abstracts the transferable
mechanism, maps that mechanism into the target context, and evaluates the resulting candidate. This
explicit decomposition also makes the reasoning process inspectable, allowing failures to be localized
to analogue retrieval, mechanism abstraction, or downstream transfer.

Directly searching for novel solutions in a large conceptual space is difficult. E-UoT therefore

starts from an existing candidate and searches for neighboring alternatives that have not yet been
explored. Repeating this process expands the frontier of Kt and gradually reaches new regions of the

Operationally, E-UoT iteratively proposes such neighboring candidates, evaluates them, and retains
promising ones for further expansion. The defining constraint is that the problem formulation and
governing rules remain fixed throughout the search, so novelty arises from traversing previously
unvisited parts of the existing space rather than importing external structure or transforming the space
itself.

E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP

We evaluate UoT across task regimes characterized by two properties that directly shape the creative
search problem: degree of constraint and objective specificity. The degree of constraint captures how
strongly problem requirements restrict the feasible solution space; stronger constraints narrow the set
of admissible solutions and limit the extent to which alternative mechanisms, rules, or solutions can be
accommodated. Objective specificity captures how precisely the desired outcome is defined. A more
5

Preprint

specific objective narrows the search target and provides a clearer criterion for evaluating candidate
solutions, whereas a less specific objective permits broader notions of relevance and similarity,
expanding the range of potentially useful analogies that can be retrieved from other domains. These
dimensions therefore affect both the space over which creative reasoning operates and how that space
can be searched and evaluated.
As illustrated in Fig. 2, we evaluate UoT in two complementary settings spanning these dimensions.
HypoArena (Zhong et al., 2026) provides externally defined scientific hypothesis-generation tasks
with relatively high degrees of constraint, while spanning higher- and lower-specificity objectives. To
complement this regime, we introduce three novel creative-reasoning tasks with substantially lower
degrees of constraint and expansive solution spaces: Bridge and Electricity provide relatively specific
objectives, while Society evaluates reasoning under a less specific objective. Together, these settings
cover distinct regions of the task space and allow us to examine how creative reasoning methods
behave as the structure of the underlying search problem changes.

4.1

N OVEL C REATIVE -R EASONING TASKS

We construct three tasks spanning transportation, energy, and social intervention: One-Lane Bridge,
which requires designing a traffic-control policy under fixed crossing constraints; Electricity Tariff,
which requires reducing peak demand without curtailing critical usage; and Social Cohesion, which
requires increasing cross-group interaction while preserving privacy, inclusivity, and safety. Full task
definitions are provided in Appendix D.1.
Following the standard view of creativity as requiring both novelty and usefulness (Runco & Jaeger,
2012), we report Creativity as the mean of Novelty and Utility, with Feasibility reported separately
as a validity measure. Outputs are canonicalized before evaluation to reduce effects of response
length and presentation. Ten PhD students provide human judgments, and Gemini 2.5 Pro provides
automated evaluations. Full scoring procedures, novelty computation, prompts, and known-solution
baselines are provided in Appendix F.

4.2

H YPOA RENA

We evaluate on a fixed panel of 100 HypoArena tasks from its three single-hypothesis research
domains: biomedical science, machine learning, and social science (34/34/32 tasks). Final outputs
are canonicalized into their core hypotheses before pairwise evaluation.
We follow the HypoArena evaluation protocol using Gemma-3-12B as the primary judge. Pairwise
judgments are aggregated with Bradley–Terry–Davidson (BTD) ratings, and we additionally report
pairwise win rate. HypoArena emphasizes contextual grounding and evidential justification while
also rewarding non-obvious inferential insight, making it suitable for evaluating creative reasoning
under scientific constraints. Additional evaluation details and judge-robustness analyses are provided
in the Appendix (Appendix E.3 and Appendix D.7).

4.3

BASELINES AND BACKBONES

We compare UoT against general-purpose reasoning baselines spanning direct generation and increasingly structured search: OnePrompt, Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022), Tree of Thoughts
(ToT) (Yao et al., 2023), and Enhanced Graph of Thoughts (EGoT) (Shin & Kim, 2025). We focus on
matched general-purpose reasoning frameworks rather than specialized scientific-discovery systems,
which often incorporate additional retrieval pipelines, tools, or domain-specific assumptions.
For the novel creative-reasoning tasks, GPT-4o is used as the shared backbone for UoT and all
matched reasoning baselines; GPT-5 and DeepSeek V3.1 are included only as contextual reference
models. For HypoArena, Qwen3-14B is the primary shared backbone, and GLM-4-9B is additionally
evaluated to assess backbone sensitivity. Full implementation settings are provided in Appendix E.5.

R ESULTS

5.1

N OVEL C REATIVE -R EASONING TASK R ESULTS

Table 1 summarizes Creativity scores under human and automated evaluation. Among matched
GPT-4o reasoning methods, T-UoT performs strongest on Bridge and Electricity, achieving the
highest human-rated Creativity on Bridge (0.680) and Electricity (0.620), as well as the highest
LLM-rated scores on Bridge (0.698) and Electricity (0.526).
As shown in Fig. 2, Bridge and Electricity combine relatively low degrees of constraint with high
objective specificity. A lower degree of constraint leaves greater flexibility in the rule-generation
stage pθ (R′ | P, H, R), since more alternative rule sets R′ can modify the search space while still
permitting solutions that satisfy the immutable requirements H. High objective specificity, in turn,
provides a clearer criterion for evaluating and selecting among the generated rule sets R′ , helping
T-UoT identify transformations that are most promising for the target objective.

Preprint

C-UoT shows the complementary pattern on Society, where it ties for the highest human-rated
Creativity among matched GPT-4o methods (0.560) and achieves the highest LLM-rated score
(0.649). As illustrated in Fig. 2, Society combines a low degree of constraint with relatively low
objective specificity. Lower objective specificity makes the analogue-retrieval stage pθ (a | P ) less
restrictive, allowing a broader range of problems from other domains to be considered functionally
relevant to the target. Once a transferable mechanism m is identified, the low degree of constraint
makes the transfer stage pθ (s | P, m) less restrictive, since fewer hard requirements must be
satisfied when adapting that mechanism to the target problem. Together, these conditions favor both
analogue discovery and cross-domain mechanism transfer, consistent with C-UoT’s stronger relative
performance on Society.
The full Novelty, Utility, and Feasibility breakdown is provided in Appendix D.2. Human and
automated evaluations show substantial agreement at the method level (r=0.708; Table 8), supporting
the use of automated scores as a complement to human judgments. Representative examples in
Appendix D.4 illustrate the distinct search behaviors induced by the three UoT modes.
5.2

H YPOA RENA R ESULTS

On Qwen3-14B (Table 2), E-UoT achieves the strongest overall performance, ranking first in both
biomedical science and machine learning, while ToT performs best in social science. E-UoT reaches
an average BTD rating of 1535.4 and a pairwise win rate of 32.7%, compared with 1515.8 and 25.5%
for the next-best method, ToT. With GLM-4-9B, OnePrompt ranks first while E-UoT remains the
strongest structured method, indicating backbone dependence (Table 10).
As illustrated in Fig. 2, HypoArena tasks have a relatively high degree of constraint, while the
biomedical and machine-learning domains additionally provide relatively specific objectives. Both
properties make the local expansion stage pθ (s′ | P, sk , R) more directed. A higher degree of
constraint narrows the admissible solution space, reducing the range of viable modifications around
the current candidate sk . High objective specificity provides additional problem context about what
constitutes a useful improvement, further guiding the search toward promising neighboring solutions.
These conditions are therefore well matched to E-UoT’s strategy of iteratively expanding within the
existing solution space while keeping the governing rules R fixed.
C-UoT and T-UoT are less reliable on average in these more constrained regimes, as cross-domain
transfer and rule transformation must satisfy a narrower set of admissible conditions. Nevertheless,
their lower aggregate rankings do not imply that these search modes are uniformly unsuccessful.
Under the primary Gemma judge, C-UoT is top-ranked on 15/100 HypoArena tasks (8 uniquely),
while T-UoT is top-ranked on 11/100 tasks (6 uniquely). These task-specific advantages also
recur across judges: C-UoT and T-UoT outperform ToT on 26 and 24 tasks, respectively, under
at least two of three judges, and on 11 tasks each under all three judges (Appendix D.8). Thus,
although exploratory search is more reliable on average in the highly constrained HypoArena
setting, combinational and transformational search can still uncover stronger hypothesis directions on
particular problems.
The main ranking conclusions are not specific to the Gemma judge: re-scoring the same frozen
Qwen3-14B outputs with two additional judges preserves the relative advantages of E-UoT over
OnePrompt and ToT (ρ = 0.59–0.79 across judge pairs), while hard preference reversals remain rare
(2.6–5.4%; Appendix D.7).
Operator ablation. To isolate individual creativity-specific operations, we remove the structured
cross-domain transfer in C-UoT, the option to switch between mechanisms during E-UoT expansion,
and rule transformation in T-UoT while otherwise preserving each mode’s search procedure. A
position-debiased pairwise judge compares full and ablated outputs on the same 100 HypoArena
tasks (Table 3).

is preferred in 54.2% of comparisons, providing a smaller but consistent indication that explicitly
modifying the rule set can expose useful hypothesis directions beyond the same downstream search

C ONCLUSIONS AND F UTURE W ORK

We introduced Universe of Thoughts (UoT), a computational framework that operationalizes combinational, exploratory, and transformational creativity as distinct executable search processes for
Large Language Models. Rather than treating creativity as a single undifferentiated capability, UoT
separates cross-domain recombination, within-space exploration, and transformation of the rules that
define the solution space.
Across complementary creative-reasoning and scientific hypothesis-generation settings, the three
search modes exhibit distinct performance patterns depending on task structure. Combinational
search performs particularly well when broader objectives permit wider analogue discovery and
relatively few constraints facilitate mechanism transfer; exploratory search benefits from more
constrained, higher-specificity settings that provide stronger guidance for local improvement; and
transformational search performs strongly when flexible solution spaces permit viable rule changes
while clear objectives support their evaluation. These results suggest that the effectiveness of different
creative search processes depends in part on the structure of the problem, rather than one mode being
uniformly strongest across settings.
The current implementation nevertheless depends substantially on the reasoning, search, abstraction,
and transfer capabilities of the underlying foundation model. Future work can therefore investigate
models trained explicitly to support these creative operations, with the goal of improving their

A

C ONNECTION TO B ODEN ’ S D EFINITIONS

Our computational formulation follows Boden’s distinction among combinational, exploratory, and
transformational creativity (Boden, 2004; 2007; 2009). Boden characterizes the three modes as
follows (bracketed glosses are added by us):
1. Combinational creativity. “Combinational creativity, produces unfamiliar combinations
[i.e., solutions] of familiar ideas, and it works by making associations between ideas that
were previously only indirectly linked.”
2. Exploratory creativity. “In exploratory creativity, the person moves through the [solution]
space, exploring it to find out what’s there (including previously unvisited locations [i.e.,
new ideas])—and, in the most interesting cases, to discover both the potential and the limits
of the space in question.”
3. Transformational creativity. “In transformational creativity, a [solution] space or style
itself is transformed by altering (or dropping) one or more of its defining dimensions [i.e.,
rules].”
We translate these cognitive descriptions into computational search operations. In C-UoT, an “unfamiliar combination” is operationalized as transferring a functionally relevant mechanism from
a contextually different source into the target problem. In E-UoT, movement through the existing
conceptual space is operationalized as expanding the currently explored candidate set while keeping
the problem rules fixed. In T-UoT, transformation of the conceptual space is operationalized as
modifying mutable rules or assumptions while preserving the task’s immutable requirements.

We reinterpret these three modes as distinct strategies for a common computational problem:
Given a problem and the solutions already explored, what should a reasoning
system change in order to reach a useful solution that has not yet been considered?
This perspective yields three different search regimes. Combinational reasoning changes the source
of structure used to construct a solution; exploratory reasoning changes the candidate solution while
preserving the current rules; and transformational reasoning changes the rules or assumptions that
define which solutions are admissible. These distinctions define the computational structure of
Universe of Thoughts (UoT). The staged conditional-generation formalization of each mode appears
in Section 3; full pseudocode and selection details are provided in Appendix B and Appendix C.

B.1

E XECUTABLE O PERATOR I NTERFACES

All UoT operators are instantiated as structured LLM calls. Across both evaluation settings, the three
modes share the same search objects—external structure, candidate solutions under fixed rules, and
mutable assumptions—while differing in intermediate representation and selection budgets.

C-UoT. G ENERATE A NALOGUES proposes source problems that are functionally related to the
target but contextually distinct from already explored solutions. S OLVE S OURCE obtains a source-side
solution or structured account of how the analogue addresses its problem. E XTRACT M ECHANISMS
removes source-specific surface detail and retains the transferable mechanism. T RANSFER A ND S YN THESIZE instantiates that mechanism in the target problem and produces a candidate solution or
hypothesis. Soft quality filters and final ranking then retain promising transfers (Appendix C).

E-UoT. E XPAND generates neighboring candidates while preserving the problem formulation and
mutable rules. Each expansion explores either a different direction by changing the current mechanism
(S HIFT) or an underexplored consequence of the current mechanism (D EEPEN). Candidates are
evaluated and retained by the shared search controller (LLMvote / beam retention), matching the
Tree-of-Thoughts control structure used by the ToT baseline. F INALIZE converts the final beam into
submitted outputs.

T-UoT. E XTRACT RULES identifies mutable assumptions and separates them from immutable task
requirements. G ENERATE T RANSFORMATIONS proposes modifications to those assumptions (e.g.,
remove, relax, invert, add, or replace). A retained transformation must preserve the original task goal
and core difficulty while opening a meaningfully different solution region. R EFRAME constructs the
working problem representation under the selected transformed rules, after which C REATIVE S EARCH
runs downstream solution search in that reframed space.

FAILURE -M ODE D IAGNOSIS AND I NTERVENTIONS

Stage-level diagnosis. The C-UoT trace analysis suggests that failures often occur after analogy
retrieval rather than because no meaningful analogy is found (Table 15). An analogue and mapping
are selected on essentially all tasks, and mapped-mechanism specificity is nearly identical when
C-UoT does and does not beat ToT. In contrast, preservation of mapped mechanism content into the
final hypothesis is higher when C-UoT beats ToT, with strong transfer more frequent in wins than
non-wins. This pattern motivates the downstream-synthesis intervention below.
For T-UoT, transformation execution alone does not distinguish success (Table 16): rule changes
are applied in both winning and losing cases, and lexical preservation into the final hypothesis is
weak in both groups. Among the 12 largest applied-transform failures, 7/12 involved reasonable
transformations that were not converted into competitive hypotheses, while 5/12 involved weak,
misaimed, or derailing transformations. This suggests a dual bottleneck for T-UoT: downstream
consequence reasoning is often weak, while the transformation gate itself can also admit poor rule
changes.
To test whether the diagnosed C-UoT and T-UoT bottlenecks are actionable, we perform targeted
interventions on frozen failure cases while keeping the upstream creative search fixed (Table 17). For
C-UoT, we preserve the retrieved analogy and mapping and modify only the final synthesis using a
human-written rewrite; this intervention tests whether the frozen intermediate analogy can support a
stronger final hypothesis when downstream synthesis is improved. For T-UoT, we keep the selected
transformation fixed but require the model to explicitly derive one mechanistic consequence of that
transformation before producing the final hypothesis.

E XPERIMENTAL D ESIGN

E.1

N OVEL C REATIVE -R EASONING TASK C ONSTRUCTION AND VALIDATION

Although the novel creative-reasoning tasks do not rely on external domain simulators (e.g., physics
engines), they were constructed and validated through a human-in-the-loop process to ensure logical
consistency and accessibility. We first generated ten candidate open-ended tasks, then manually
solved each candidate to verify that the problem statement was coherent and that the solution space
was reachable. From this pool we selected three diverse tasks (One-Lane Bridge, Electricity Tariff,
and Social Cohesion) and refined their rules to balance assessable constraints against sufficient
open-endedness for creative reasoning.
Our goal was to isolate creative reasoning rather than domain-expertise retrieval. Heavy domainspecific constraints that require complex engineering simulations would risk confounding the two.
By restricting the tasks to self-contained logic settings validated by human solvers, solution validity
is defined by explicit task constraints and objectives, which supports reproducible evaluation.
E.2

B ENCHMARK S ELECTION R ATIONALE

As discussed in Section 2.3, existing creativity benchmarks tend to emphasize either highly openended generation or more tightly constrained problem solving. Open-ended ideation tasks permit
diverse outputs but often make functional success difficult to assess objectively, whereas benchmarks
such as MacGyver (Tian et al., 2024) and scientific hypothesis-generation tasks provide clearer
problem definitions but impose stronger constraints on the admissible solution space. Our novel
creative-reasoning tasks and HypoArena evaluation are chosen as complementary settings that jointly
cover clear objectives with expansive solution spaces and scientifically grounded multi-hypothesis
comparison.
E.3

H YPOA RENA P ROTOCOL

HypoArena provides scientific hypothesis-generation cases grounded in source documents. We
restrict evaluation to its three single-hypothesis research domains—biomedical science, machine
learning, and social science. The remaining domains—financial analysis, IT operations, and safety
26

Preprint

investigation—require multi-hypothesis submissions and are therefore outside the scope of our singlehypothesis generation setting. From the eligible research-domain pool, we evaluate a fixed panel of
100 tasks, stratified approximately evenly across domains (34 biomedical, 34 machine learning, and
32 social science). The same panel is held fixed across all methods and backbone variants.
Because different reasoning methods can produce outputs with substantially different levels of detail,
we first canonicalize each final response into its main core hypothesis before pairwise evaluation.
This ensures that comparisons focus on the underlying scientific idea rather than rewarding longer or
more elaborated explanations.
HypoArena is not designed as a direct metric of creativity; its primary rubric dimensions emphasize
Contextual Grounding and Evidential Justification. At the same time, the Inferential Insight dimension
explicitly asks whether a hypothesis synthesizes context observations into a non-obvious but still
context-constrained explanatory or mechanistic claim. Because judges therefore partially reward
non-obvious scientific insight when it remains well supported, creative hypotheses can still receive
high scores.
For each task, an LLM judge compares every pair of methods using bidirectional prompts and a
five-level preference scale ranging from a clear preference for one hypothesis to a clear preference
for the other, with ties permitted. Judgments follow the HypoArena rubric, emphasizing context
grounding and analytical quality rather than stylistic fluency or verbosity.
We use Gemma-3-12B as the primary judge. Pairwise outcomes are aggregated with the Bradley–
Terry–Davidson (BTD) model, initialized at 1500, and we additionally report pairwise win rate.
BTD ratings are computed separately within each domain, and the overall score is the mean of
the biomedical, machine-learning, and social-science ratings. Judge-consistency analyses under
additional evaluators are reported in Appendix D.7.
E.4

BASELINES

Our primary goal is to evaluate whether explicitly modeling creative reasoning provides benefits
beyond general-purpose reasoning and search strategies. We compare UoT against the following
established reasoning frameworks across both evaluation settings:
• OnePrompt: The task description is provided directly to the model without an explicit
reasoning scaffold.
• Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022): The model generates a sequential step-by-step
reasoning trajectory.
• Tree of Thoughts (ToT) (Yao et al., 2023): The model explores multiple branching reasoning
trajectories through iterative generation, evaluation, and selection.
• Enhanced Graph of Thoughts (EGoT) (Shin & Kim, 2025): A graph-based reasoning
framework that supports generation, aggregation, and refinement of intermediate thoughts.
These baselines span increasingly structured forms of general-purpose reasoning, from direct generation to sequential, tree-based, and graph-based search. This allows us to test whether explicitly
modeling creative reasoning provides benefits beyond increasing the complexity of generic reasoning
search.
We intentionally focus on general-purpose reasoning frameworks rather than domain-specific
scientific-discovery agents. UoT is designed as a domain-agnostic creative reasoning framework, and
our HypoArena experiment tests whether the same creative reasoning procedures transfer to scientific
hypothesis generation under matched reasoning conditions, rather than whether UoT outperforms
specialized discovery systems that rely on additional retrieval pipelines, tools, or domain-specific
assumptions.

F

N OVEL C REATIVE -R EASONING TASK E VALUATION M ETRICS AND
P ROMPTS

Our three-task evaluation framework is guided by the classic definition of creativity (Runco &
Jaeger, 2012), which comprises two primary components: Utility and Novelty. We also introduce
Feasibility as a critical third metric to ensure solutions are valid and practical by adhering to all
problem constraints.
A key challenge in evaluating LLM-generated outputs is their varying granularity. One model might
offer a concise core idea, while another provides a detailed plan with sub-solutions. To ensure a fair
comparison focused on the generated core creative insight, we introduce a Solution Canonicalization
step. Before scoring, each generated solution is processed by an LLM (Gemini 2.5 Pro, temperature 0)
to extract its single, core conceptual idea. This pre-processing step standardizes all outputs to a
consistent format, enabling an equitable assessment of creativity. Following canonicalization, each
solution is assessed using the metrics below:
• Utility: This metric measures how effectively the solution solves the problem. It is scored
on a continuous scale from 0 to 1. An LLM evaluator places the solution on a 0–10 rubric
relative to known solutions (and an optional optimal-solution upper reference), then rescales
the score to [0, 1].
• Novelty: To measure conceptual novelty, we first extract a short conceptual fingerprint
(“theme”) of the canonicalized solution with Gemini 2.5 Pro. We then embed that theme
with gemini-embedding-001 and compute its cosine distance from the embeddings
of known solution concepts. The novelty score is 1 − max(similarity) to known concepts,
normalized by the maximum known-to-optimal concept distance so that the optimal reference
provides a practical upper bound on the scale.
• Feasibility: This metric is scored from 0 to 1 and assesses whether a solution is plausible and
meets the rules of the problem. The LLM evaluator is provided with a pre-defined checklist
of all explicit constraints and scores the solution based on adherence to those constraints.
The final Creativity Score is the mean of the Utility and Novelty scores. Feasibility is reported
separately as a validity check.

For each novel creative-reasoning task, we maintain a fixed set of known solutions: author-curated
conventional baselines that represent standard approaches to the problem, not outputs of any evaluated
model. Novelty compares each candidate against short conceptual fingerprints of these baselines
(known solutions concept); Utility uses the corresponding fuller descriptions as calibration
anchors. The baselines used in our experiments are:

Society. (1) Structured mixers that force cross-group interaction. (2) Cross-group micro-grants
