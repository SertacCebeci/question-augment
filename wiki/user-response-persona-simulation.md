# User response persona simulation

A clarifying question is answered by a user, and the user's reply decides whether the next turn can be an answer. ClarifyMT-Bench makes this reply a controlled variable. Its simulator "models user behavior along five dimensions: information coverage, truthfulness, self-consistency, cooperativeness, and specificity."[^c1] Those dimensions are combined into six canonical personas. **Precise** gives "specific, accurate, and fully relevant information that directly fills the target slot."[^c2] **Partial–Vague** "offers partially relevant but underspecified or indecisive responses with low specificity." **Off–Focus** "replies with off-topic or tangential content that does not address the requested slot."[^c3] **Contradictory** "gives internally or cross-turn inconsistent responses that hinder stable intent inference."[^c4] **Factually–Wrong** "provides incorrect factual information." **Refusal** "declines to clarify, avoids answering, or explicitly urges the model to proceed without clarification."[^c5]

The personas differ in what the next turn should be. The paper states that "Precise and Refusal users typically provide enough information or explicitly decline to provide more, permitting a direct answer," while "four personas generally require additional clarification."[^c6] So the same reference decision, Clarify, is followed by a reply that either supplies the missing slot or leaves it open, and the persona is what separates the two. This is the mechanism that makes the simulator useful: it turns "the user was vague" into a labelled condition under which a model's decisions can be scored.

The results use this condition. Accuracy varies by persona, and "user behavior is a major factor shaping clarification performance."[^c7] Performance on Refusal-style inputs is "uniformly low," which the authors read as over-clarification (see [[ask-answer-error-direction]]). The personas also separate the agentic method from the baselines: its gains are largest on the noisy personas, and the paper says it "does not explicitly optimize for the Precise persona" ([[persona-inference-before-ask-answer]]).

The replies are generated by an LLM prompted to a persona, and "validated by human annotators for quality and diversity."[^c8] The annotator reliability figure in the paper (Cohen's κ = 0.5980) is for binary retain-or-remove decisions on sampled instances, not for whether simulated replies resemble real users.[^c9]

## Limits

- Dialogues are short by design: "We limit each simulated dialogue to two or three turns."[^c10] The paper argues that "extending them synthetically risks accumulating distributional artifacts."[^c11] The simulator is therefore not tested on long, drifting conversations.
- The personas are discrete categories drawn by the same generator that produced the data. Nothing in the sections I read compares them with reply behaviour from real users, so the simulator measures how models respond to these labelled replies, not how often real users give them.
- Each persona is a single behavioural mode. Real users combine them (vague and off-focus at once), and the text does not model mixtures.
- Refusal and Factually–Wrong are different kinds of problem: one asks the system to proceed, the other gives false content to act on. Averaging them into one accuracy hides this, and the paper's per-persona table is the only place the split appears.
