# Referring cue density

A referring expression names one region of an image by a set of visual cues: the colour or material of the target, its place relative to other objects, its role in the scene. **Cue density** is the number of such cues divided by the number of words. RefBench-PRO uses this measure when it sets its expressions against those of older benchmarks, defining "semantic density, defined as the number of visual cues per word."[^c1]

Two expressions of the same length can differ in density. An expression that spends its words on context the target does not need carries few cues per word. The authors state the contrast with Ref-L4 directly: Ref-L4 "generates longer expressions containing context-irrelevant content, whereas our RefBench-PRO produces comparably long descriptions by incorporating discriminative visual cues, achieving higher information density."[^c2]

The construction makes the difference deliberate rather than incidental. A vision-language model first writes "a structured property dictionary encoding the visual elements, including the object category c, inherent attributes a, interactive relationships with other objects r, and a brief description phrase d."[^c4] Each property is then checked against the region, and the expression is validated by a uniqueness check that ensures "the expression unambiguously refers to a single, distinct region in the image."[^c3] Length is therefore not the design target; the cues the expression spends per word are.

For asking, the measure separates two things a longer question can do. Each word that adds a discriminating cue narrows the set of regions the question could mean. A word that adds no cue only lengthens the question and leaves the match set as large as before. Density is a property of the formulation itself, distinct from how small or hard the target is to see. My reading is that a question can be long and still leave several candidates, which is the situation [[referent-match-set-ambiguity]] types by match-set size.

## Limits

- The text does not say how cues are counted or by whom. The density values appear in a figure comparing four benchmarks, and the numbers are not in the extracted text; the comparison rests on the authors' description of that figure.
- The claim is about benchmark construction statistics. In the sections read I found no breakdown of model accuracy by cue density, so the measure is not shown to change how well a grounder or a human answers.
- The contrast is with one benchmark family (RefCOCO-style sets and Ref-L4). Human referring questions in ordinary conversation are not measured.
- The expressions are generated by Qwen2.5-VL-72B and then corrected by annotators. A generator with a different prompt would produce other densities; that is my reading, not a tested result.
