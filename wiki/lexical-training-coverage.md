# Lexical training-data coverage

The question is whether the words of a question, and of a model's answer, have been seen in the pretraining corpus, and how often. The measurement is a lookup, not a probe of the model. The paper builds a suffix array over RedPajama's pretraining corpus, tokenized with the model's own tokenizer, and queries it for each n-gram (1 to 5 tokens) of the prompt and of the generation.[^c1] For the prompt it also extracts answer-seeking key phrases, generated by a language model, and counts those too.[^c2] Each text then gets a feature. One is the mean raw count of its n-grams, Sraw. The other is a count-based n-gram score, Sng, which takes the log ratio of each n-gram's count to its prefix's count and averages it. The paper reads this score as "a data-driven measure of lexical familiarity".[^c3]

The features are tested two ways: alone, by AUROC against a hallucination label, and inside small classifiers together with the model's own log-probabilities. Alone, the occurrence features lose. "generation token logprobabilities consistently outperform all other features in AUROC".[^c4] Their direction also depends on the side of the exchange. On the prompt side, more frequent wording goes with fewer hallucinations in open-domain QA, with an AUROC of about 0.6, and shows no measurable correlation in extractive QA.[^c5] On the generation side the trend is S-shaped. Very frequent generations are often wrong, because they are templated: "hallucinated, reflecting memorized or templated" output. Less frequent generations track faithfulness.[^c6] In extractive QA the picture is cleaner. The frequency of the answer span "is a strong indicator of truthfulness."[^c7]

Added to log-probabilities, the occurrence features give gains in test accuracy with decision trees and a small MLP. The gains are largest on NQ-Open, "observed on NQ-Open, where questions are more" difficult and the log-probability signal is less reliable.[^c8] The mechanism the paper gives is format rather than knowledge. A shallow tree separates a cluster of generations that repeat input-style prompts instead of answering. "Such artifacts cluster within a narrow 2-gram occurrence" range under the pretraining distribution, so a count flags a case that the probability alone misses.[^c9]

Zero counts are common, not exceptional. Even simple natural-language questions contain spans that never occur in the corpus: "21.1% of 4-grams and 44.4% of 5-grams in NQ" have no occurrence.[^c10] The paper reads this as models being exposed to sequences they "have never explicitly encountered during training", so that they must generalize compositionally rather than by memorized sequence.[^c11]

What survives the transfer from corpus statistics to answer checking is a cheap, model-agnostic proxy for how much of a question's wording the model has seen. The paper's motivation is the long-tail effect, in which QA accuracy "declines for entities with limited" presence in the pretraining corpus.[^c12] What does not survive is meaning. A count is over exact token sequences, so a paraphrase of a well-covered fact scores like a rare one. The n-gram score Sng is borrowed from count-based language modelling, and the paper uses it as a familiarity score, not as a probability of the answer being true; the ratio of counts is not a calibrated likelihood (my inference).

For a question-asker, the idea suggests that the wording of a question carries a signal about whether an answer will be recalled or reconstructed. For an answerer, a generation whose surface is very common in training is a candidate for template output rather than for knowledge. Both readings are my inference from correlational results; the paper does not test either use.

## Limits

- The relation is correlational, and the paper says so: "our results are correlational and do not establish a causal relationship between training data" exposure and hallucination behavior.[^c13]
- Matching is exact. The paper's analysis "does not account for paraphrasing, synonymy, or semantic similarity."[^c14]
- One corpus and one model family. The experiments use RedPajama-INCITE models at 3B and 7B, and the book subset is omitted. The suffix array "does not reflect the full pretraining corpora used in proprietary models", and the authors say the findings "may not fully generalize to larger models".[^c15]
- The standalone signal is weak. The paper's own words are that "occurrence-based features yield weaker and" more variable signals than log-probabilities.[^c16]
- The gains are small. The paper calls the stability improvement "moderate in magnitude": "This improvement, though moderate in magnitude, reflects" a directional trend, not a large effect.[^c17]
- The paper's own summary is that occurrence features "capture a weak but consistent bias associated" with hallucinated answers, and that their "entanglement with data frequency and format limits their standalone predictive power".[^c18][^c19]
- The reading of the gains as format artifacts is one tree's split on one dataset. The paper does not show that the feature separates hallucinations that are not format-driven (my inference).
