Abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards
has driven major gains in LLM reasoning, and
it is intuitive to assume this recipe will transfer
well to multimodal models. However, multimodal models do two things: first, perceive
what is in an image, then reason about what
it implies. Because these stages are graded
jointly, it is hard to tell how much room reasoning alone has to grow. We study this on algorithmic visual puzzles, where both components
are necessary and show that perception, not
reasoning, is the binding constraint. Replacing
images with simple textual descriptions raises
performance by over 20 points on average for
Claude models. We then evaluate six reward
designs aimed at inducing visual grounding during reasoning without chain-of-thought supervision. Training Qwen-2.5-VL-7B with GRPO,
reward design induces long, structured reasoning with self-reflection and visual references,
yielding a 5.56-point gain over the base model.
These gains are, however, uneven; no single reward improves all categories, and rewards with
verifiable accuracy signals trade out-of-domain
transfer for in-domain accuracy. These results
point to perception-aware reward design as a
path forward, so that signals correct perception at its source rather than the reasoning that
inherits its errors.

Introduction

We tend to evaluate multimodal models in a simplified way: the answer is right or it’s not, and
what happened between seeing the problem and
writing the answer is effectively a black box. This
obscures the fact that there are two steps in multimodal problems: perception and reasoning. For
example, given an image of an analog clock and
asked what time it is an hour later, the model must
first see what is in the image (read the clock), and
* Equal advising.

then reason about it (add an hour). A wrong answer
can mean it misread the image, miscomputed from
a correct reading, or both. From the answer alone,
we cannot immediately tell which one is true. Indeed, while modern such models appear to excel
on tasks requiring extensive domain knowledge,
they often struggle with tasks that demand visual
perception (Shojaee et al., 2025).
A related observation concerns how current
MLLMs (Yang et al., 2024, 2025a), despite being trained to reason in many cases, often produce
reasoning that is weakly grounded in the visual
input (Fan et al., 2025a). Indeed, in our own experiments, models frequently address the algorithmic component of a question without explicit reference to the image, or jump directly to an answer
with minimal intermediate description. Two failure
modes thus appear together: inadequate interpretation of the image, and a tendency to bypass visual
grounding altogether in the reasoning chain.
Investigating these failure modes requires both
(i) an evaluation setting in which perception and
reasoning can be disentangled, and (ii) studying
training methods that can shape both. For (i), we
turn to visual puzzles, which require both accurate visual perception and algorithmic reasoning.
These have become an effective way to evaluate
reasoning capabilities in MLLMs independent of
specialized knowledge (Chia et al., 2024; Ghosal
et al., 2025), showing that such models consistently
underperform relative to other reasoning-oriented
tasks (Song et al., 2025). They thus provide a useful setting for asking where in the pipeline performance breaks down. In other words, we ask what
is the bottleneck of MLLMs for solving visual
puzzles? Then, to separate the contributions of
perception and reasoning to this gap, we hold the
reasoning task constant and vary only the perceptual demand.
For (ii), we turn to reinforcement learning motivated by its empirical success in encouraging ef-

Design of Reward Functions

We design six reward functions that vary along
three complementary axes: (i) how the final answer is scored: by exact match, by partial credit
for near-correct answers, or not at all; (ii) what
intermediate output structure is rewarded: a single
<think> block, or multiple stages spanning image
description, reasoning, and self-reflection; and (iii)
whether visual grounding is explicitly rewarded
through a dedicated <visual> tag. Each variant
thus probes a distinct hypothesis about which components of the reward signal contribute to long,
image-grounded multimodal reasoning. Table 1
summarizes the six variants and their motivating
questions; all are implemented within a single modular scoring framework, with prompts and pseudocode provided in Tables 9 and 10.
2.1

Reward Variants and Implementation

RFORMAT : Following the formulation in
DeepSeek-AI (2025), this variant rewards both
the presence of a <think> block and exact-match

accuracy on the <answer> content. It tests whether
the standard text-domain recipe (i.e. format plus
accuracy) is sufficient in the multimodal setting.
ROUTCOME : This variant rewards only exactmatch accuracy on the <answer> content, with no
credit for intermediate structure. It isolates the
contribution of the accuracy signal alone and tests
whether reasoning structure can be elicited from
the prompt without being explicitly rewarded, motivated by DAPO (Yu et al., 2025).
RSTRUCTURED : This
variant
distributes
credit across three reasoning stages (<image
description>, <think>, and <rethink>) in
addition to the final answer. Each stage is scored
for both multiple-tag presence and the amount of
unique content within the tag, with repetition penalized and counts smoothed by tanh(·) to prevent
reward hacking through redundant output. Answer
correctness is assigned the highest weight in the
composite to maintain factual accuracy. The goal
is to test whether explicitly rewarding multi-stage
structure induces longer, more grounded reasoning
trajectories without chain-of-thought supervision.
RGRADED : Identical to RSTRUCTURED but replaces
exact-match accuracy with partial credit for nearcorrect answers (e.g. prediction of 4 or 6 when
ground truth is 5). All other components (repetition
penalties, tanh(·) smoothing, and stage-wise structural rewards) remain unchanged, to test whether
softer accuracy supervision improves reasoning,
e.g. on tasks with dense numeric answer spaces.
RGROUNDED : This reward focuses on visual
grounding by requiring models to integrate imagespecific information into their reasoning. It introduces an explicit <visual> tag inside the <think>
block and rewards unique visual descriptions
within it, alongside the standard <think> and exactmatch accuracy rewards. The same repetition
penalty and tanh(·) smoothing from RSTRUCTURED
also apply. This tests whether explicitly rewarding
image-referent content within the reasoning chain
improves visual grounding.
RPROCESS : Finally, this configuration removes
the accuracy signal entirely. The model is rewarded only for unique content in the <image
description>, <think>, and <rethink> tags,
and receives full credit for producing any <answer>
tag regardless of its content. This effectively tests
a lower bound on supervision: whether reasoning

behavior can emerge from structural rewards alone,
without any verifiable signal on correctness.

3

Dataset Collection and Generation

We use the AlgoPuzzleVQA dataset introduced by
(Ghosal et al., 2025), which comprises challenging visual puzzles designed to evaluate the capabilities of MLLMs in visual perception, language
comprehension, and algorithmic reasoning. For
our experiments, we selected nine categories from
AlgoPuzzleVQA based on their suitability for conversion into text-only representations. This enables
us to isolate and examine the reasoning bottlenecks
of MLLMs without relying on visual inputs. The
selected categories include: Clock, Checker Move,
Maze, Move Box, N-Queens, Number Slide, Tower
of Hanoi, Rotting Kiwi, and Water Jugs. Descriptions and examples are provided in Appendix C.
Training Data Generation. The dataset we use
(AlgoPuzzleVQA) provides only 100 visual samples per category, which is insufficient for RL training. To scale up the dataset, we generated additional training samples by adapting the source
code1 with necessary modifications. Specifically,
we created 9,000 new training samples across
four categories: Clock, Maze, Move Box, and NQueens. The Clock category contains 3,000 samples, while each of the other three categories includes 2,000 samples. These samples were generated by varying features such as the clock’s shape,
the color and size of the clock hands, the orientation of the grid, and the positions of the boxes and
queens. The original AlgoPuzzleVQA dataset is retained as the gold test set, and we ensured that none
of the generated training samples overlap with the
test data. Additionally, all training examples are
unique and contain no repetitions.

Experimental Setup

We design experiments to address three questions.
First, to what extent are the limitations of MLLMs
due to challenges in visual perception or algorithmic reasoning? Second, can different reward
functions affect RL training to unlock long visual
reasoning? Third, how well does the RL-trained
model generalize to out-of-distribution classes?
We extract the final answer from the model’s prediction and compare it with the ground truth using
exact match, reporting Accuracy as the evaluation

et al. (2025) evaluated model performance in both

In our preliminary experiments, we found that in
the MCQ setting, models often guessed the correct answer from the provided options, even when
their reasoning was incorrect. This limits the evaluation’s ability to accurately reflect the model’s
true capabilities. Therefore, we evaluate all models exclusively in the open-ended setting, where
no answer options are provided. We recommend
that future work adopt the open-ended format to
accurately assess models’ performance.

Bottleneck Experiments

To analyze the reasoning bottlenecks, we evaluate
Claude-3.5-Sonnet and Claude-3.7-Sonnet in
thinking mode across the nine selected categories.
We conduct the experiments in two settings: i)
Multimodal (<image, question>): where the image and corresponding question are provided to
the model, ii) Text-only (<text-representing
image, question>): where the image is converted
into a text description, which is then passed to the
model along with the question. For instance, in
the ‘Checker-Move’ category, text descriptions are
simple strings where colors are encoded as characters—‘R’ for red, ‘G’ for green, and ‘-’ for white
space. Other categories follow similarly intuitive
character-based representations. Examples of how

Training Settings

our experiments (Yang et al., 2024). We train the

reinforcement learning may influence the model’s

each aligned with a complementary hypothesis.

model’s longer reasoning abilities. Since Clock is

3,000 samples exclusively from this category, referred to as Clock-3k. Second, we hypothesize that

5.2

Diverse training data yields broad gains

As illustrated in Table 3, training with the diverse
dataset yields improvements across all reward functions compared to training solely on hard samples
(Clock-3k). The RSTRUCTURED reward achieves the
highest average score (15.56), followed closely by
the ROUTCOME reward (15.11). Interestingly, the
RPROCESS reward produces unexpected behavior: in

both settings, it surpasses the baseline, and in the
Clock-3k setting, it outperforms several other reward functions. These findings are consistent with
Shao et al. (2025), which reports that RL training
without a verifiable reward signal can still improve
performance. Nevertheless, certain categories remain challenging: the model consistently struggles
with Clock and Tower of Hanoi tasks, in line with
Fu et al. (2025), showing that these puzzles are intrinsically difficult due to abstract temporal and recursive reasoning demands. We present additional
analysis in Appendix A.
5.3

Structured rewards elicit visually
grounded reasoning, but long reasoning
alone does not guarantee accuracy

Our reward function design incentivizes the emergence of long visual reasoning in the model. Under RFORMAT and RPROCESS rewards, the model produces minimal intermediate reasoning, often jumping directly to the answer with little explanation
of the image. Both RSTRUCTURED and RGRADED
achieve the intended behavior: as shown in Figure 4, the model generates structured reasoning
steps that reference and explain different parts of
the image, exhibiting the ‘think with image’ phenomenon (Zheng et al., 2025b; Fan et al., 2025a).
This behavior emerges across both in-domain and
out-of-domain categories, indicating that the reward design and training strategy successfully unlock MLLMs’ long reasoning capability. However,
ROUTCOME ’s accuracy is on par with or better than
RSTRUCTURED in about half of the classes, especially
in out-of-domain settings, suggesting that long reasoning alone does not guarantee improved accuracy
for MLLMs (Shao et al., 2025).

5.4

An in-domain/out-of-domain trade-off

To evaluate generalization, we measure performance on both in-domain (seen) and out-of-domain
(unseen) classes. As shown in Table 4, training on
a single-category dataset (Clock-3k) results in limited generalization: although RFORMAT achieves a
large in-domain gain on the trained category (3
→ 37 on Clock), its out-of-domain accuracy drops
well below the no-RL baseline (10.75 → 4.25), and
3/6 variants in total fall below the baseline on OOD.
In contrast, the Diverse-8k dataset substantially improves performance across nearly all classes, outperforming the baselines in both in-domain (ID)
and out-of-domain (OOD) settings, with 5/6 variants improving OOD relative to baseline. The pattern across both datasets reveals a trade-off: variants that achieve the largest in-domain gains tend to
generalize less well, while variants with weaker accuracy signals (RPROCESS , RGRADED ) show stronger
or more balanced OOD performance. This is consistent with overfitting to the training distribution
during RL training, an effect mitigated but not eliminated by training on a diverse set of categories.
Notably, the model demonstrates emergent reasoning capabilities across both in-domain and
out-of-domain categories (Figures 14 and 15).

the best-performing reward function for each category with the model trained on the Diverse-8K

Reinforcement Learning has recently emerged
as a way to improve reasoning in MLLMs. This
is primarily motivated by the advances in process supervision and verifiable reward modeling
from text-only systems (Lightman et al., 2023;
DeepSeek-AI, 2025). In the visual setting, these
ideas are adapted by using reward signals that verify whether intermediate reasoning steps are consistent with the image. For instance, vision-language
process reward models (Wang et al., 2025; Ong
et al., 2025) provide feedback on reasoning steps
by checking them against visual evidence. In parallel, some studies explore multimodal extensions of
R1-style training to elicit longer reasoning trajectories for diagrams, mathematical problems, and
synthetic visual tasks (Yang et al., 2025b; Shen
et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025). Complementary
approaches such as GRIT (Fan et al., 2025b) and
DeepEyes (Zheng et al., 2025c) incorporate explicit
visual grounding, encouraging models to reference
image regions during multi-step explanations.
However, these approaches rely on intermediate
supervision such as executable tool outputs, bounding boxes, or annotated reasoning traces. An open
question thus remains as to whether structured reasoning can be induced through reward design. We
take a step toward addressing this by designing reward functions and systematically evaluating their
impact on encouraging long, structured visual reasoning without requiring additional supervision.

Related Work

Research on the visual reasoning abilities of
MLLMs has grown rapidly (Chen et al., 2024; Li
et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2024), with benchmarks
spanning abstract visual puzzles (Song et al., 2025),
diagrammatic and symbolic reasoning (Li et al.,
2025a), and algorithmic visual reasoning (Ghosal
et al., 2025). Across these settings, MLLMs consistently struggle to uncover the visual relations necessary for correct inference. While CoT prompting elicits reasoning effectively in text-only domains (Wei et al., 2022; Kojima et al., 2022),
its visual extensions often produce interpretations
weakly grounded in the image (Dong et al., 2025;
Zhang et al., 2024). Because existing studies evaluate on prediction accuracy alone, they offer limited
insight into whether errors stem from visual misinterpretation or faulty algorithmic reasoning (Ghosal

Conclusion

Our results suggest a pressing need to decouple
perception and reasoning in post-training for multimodal models. Today’s conventional recipes,
largely adapted from text, apply reward at the level
of the answer or the reasoning chain and leave the
perceptual stage to improve as a side effect. We
argue this is not sufficient: long, structured reasoning can faithfully propagate whatever the model
thought it saw. The six reward variants we study begin to establish how supervision can be distributed
across these stages and isolate which signals shape
which behaviors. We see them as a first step toward a broader program in which perception and
reasoning are trained, rewarded, and evaluated as
the distinct competencies they are.

Limitations
Our primary objective is to investigate whether
reward-based RL fine-tuning can encourage models to perform long, multi-step visual reasoning
required to solve complex puzzles. While alternative approaches such as SFT could potentially
boost performance on a specific dataset, they cannot guide a model to exhibit step-by-step reasoning
behavior. Another possibility could have been SFT
with explicit reasoning traces. However, for visual
puzzle datasets, collecting high-quality chain-ofthought annotations is extremely challenging and
costly, mainly because the reasoning is often multihop, brittle, and domain-specific. For these reasons,
we focus on reward-driven RL fine-tuning, where
the aim was not merely to fit the dataset but to
induce emergent long-horizon reasoning behavior,
which we believe is better aligned with the nature
of visual puzzle solving.
Another concern is that the textual representations of images are highly structured and likely
overstate the perception gap. However, the purpose
of this experiment is to establish an upper bound
that quantifies the maximum performance gap attributable to visual perception. The text representations are kept minimal (e.g., “G-RR” for checker
states, simple grid characters); they encode the
same spatial information present in the image without introducing any reasoning shortcuts. Moreover,
there is no perfect middle ground between an image
and structured text for isolating the visual perception bottleneck from reasoning capability. Possible
alternatives, such as noisy or partially corrupted
text descriptions or caption-based representations
generated by a vision model, each introduce their
own biases and assumptions, making it difficult
to draw clean conclusions. Our approach instead
provides a clear and interpretable upper bound that
cleanly separates the perception bottleneck from
reasoning capability.

A

Additional Result Analysis

We perform additional analysis of how reward
design affects performance. Tables 3 and 4 surface several patterns worth examining: ROUTCOME ,
which uses no reasoning signal, is often competitive with more complex rewards, and RPROCESS ,
which does not grade the answer, achieves the
strongest out-of-domain generalization. We account for these as follows.
• Why ROUTCOME is competitive. ROUTCOME
leaves the model free to reason however it
wants, which suffices for some tasks and
yields competitive accuracy. The difference is
qualitative rather than quantitative: ROUTCOME
does not produce the extended reasoning that
RSTRUCTURED does (Figure 4). Accuracy is
close, but reasoning quality differs substantially, and that quality is what we aim to improve.
• Why RPROCESS generalizes in out-ofdomain. A strong accuracy signal pushes
the RL-trained model toward task-specific
answers, raising in-domain accuracy at the
cost of transfer. RPROCESS sidesteps this by
design: it rewards only general reasoning
behavior (describing the image, thinking,
rethinking), which carries over to unseen
tasks.
• Why some rewards show large ID–OOD
gaps. Rewards with high accuracy weight
(αa = 0.80–0.90) reinforce task-specific
patterns and widen the gap.
Rewards
that spread weight across reasoning components (RPROCESS ) or relax accuracy matching
(RGRADED ) reward more transferable skills,
producing more balanced performance

FUNCTION HourScore(pred_hour, true_hour):
hour_diff = circular distance on 12-hour clock
IF hour_diff > 2:
RETURN 0.0
ELSE:
RETURN 1 - (hour_diff / 5)
FUNCTION MinuteScore(pred_min, true_min):
minute_diff = circular distance on 60-minute clock
IF minute_diff > 10:
RETURN 0.0
ELSE:
RETURN 1 - (minute_diff / 20)
FUNCTION ClockReward(pred_time, true_time):
CONVERT pred_time to (pred_hour, pred_minute)
CONVERT true_time to (true_hour, true_minute)
hour_score = HourScore(pred_hour, true_hour)
minute_score = MinuteScore(pred_minute, true_minute)
RETURN 0.5 * hour_score + 0.5 * minute_score
