Driven by recent advances in artificial intelligence, a growing literature has demonstrated the potential of
using large language models (LLMs) as scalable surrogates to generate human-like responses. Two common
approaches to improve the performance of LLMs include: fine-tuning, which aligns the LLM more closely
with human responses, and rectification, which corrects biases in LLM outputs. In this paper, we develop
a two-stage framework that combines fine-tuning and rectification, and optimally allocates limited labeled
samples across the two stages. A key insight is that the conventional fine-tuning objective of minimizing
mean squared prediction error is generally not aligned with the downstream rectification stage. For mean
estimation, we propose to minimize the variance of the prediction errors; for general M -estimation, we
propose to minimize a scalarized variance metric as the fine-tuning objective. Building on this insight, we
leverage the scaling law of fine-tuning to optimally allocate the limited labeled human data between the
fine-tuning and rectification stages. Our empirical analysis validates the fine-tuning scaling law and confirms
that our proposed optimal allocation rule reliably identifies the optimal sample allocation. We demonstrate
substantial efficiency gains in estimation and inference performance relative to fine-tuning or rectification
alone, or to employing the conventional mean squared error objective within the fine-tuning then rectification
framework. Such efficiency gains translate to significant cost savings for making reliable decisions.
Key words : Large Language Models, Fine-Tuning, Prediction-Powered Inference

1.

Introduction

Businesses routinely conduct surveys to inform managerial decisions, such as conjoint analysis
(Green and Srinivasan 1990), new product development (Wind and Mahajan 1997), and pricing
(Wertenbroch and Skiera 2002). Traditional survey methods are often recognized to be costly,
time-consuming, and difficult to scale.
The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly in Large Language Models
(LLMs), has offered a potential solution to this bottleneck: the use of LLMs as human surrogates.
Rather than solely relying on recruiting thousands of human participants, businesses can use LLMs
to simulate human-like responses, such as evaluating products and expressing preferences, with
minimal latency and near-zero marginal cost. This solution has proven successful well beyond

survey studies. It enables a wide range of business applications, including estimating demand curves
and price elasticities (Gui and Toubia 2023), modeling discrete choices for assortment optimization
(Wang et al. 2024), and content optimization (Ye et al. 2025).
There is growing empirical evidence supporting the use of LLMs as human surrogates. Trained
on vast corpora of human-generated text, LLMs have demonstrated the capacity to encode complex
human behavioral patterns and social norms. Recent studies confirm that these models can closely
approximate human responses in diverse market research and social science applications (Brand
et al. 2023, Li et al. 2024, Toubia et al. 2025, Peng et al. 2025). As a result, LLM-as-surrogate has
become an increasingly attractive tool for accelerating insight generation across different domains,
allowing managers to conduct “virtual” market tests and experiments with unprecedented speed
and scale. There are a growing number of commercial platforms, such as Synthetic Users, Delve
AI, and Quantilope, which offer tools that leverage human-like AI participants to automate market
research.
To serve as effective surrogates, LLMs must be properly aligned with the target human population. However, off-the-shelf LLMs are often not suited for this task, as their pretraining optimizes
for next-token prediction rather than human behavioral alignment; furthermore, their reliance on
public corpora means they lack access to proprietary datasets, often leading to knowledge gaps
in specialized domains. Consequently, empirical studies document consistent and systematic discrepancies between LLM-generated outputs and survey responses (Motoki et al. 2024, Li et al.
2025). Furthermore, LLM predictions are also highly sensitive to prompt designs, where minor
syntactic changes can yield widely divergent outcomes (Brucks and Toubia 2023). The accuracy
of LLM responses could be improved through inference-time prompting techniques that do not
update model parameters, such as chain-of-thought prompting to elicit step-by-step reasoning (Wei
et al. 2022), in-context learning with several demonstrations to clarify the task (Brown et al.
2020), and persona prompting to simulate specific subpopulations (Peng et al. 2025). However,
their ability to consistently and accurately reflect human respondents remains questionable in the
literature (Pezeshkpour and Hruschka 2024, Ye et al. 2025, Krsteski et al. 2025). These limitations of inference-time prompting techniques highlight the need for methodological advances that
leverage LLMs as human surrogates more effectively. Broadly speaking, existing approaches can
be characterized into two methods: fine-tuning, and rectification.
Fine-tuning aligns an LLM by explicitly updating its underlying parameters during a posttraining phase using labeled samples. This method has proven successful in bridging the gap
between model outputs and human responses across various business applications (Ye et al. 2025,
Cheng et al. 2025). Its adoption has been further accelerated by parameter-efficient fine-tuning
(PEFT) methods, such as LoRA (Hu et al. 2022), which trains only small low-rank adapters and

thus is computationally more efficient. Recent studies confirm that PEFT achieves performance
comparable to full fine-tuning at a fraction of the computational cost (Han et al. 2024). However,
fine-tuning relies heavily on the availability of high-quality, task-specific labeled samples. Empirical
evidence indicates that fine-tuning performance generally follows a power-law scaling relationship
(Kaplan et al. 2020, Hernandez et al. 2021, Hoffmann et al. 2022, Zhang et al. 2024). This implies
diminishing marginal returns: the value of each additional labeled sample decreases as the total size
of labeled samples grows. Moreover, fine-tuning alone does not provide statistical guarantees on
the magnitude of errors that still remain in the model, making it difficult to quantify the reliability
of the aligned model. From a business perspective, the absence of guarantees makes it difficult to
assess whether errors are small enough to support an important decision.
In contrast to fine-tuning, the rectification method does not update the underlying parameters
of the pretrained LLM. Instead, the rectification method treats the LLM as a black box, uses
labeled samples to quantify the errors of the LLM, and rectifies the outputs from the LLM using
the quantified errors post hoc. One of the most prominent examples of the rectification method
is prediction-powered inference (PPI), which combines LLM predictions with labeled samples to
construct unbiased estimators and valid confidence intervals (Angelopoulos et al. 2023a). This focus
on bias correction and inference reliability is particularly valuable for business decisions, where
even small systematic errors can be costly. Similar to fine-tuning, one primary requirement for PPI
is also the availability of high-quality labeled samples. Because the variance of the PPI estimator
scales inversely with the sample size, this approach also exhibits diminishing marginal returns: the
value of each additional labeled sample decreases as the total size of labeled samples grows.
Since both fine-tuning and rectification are subject to diminishing marginal returns, relying
exclusively on either method could be inefficient. This motivates us to combine both fine-tuning
and rectification methods to take advantage of the rapid initial gains of each method, efficiently
allocating our limited labeled data among two methods.
1.1.

A Fine-Tuning then Rectification Framework

In this paper, we propose a new fine-tuning then rectification framework designed to combine both
methods. At a high level, our framework partitions the labeled samples into two disjoint subsets:
the first is used to fine-tune the LLM, and the second is used in the subsequent rectification stage.
We analyze how the variance of the final estimator depends on the quality of the fine-tuned LLM.
This analysis motivates us to use a new metric, which we refer to as the “residual variance,” as the
fine-tuning objective function. Theoretically, we show that this objective function is more suitable
than the traditional mean squared error (MSE) objective function for the downstream rectification
stage. Empirically, we demonstrate the advantage of using the residual variance objective over

the MSE objective. Our usage of the residual variance is conceptually similar to the predict-thenoptimize literature (Elmachtoub and Grigas 2022, Ferreira et al. 2016). This paper can be viewed
as proposing a surrogate objective function to replace the traditional MSE objective function. Our
usage of the residual variance is also conceptually similar to the empirical efficiency maximization
literature (Rubin and van der Laan 2008, van der Laan and Van Der Laan 2026). This paper can
be viewed as a model fine-tuning version of empirical efficiency maximization for PPI.
Building on this insight, we propose a data-driven procedure that leverages empirical scaling laws
to determine the optimal sample allocation between fine-tuning and rectification. The literature has
extensively studied the scaling law, showing that LLM performance improves in smooth, predictable
ways as model size and sample size increase, often following simple power-law patterns in both
pretraining and fine-tuning stages (Kaplan et al. 2020, Hernandez et al. 2021, Hoffmann et al.
2022, Zhang et al. 2024). In our setting, the scaling law describes how the quality of the fine-tuned
LLM improves with additional labeled data, which enables us to identify the optimal allocation
of limited labeled samples between fine-tuning and rectification that minimizes the variance of
the final estimator. Furthermore, we characterize the properties of the optimal allocation rule and
analyze the magnitude of variance reduction under our proposed framework.
In the sample allocation procedure described above, each sample is used either for fine-tuning or
for rectification, but not both. Our framework can be naturally combined with cross-fitting type
sample splitting, so that, across different splits, each sample contributes to both fine-tuning and
rectification. This idea has been studied by Zrnic and Candès (2024) in the context of PPI, and has
long been recognized in the cross-validation literature as a way to improve upon a single train-test
split (Bates et al. 2024, Geisser 1975, Stone 1974). In Online Appendix EC.2.2, we illustrate how to
naturally combine our framework with cross-fitting type sample splitting. Importantly, cross-fitting
does not eliminate the need to choose a new fine-tuning objective; see Online Appendix EC.3.2.2.
We validate our framework through numerical experiments using the Wine Reviews dataset to
estimate (i) the average ratings in the population and (ii) the price-rating regression coefficient.
These tasks mirror the day-to-day operations decisions faced by store managers in brick-and-mortar
retailing. When store managers select which stock-keeping units (SKUs) to sell in their stores, they
usually need to understand the perceived product quality of each SKU. We show how to formulate
this problem as a mean estimation problem, which we consider in Sections 2 and 3. Additionally,
when the store managers make pricing decisions, they usually need to understand how perceived
quality changes with price. We show how to formulate this problem as a linear regression problem
through the more general view of M -estimation, which we consider in Section 4. In both cases, we
show that our framework provides an effective way to understand the perceived quality at scale
without incurring huge costs or delays associated with extensive human labeling.

Our empirical analysis shows four main findings. First, we empirically confirm a scaling law in
LLM fine-tuning with our proposed variance-based training objective. This empirical scaling law
predicts the marginal value of one additional labeled sample, which we then use to guide the optimal
sample allocation. Second, we validate our optimal sample allocation. We show that the theoretical
optimum derived from the scaling law accurately guides the optimal sample allocation. Third, we
show the empirical advantage of our proposed framework over a number of existing benchmarks:
the sample mean estimator using only the human labeled dataset, standard fine-tuning, standard
PPI, a conceptually similar prompting then rectification framework that replaces fine-tuning with
prompting, and fine-tuning then rectification but under the standard mean squared error objective.
Our proposed framework outperforms the above benchmarks in most cases. The only exception is
the prompting then rectification framework, which performs better when the sample size is very
small. Beyond mean estimation, when estimating the price-rating regression coefficient, our finetuning then rectification framework significantly outperforms the benchmark of linear regression
using only the human labeled dataset.
These performance gains translate into substantial sample savings: our method reduces the
labeling budget by 48% to 54% compared to the sample mean estimator, to achieve the same level
of accuracy. Finally, we show that the choice of fine-tuning objective is important for the fine-tuning
then rectification framework. Replacing the MSE fine-tuning objective with our proposed variancebased objective brings 22% to 42% variance reduction across different data budget settings.
1.2.

Other Related Literature

In addition to the works mentioned above, there are three lines of other related works.
LLM Fine-Tuning. Fine-tuning has become a standard approach to adapt pretrained language
models to downstream tasks by updating model parameters using labeled samples (Brown et al.
2020, Ouyang et al. 2022). For large models, parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods, such as LoRA
(Hu et al. 2022) introduce task-specific low-rank matrix adapters while freezing most parameters,
making fine-tuning computationally efficient in practice. A large empirical literature on neural
scaling laws shows that model performance tends to follow smooth power-law relationships with
model size, data size, and compute scale (Kaplan et al. 2020, Hernandez et al. 2021, Hoffmann
et al. 2022, Zhang et al. 2024), providing us with a parametric function to use the size of labeled
samples to predict the model quality.
Other fine-tuning methods, such as RLHF and DPO, update models using a separate trained
reward model or preference data (Ouyang et al. 2022, Rafailov et al. 2023). Beyond vanilla finetuning with commonly used objective functions such as mean squared errors and cross-entropy
losses, recent works have started to modify objectives to target downstream tasks directly. For

6

Ye, Zhao, and Wang: Fine-Tune, Then Rectify

example, Vafa et al. (2025) propose a debiased fine-tuning method for using foundation models
to estimate the gender wage gap. In our proposed framework, we focus on the setting where an
LLM is used as a surrogate for a PPI estimator and design a fine-tuning objective that directly
minimizes the variance governing the efficiency of the final PPI estimator.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to formally analyze the connection between
fine-tuning and rectification. While recent independent work by Krsteski et al. (2025) empirically
observes that combining these methods yields improvements, their approach relies on standard finetuning procedures that do not take into account the downstream rectification task. In contrast, our
framework derives a fine-tuning objective explicitly tailored to the rectification stage and provides
a rigorous method for optimally allocating labeled samples between the two stages.
Prediction-Powered Inference. The PPI method combines a small set of labeled samples
with a large set of model predictions to construct unbiased estimators and confidence intervals,
while treating the prediction model as a potentially biased black box (Angelopoulos et al. 2023a).
Follow-up works, such as the PPI++ method from Angelopoulos et al. (2023b), develop more efficient variants. Recent research further connects PPI to the classical surrogate outcome literature,
showing that PPI-type estimators can be interpreted as using predictions as surrogates and proposing re-calibration procedures that achieve improved efficiency (Ji et al. 2025). Our work builds on
this line of research by taking the prediction model to be an LLM that can itself be improved via
fine-tuning, and by explicitly designing both the fine-tuning objective and the allocation of labeled
data to minimize the asymptotic variance of the PPI estimator.
In an earlier work, Zrnic and Candès (2024) propose a method called the cross-PPI, which allows
every label to serve both model training and rectification through cross-fitting. The cross-PPI
approach and our fine-tuning then rectification approach are not mutually exclusive: our approach
can itself be combined with cross-fitting. Online Appendix EC.2.2 describes the details of combining
our approach with cross-fitting, and Online Appendix EC.3.2.2 reports the results from numerical
experiments. Importantly, cross-fitting does not eliminate the need to choose a new fine-tuning
objective. Online Appendix EC.3.2.2 shows that the variance-based objective consistently improves
conventional MSE objective when our approach is combined with cross-fitting.
PPI is only one rectification method. There are other rectification methods. For example, Wang
et al. (2024) propose a transfer learning based method to correct the bias from LLMs. Han et al.
(2026) consider a penalized regression estimator that shrinks an estimator generated from human
data toward the LLM simulator. In this paper, we focus on the standard PPI method for clarity,
though our core insights extend to other rectification approaches.

LLMs for Business Applications. There is a fast growing literature that evaluates whether
LLMs can serve as surrogates for human respondents in high-fidelity business applications, ranging
from demand estimation to consumer choice modeling. Brand et al. (2023) examine the use of
LLMs to estimate willingness to pay. They document mixed results: LLMs can reproduce aggregate patterns reasonably well, but they generate insufficient heterogeneity across individuals even
after fine-tuning. And the fine-tuned models struggle to extrapolate beyond the training distribution. Other work studies whether LLMs can reproduce human preferences or attitudes in specific
domains, such as brand perception (Li et al. 2024), computational social science (Argyle et al. 2023,
Ziems et al. 2024), and content experiments (Ye et al. 2025), all highlighting their potential to
augment, rather than replace, human data. At the same time, several recent papers also document
important limitations. Gui and Toubia (2023) raise endogeneity concerns when using LLMs to
simulate human behavior from a causal-inference perspective. Wang et al. (2024) show that while
LLM-generated responses are useful for data augmentation in choice modeling, they often exhibit
systematic biases relative to human samples and require careful calibration.
Our contribution is methodological and complements this literature. Rather than assessing LLMs
as stand-alone survey respondents, we study how to combine fine-tuning and rectification when
LLM outputs are used as surrogate responses. We further show how to optimally allocate scarce
human-labeled data between improving the surrogate model and correcting its residual biases to
obtain accurate estimates and make reliable decisions.
Roadmap

define the combined fine-tuning and rectification estimator. In Section 3, we derive the main theoretical results of our framework. We analyze the estimator’s variance to motivate a variance-based

baselines. In Section 4, we extend this framework from mean estimation to the more general M estimation problems. In Section 5, we conduct empirical analysis for mean estimation on the Wine
Reviews dataset. Section 6 studies the price-rating regression coefficient as an M -estimation case
study. In Section 7, we conclude the paper.

Example 1 motivates the use of variance Var(Y − f (s) (X)), rather than the traditional mean
squared error (MSE), as the loss function for fine-tuning. We refer to this variance Var(Y − f (s) (X))
as the “residual variance.” The residual variance is different from Var(f (s) (X)), the variance of the
fine-tuned LLM.
Remark 1. For mean estimation, the MSE and residual variance objectives are equivalent under
some conditions. When the model class allows for additive constant shifts, such as through an
unconstrained intercept, minimizing MSE implicitly also minimizes the residual variance. In the
setting with an unconstrained intercept, the two objectives essentially lead to the same solution.
However, the two objectives can behave differently in more practical settings where data is not
large enough or when the model is trained with mini batches, under regularization, or under early
stopping. In Section 5.4, we empirically compare the two objectives and demonstrate the benefits
of using the residual variance as the objective.
For general M -estimation, however, the objectives are generally not equivalent. The residual
that governs downstream precision is weighted by each observation’s influence on the estimand
(Section 4); when that weight varies across observations, an additive shift of the surrogate changes
the weighted residual by a nonconstant amount, so a free intercept no longer aligns MSE with
the estimand-aware criterion. Standard fine-tuning typically minimizes cross-entropy in next-token
prediction, which equivalently maximizes the likelihood of predicting the next token. Such a standard fine-tuning objective is not tied to the downstream rectification stage at all. Sometimes during
fine-tuning, numerical outcomes are also commonly handled by a discrete classification head, which
we discuss in Online Appendix EC.3.1.3. Section 4 derives the general estimand-aware objective,
shows that minimizing it weakly dominates any alternative fine-tuning objective, and exhibits a
strict gap in an example; Section 6 provides an empirical boundary case in which the two objectives
nearly coincide and a controlled setting in which they do not.

samples n grow, the samples allocated to fine-tuning naturally grow with it.

establishes a theoretical baseline: in the case where there is no irreducible variance, i.e., b = 0, the
optimal fraction is a perfect constant, α/(α + 1), which depends solely on the power-law exponent.
This value serves as the upper bound for the allocation ratio. In the more common case where b > 0,
property (v) reveals that the ratio s∗ /n deviates from this baseline. It is strictly decreasing in both
b and n. Theoretically, this fraction vanishes asymptotically as the dataset grows indefinitely, i.e.,
limn→∞ s∗ /n = 0. This implies that given an infinite budget of labeled data, the optimal fine-tuning
set size s∗ would grow indefinitely, yet the fraction s∗ /n will asymptotically vanish.
For practical purposes, Equation (5) provides an important bound on this behavior. It establishes
that the rate of this decay is uniformly bounded by a term proportional to b/a. This implies that
if b/a is small, the optimal ratio remains close to the theoretical ceiling α/(α + 1) if the dataset
size n is not too large.

Empirical Setup

Textual product reviews are a common source of information for assessing perceived quality in
many markets. Such reviews encode rich but noisy signals about latent product attributes that are
difficult to measure directly. A central challenge for firms is to aggregate these textual evaluations
into reliable quality measures.
This challenge is particularly important in settings where high-quality evaluations are costly. In
such settings, each additional labeled observation is expensive, and improving statistical efficiency
directly reduces the number of human evaluations needed to achieve a target level of precision.
As a result, variance reduction is not only the technical goal; it has direct implications for how
quickly and reliably firms can form aggregate assessments of product quality under fixed evaluation
budgets.
Dataset and Estimand. We study this problem using the Wine Reviews dataset, consisting of
wine reviews written by professional wine critics at Wine Enthusiast magazine and publicly available on Kaggle (Thoutt 2017). This dataset has also been used in prior work on prediction-powered
inference and surrogate outcomes (Ji et al. 2025).
The dataset comprises 129,971 wine reviews, each containing a free-form natural language
description of sensory characteristics, such as aroma, flavor, balance, and finish, alongside structured metadata including price. After deleting duplicated reviews and records without a valid price
field, we have 90,610 unique reviews for our empirical analysis. The outcome variable Y is an overall
rating given by expert reviewers, ranging from 80 to 100 points (as the magazine only publishes
reviews scoring 80 or above). This metric reflects a standardized professional rubric widely adopted
in the industry. Each observation’s input X has two components: the free-form review text and
the wine’s retail price. Two representative examples are provided in Table 1.
To get the population mean of ratings, we randomly partition the dataset into two disjoint sets:
one with 80,610 unique reviews as the target population, another with 10,000 reviews to estimate
the fine-tuning scaling law in Section 5.2. The population mean rating over the target population

latter reduces the slope-specific variance criterion by approximately 31%. This gap persists with
a free intercept and centered targets, so it cannot be removed by an additive-level adjustment
(Online Appendix EC.4.2).

Conclusions

In this paper, we propose a fine-tuning then rectification framework for leveraging LLMs as human
surrogates under limited human labeled data. We show that the statistical efficiency of the downstream rectification estimator is governed primarily by the residual variance of the LLM surrogates.
Consequently, we propose a variance-based fine-tuning objective that outperforms the standard
fine-tuning objective of mean-squared error. Furthermore, we leverage empirical scaling laws to
derive a data-driven procedure for optimally allocating scarce labeled samples between fine-tuning
and rectification. Empirical results confirm that our fine-tuning then rectification framework outperforms using either fine-tuning or rectification alone, as well as using the standard fine-tuning
objective of MSE. Empirical results also confirm that our theoretically derived allocation closely
matches the empirical optimum. Beyond mean estimation, the same framework applied to the
price-rating regression coefficient delivers confidence intervals narrower than OLS.
We view this work as an initial step toward a systematic understanding of how to jointly optimize
LLM model improvement and statistical correction. We discuss a ramp-up procedure for estimating
the scaling-law parameters and a procedure to handle independent but non-identical fine-tuning
data in Online Appendices EC.2.3 and EC.5, respectively. These procedures are sometimes effective in practice and offer fertile grounds for future research. For instance, we currently model
the non-identical setting simply through changes in scaling law parameters, yet more theoretical
and empirical analysis of such transfer-learning dynamics remains to be explored. Similarly, the
optimality of the data-driven ramp-up strategy could be further investigated. As LLMs become
increasingly integrated into market research, developing such rigorous methodologies for combining
residual variance reduction (via fine-tuning) with bias correction (via rectification) will be essential
for conducting market research at scale.

Practical Implementation and Robustness of the Allocation
Rule

This section examines the practical implementation of the allocation rule when the scaling-law
parameters are unknown. We first assess the stability of the fitted response curve and the allocation
it implies, and then discuss how to combine the optimal sample allocation with cross-fitting type
sample splitting, and finally describe a data-driven ramp-up procedure for estimating the curve
from limited pilot data.
EC.2.1.

Scaling-Law Robustness and Allocation Stability

This subsection evaluates the stability of the scaling-law-based allocation rule through two complementary analyses. We first quantify sensitivity to data sampling and training randomness using a
hierarchical bootstrap, and then examine a more data-limited setting in which the response curve
is fitted from only a small number of early development sizes. At the smallest deployment budget,
n = 1,000, the allocation rule selects s∗ = 141, slightly below the 250-label floor of the measured
development grid; the corresponding criterion value therefore relies on a short extrapolation of the
fitted scaling law rather than interpolation. At all larger budgets, the rule-selected allocations lie
within the measured range.

EC.2.1.2.

One-Shot Fits from Early Sizes To assess whether a few small training sizes

suffice to guide allocation, we fit the scaling law using a single training run at each of the four

for each of the ten runs. For each run, we fit a reference scaling law to the other nine runs and use
it to evaluate the selected allocation.
We compare the predicted confidence interval width at the selected allocation with the minimum
width predicted by the same reference law. The selected allocation ratios vary substantially: at
n = 1,000, they range from 10% to 28%. However, the corresponding increases in predicted interval
width are modest. For each budget, we compute the median percentage increase across the ten
runs; these medians range from 0.3% to 5.3% across the tested budgets, and the largest increase
across all runs and budgets is 10.4%.
Extending the fitting grid to six sizes up to 2,000 reduces the median increase to below 3% at
every budget, with a maximum of 7.9%. Thus, even when the estimated allocation differs from the
reference optimum, inference precision changes relatively little because the interval-width objective
is flat near its minimum.

Data-Driven Ramp-Up Procedure

In this subsection, we propose a data-driven ramp-up procedure that sequentially estimates the
scaling law and determines when to stop allocating additional labeled observations to surrogate
development.
The procedure reserves a fixed validation subset of size nv for estimating the response curve.
This reserve is distinct from any internal validation split used for checkpoint selection within a
development run. Consequently, after reserving these observations, the effective labeled budget
available for surrogate development and PPI rectification is
neff = n − nv .

rectification sample has size neff − s. The relevant allocation objective is therefore

Supplementary Analyses for Mean Estimation

This section provides supplementary evidence for the mean-estimation analysis in Section 5. We
examine four dimensions: diagnostics and boundary conditions for the training objective, alternative training and inference designs, the choice and quality of the surrogate model, and additional
mean-estimation applications beyond the main Wine Reviews corpus.
EC.3.1.

Training-Objective Diagnostics and Boundary Conditions

This subsection examines when and why variance-loss training differs from conventional MSE
training, and how the comparison changes under alternative training specifications. We consider
three analyses: a residual-variance diagnostic on the validation set, a sensitivity analysis of outcome
centering, and a comparison with cross-entropy training.
EC.3.1.1.

Residual-Variance Comparison We analyze the behavior of the fine-tuned LLM

on the validation set to explain the performance differences observed in the main analysis. As
established in Proposition 2, the efficiency of the PPI estimator is primarily governed by the
magnitude of the residual variance Var(Y − f (s) (X)). For a fixed allocation s and a sufficiently
large unlabeled sample, a lower residual variance directly reduces the leading variance term of the
PPI estimator.

EC.3.3.

Surrogate Model Choice and Quality

This subsection studies how downstream efficiency and the preferred deployment change with
three features of the surrogate pipeline: the text representation used for supervised adaptation,
the quality of the census-wide surrogate available before adaptation, and the complexity of the
fine-tuning class.
EC.3.3.1.

Classical Text Baseline: TF-IDF We first assess whether the gains from the

main LLM-embedding pipeline persist relative to a classical sparse-text representation. We replace
the pretrained embedding with a 20,000-feature TF-IDF representation with bigrams and fit ridge
regression as the prediction model. At labeled-data budgets of n = 1,000 and n = 10,000, the
LLM-embedding pipeline attains 43% and 26% lower estimator variance, respectively, than the
TF-IDF baseline. Thus, in this application, the LLM-embedding pipeline remains substantially
more statistically efficient than a conventional sparse-text alternative.
EC.3.3.2.

Controlled Synthetic Analysis of the Objective Gap

This subsection uses a controlled synthetic design to isolate the mechanism underlying the difference between estimand-aware and conventional training. The design creates a setting in which
a constrained surrogate must trade approximation accuracy between observations with different
influence on the target estimand. This generates a clear objective gap whose source can be examined directly. We then remove the features that generate this trade-off to verify the conditions
under which the two training objectives become equivalent.
Design. We construct a two-region data-generating process consisting of a large low-influence
bulk and a smaller high-influence cluster. The outcome is
Y = gL (Z) + κgH (Z) + δU + ε,
where
gL (Z) = tanh(1.5 − Z),

gH (Z) = tanh(Z − zhi + 1).

The first component primarily captures variation in the low-influence bulk, whereas the second primarily captures variation in the high-influence cluster. The auxiliary variable U is an independent
persona-like signal available to the surrogate, and ε is homoskedastic outcome noise.
The key restriction is imposed through the surrogate class. Rather than observing the two regionspecific components separately, the surrogate receives an entangled feature that combines them, so
that a common coefficient must trade approximation accuracy across the two regions. Conventional
MSE training weights this trade-off primarily according to the relative frequency of observations in
each region. In contrast, the estimand-aware objective assigns greater weight to observations that
contribute more strongly to the reported slope. The estimand is the census regression slope, and

Objective gap and mechanism. Across 100 paired training trials, estimand-aware training attains
a slope-specific variance criterion approximately 31% lower than conventional MSE training. The
conventional surrogate includes a free, unpenalized intercept, and centering the training outcomes
leaves the gap essentially unchanged, at 31.8% with a paired t-statistic of 60. The separation
therefore does not arise from the additive-level effect that can distinguish the two objectives in
finite-sample mean-estimation training. The corresponding downstream slope-estimation results
are reported in Table 5 of the main text.
The mechanism instead comes from how the two objectives allocate approximation error across
the two regions. Conventional MSE training places greater emphasis on the low-influence bulk
because it contains most observations. Estimand-aware training accepts more residual error in that
region in exchange for substantially reducing error in the smaller high-influence cluster, approximately halving the cluster-level bias in the fitted design. Because residual errors from high-influence
observations contribute more strongly to the variance of the reported slope, this reallocation reduces
the downstream variance criterion even when it need not improve conventional prediction accuracy
by the same amount.
Removing the preconditions. We consider two variants that separately remove the features
responsible for the objective gap. First, we give the surrogate separate access to the two regionspecific components rather than only their entangled representation. This removes the forced
approximation trade-off because the surrogate can fit the low- and high-influence regions independently. Second, we remove the high-influence cluster (drawing Z uniformly on [−1, 1]) while
retaining the restricted surrogate representation. The entangled feature then no longer has to serve
two regions, so the surrogate class becomes nearly correctly specified; the influence weights remain
heterogeneous in this variant.
In both variants, the objective gap becomes essentially zero. Thus, the separation between
estimand-aware and conventional training requires both heterogeneous influence on the downstream estimand and a misspecified surrogate class that cannot simultaneously fit the relevant
regions. When the misspecification is removed, as in both variants, the two objectives yield nearly
identical solutions; when the influence is constant, the mean-estimation equivalence of Remark 1
applies. This boundary regime is consistent with the near-equivalence observed for the real-corpus
price-rating case study in Section 6.

In many real applications, however, practitioners also have access to additional labeled data that
are independent but not necessarily identically distributed with the target task. For example, in a
survey study, it is common to have a large archive of previous questionnaires and responses across
products, features, and customer segments, and then to field a new survey on a specific question

questionnaire; see, e.g., Brand et al. (2023). This is an “independent but non-identical” setting:
the prior surveys are not drawn from the same distribution as the new question, but they provide
useful prior information about how to map text responses to numerical outcomes.
In the following, we show that our framework naturally accommodates such extra fine-tuning
data, and that the optimal sample allocation on the new task remains valid under mild independence assumptions.
