# Correctness-congruence gap

Checking generated code can separate two things. Correctness is whether the artefact follows the formal rules of its language: the provider schema, the resource syntax, the required fields. Congruence is whether the artefact does what the person asking meant. Nekrasov et al. measure both on Terraform generated by GPT-4o. Technical validation asks whether the code is valid. Intent validation "compares the dependency graph of the generated IaC against the user-intended infrastructure specification".[^c1]

Knowledge injection moves the first check a long way. The paper reports "technical validation rates increasing from 37.2% to 70.2% for Naive RAG", and its best Graph RAG variant, GR-OptMatch, "increased the technical success rate to 84.2%".[^c2][^c3] The second check does not follow. Once technical validation is achieved, "intent validation rates remain relatively stable within a narrower band (72.8% to 76.3%) once technical" validation is passed.[^c4] The authors report that the intent validation rate "plateaued at 72-76% across all RAG-based methods".[^c5] Their summary is that "intent alignment represents a distinct challenge that is less responsive to the structural and retrieval enhancements that dramatically improve technical correctness."[^c6]

The authors name the two sides. Correctness is "a closed-world problem, verifiable" against a formal specification, which for them is modelled as a knowledge graph.[^c7] Congruence is different in kind: "This is an open-world problem requiring architectural reasoning".[^c8] Their reading is that the methods were built for the first side: "our methods are optimized to solve for Correctness", and what remains failing is "entirely problems of Congruence."[^c9][^c10]

The mechanism, as far as the paper shows it, is one of coverage. Retrieval from a schema or dependency graph supplies the rules a generator needs to be valid. The unstated goal of the requester, such as which resources must exist together for the service to serve its purpose, is not a rule in that graph. My inference, not the paper's measurement: enlarging the retrieved material extends the first kind of knowledge and leaves the second untouched, which fits the flat intent band across methods.

The check itself also carries the gap. Intent validation is binary, and the authors note that "A generated configuration that is 90% aligned with user intent is currently treated the same" as one at 10%.[^c11] A pass rate on such a check cannot show whether congruence rose inside the failing cases. They propose graded measures, and say that "Bridging this gap may require new approaches that explicitly model design patterns and constraints".[^c12]

## Limits

The gap is a single-study observation. The paper's external-validity section says "The research focuses exclusively on a single large language model (GPT-4o)", and the scope is "constrained to Terraform configurations for AWS infrastructure".[^c13][^c14] The benchmark is "458 human-curated scenarios".[^c15]

Intent validation "relies on human-authored specifications of what constitutes correct infrastructure."[^c16] The plateau is a plateau against those specifications; a different specification could move the band. The "congruence" label is the authors' qualitative reading of error categories. The paper does not test architectural reasoning, and it does not show that the gap is a fixed property of language models rather than of the methods tried.

Nothing here tests asking or answering in general. Whether the same split appears where the output is a clarifying question, a prose answer or a plan is untested. What transfers is the question the note raises: where a check against a formal standard stops short of the asker's goal, and whether a method that improves the check can be expected to improve the goal.
