# Willingness to return

> When there is no ground truth for the answer, the field asks the only party
> who knows whether the question was understood: would you bring your next
> question to this answerer?

## Definition / Mechanism

The measure is a single post-transaction item put to the asker — *would you[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s4][^s5]
return to this same person with another question?* — recorded as a proportion.
It entered as a deliberate replacement for accuracy. Hernon & McClure (1986)
had established the **55 percent rule** by unobtrusive testing in 26 US
libraries: staff answered about 55% of factual and bibliographic queries
correctly. Durrance (1989) asked whether that number tells the whole story,
examined the setting of the interview and the answerer's behaviour, and
associated the asker's likelihood of returning to the same individual with
competencies rather than with the answer alone.

| Measurement | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| MLIS students asking their own real question at a desk (n = 77) | 59.7% would return to the same librarian; willingness and satisfaction significantly related to the staff member's behaviour and answer quality | Dewdney & Ross (1994) |
| Library Visit Study, physical desks (261 visits, phases 1–2) | 65% | Nilsen (2004), Library Visit Study — key `ross-2004` |
| Virtual reference desks (42 reports, 2003) | 62% | Nilsen (2004), Library Visit Study — key `ross-2004` |
| What predicts it | askers are more willing to return to answerers they rate highly for interviewing skill, ability to determine need and search strategy, and to those who *opened* with an open question — though answerers open with a statement 37%, a closed question 31%, an open question 27% of the time | Durrance (1989), as reported by Radford et al. (2011) |

The Library Visit Study is a multi-phase project Ross and Nilsen ran together
over years, but the 2003 virtual-desk write-up that carries the two figures
above is Nilsen's alone; this note attributes them to her.

Filing them as one quantity is the error this row exists to block |

## Why it matters

1. **It is defined where accuracy is not.** Most questions have no verifiable
   correct answer; Radford et al. could score accuracy on only a small usable
   subset of their chat transactions. A comprehension measure that requires
   ground truth is unavailable for the majority of transactions, and this one
   is not.
2. **It prices the interview, not the answer.** What moves it is the
   answerer's behaviour — how the encounter opened, whether the need was
   determined — which makes it the only outcome measure in this literature
   that responds to stall 9 at all.
3. **It is a revealed judgement about the source, not about the answer.** The
   asker is stating whether it will spend its next question here; that is the
   asker's side of stall 4 fed back as the answerer's score.
4. **It is corruptible in a known direction.** Radford's earlier work found
   74% of users rated relational, interpersonal factors above content-related
   ones in assessing face-to-face reference. An answerer optimising return
   intention can buy it with warmth instead of comprehension — the same
   structure as [[sycophantic-capitulation]].

## Transfer

| | |
|---|---|
| **Home field** | evaluation of library reference service, where the alternative measure — unobtrusive accuracy testing with planted factual questions — is expensive and covers only answerable questions |
| **Here** | scoring any answerer on a transaction whose correctness cannot be checked: the asker's stated intention to bring the next question back |
| **What survives** | the availability argument (a measure defined on every transaction); the sensitivity to the answerer's comprehension behaviour; the explicit refusal to treat accuracy as the whole of success |
| **What does not** | the identification of the answerer as a person one can choose to return to — an asker with one available answerer cannot express the judgement; and the assumption that the asker can tell whether its need was met, which is exactly what [[levels-of-information-need]] denies for a $Q_4$ request |

## Limits

- Respondents in the studies with numbers are MLIS students acting as
  participant-observers with questions of their own, not naive users; they
  know they are evaluating reference service.
- It measures an intention, not a behaviour. No study read here follows up on
  whether the asker returned.
- It cannot separate a recovered need from a pleasant transaction; point 4 is
  the note's own warning, and no source read here tests whether return
  intention tracks need [[recovery-postulate|recovery]].
- The 55 percent rule is used here only as the measure Durrance argued
  against; Hernon & McClure's design is known here from its published record,
  not from the article, and later work disputes the figure.
- The two Library Visit Study figures were confirmed verbatim against the
  paper (261 visits, 42 reports, 65% and 62%), but the joint Ross & Nilsen
  byline this note first gave them could not be confirmed `[?]`: *Information
  Research* 9(2) paper 171 carries Nilsen as sole author. The key `ross-2004`
  is retained for continuity with the sibling notes of this cell.
- Durrance's percentages reach this note through Radford et al.'s review; the
  1989 article was not read in this pass. `radford-2011` itself is confirmed
  and its headline findings hold, but its full text could not be read this
  pass, so three granular items are unconfirmed against the article `[?]`:
  the accuracy-subset count behind *Why it matters* 1, the 37/31/27 opening
  split, and the review's restatement of Durrance's 74% relational-over-
  content finding.
