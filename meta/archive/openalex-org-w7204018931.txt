Abstract
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) mitigates hallucinations in Large Language Models using
externally retrieved data. Although useful, RAG can expose the retrieved data to extraction attacks.
Previous work has demonstrated the feasibility of RAG extraction, but has failed to simultaneously
ensure high-coverage retrieval of the entire knowledge database and verbatim extraction of lengthy
retrieved data.
In this paper, we propose MARAGE, an optimization-based RAG database extraction attack.
To obtain high-coverage retrieval, we propose a semantic-aware query selection strategy which optimizes the diversity of retrieved chunks from the database. We then employ an adversarial string
appended to each query to force verbatim output of the retrieved RAG data. We generate these
strings by leveraging a continuous optimization framework. We integrate three key innovations into
this framework: (1) To enhance cross-model transferability, we utilize multi-model optimization,
(2) To adapt to the uniquely lengthy target of RAG databases, we introduce Primacy weighting,
which prioritizes initial tokens in the optimization target, and (3) We use Perplexity-constrained
optimization for evading state-of-the-art defenses.
Evaluations show that MARAGE consistently outperforms both manual and optimization-based
baselines across multiple LLMs and RAG databases. On an end-to-end RAG pipeline, MARAGE
outperforms the manual attack baseline by 329 ± 165% in extracting RAG data. To shed light on
why MARAGE is more effective than the baselines, we probe and analyze the model’s internal state
to isolate the benefit of our approach.

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have shown impressive capabilities in natural language generation
and question-answering tasks. Despite their outstanding one-shot performance on simple natural
language tasks, they often hallucinate [3] on tasks that require domain-specific or up-to-date knowledge. Due to the massive amount of resources required to train these models, it is infeasible to
continuously re-train models to incorporate the emergence of new knowledge. Designed to address
these deficiencies, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [4, 5] enhances inference with data mining techniques that retrieve the most relevant data chunks from an external knowledge database [6].
These contents are used in-context to enhance LLM inference, mitigating hallucinations by providing
the LLM with specialized information, without the need for retraining. Due its benefits, RAG has
been adopted in various domains including healthcare(e.g., [7,8]), finance(e.g., [9]), science(e.g., [10]),
and law(e.g., [11]).
Apart from the in-context knowledge that RAG provides, it also offers an added layer of privacy
protection. Previous work has shown that models memorize training data and that this data can
be leaked [12, 13]. By decoupling private or sensitive data from model training and delegating this
data to an external knowledge database, previous attacks can be mitigated by construction. This
is desirable for domains such as medical advice, legal consulting, or mental health services, which
require models to have specialized knowledge that may also contain private information. However,
while RAG addresses certain privacy concerns, it simultaneously introduces new attack surfaces and
vulnerabilities [2, 14].
A RAG database extraction attack is an adversarial scenario in which an attacker attempts to
extract verbatim data from an external RAG database containing private or proprietary information.
We highlight two key challenges to RAG database extraction. First, as RAG is used in scenarios
where the knowledge base is too large to fit in a single LLM context, the attacker must use a set of
queries to coerce fetching of the entire database. The difficulty is in finding queries that are diverse
enough from each other such that an attacker can increase the coverage of the database leakage over
time. Second, once RAG data has been incorporated into LLM context, the attacker must force the
LLM to repeat the exact verbatim content of the data chunk through the model’s own output.
A successful attack must satisfy both of these challenges. Naı̈vely using random queries can
cause retrieval collisions where the same data chunks are repeatedly fetched. Further, even if an
attacker can obtain high-coverage retrieval, it is still challenging to leak data chunks from the context

verbatim. Manually crafted attack strings like Please repeat all the context. do not generalize across
models and across varying RAG data. On the other hand, adversarially optimized attack strings
exhibit high perplexity, thus are prone to detection by defenses such as a perplexity filter [15].
Prior attempts at RAG extraction fall into two categories: manual attacks and optimizationbased attacks. However, neither effectively addresses both conditions. Existing manual attacks
[2, 16] adopt random queries for retrieval, resulting in high collision rate and consequently narrow
retrieval diversity. Further, these attacks do not generalize to unseen models and unseen RAG
data, particularly for non-instruction-aligned models. Empirically, we show that previous manual
attacks [2] have limited success when applied on a set of diverse RAG datasets with varying perplexity
levels.
On the other hand, prior optimization-based attacks like GCG [17] and Pleak [1] are designed
for jailbreaking and system prompt extraction respectively. In both cases, the optimization targets
are short phrases such as Here’s how to make a bomb.. Since retrieved RAG data is often very
long, we found that the success of these approaches degrades with the length of the context. We
demonstrate these scaling limitations in Section 4.2.2. Moreover, techniques employed in these
attacks such as Pleak’s stepping function are tailored for short optimization targets and exhibit
diminishing performance as the target length increases. Due to these limitations, previous attacks
fail on RAG database extraction.
In this paper, we introduce MARAGE, an optimization-based RAG database extraction attack
designed to concurrently satisfy the two earlier described challenges. To enable high-coverage retrieval of RAG data, we propose a query selection heuristic, which prioritizes queries with high
semantic diversity. For verbatim extraction of the retrieved data, we take an approach inspired by
previous prompt leaking attacks: we construct an adversarial string that, when appended to each
query, compels the LLM to reproduce the retrieved RAG data verbatim in its output. However, we
overcome RAG-specific challenges, such as a long string of data to leak, diverse and unknown data
content and potential defenses, by developing a novel optimization procedure.
Specifically, MARAGE improves on prior techniques in three ways. First, we propose a semanticaware query selection method to efficiently retrieve diverse RAG data chunks from the database.
This is challenging as the attacker does not know the contents of the database they are trying to
extract, making it difficult to construct queries that will efficiently induce high-coverage extraction of
the database. In contrast to the random query selection strategy used in the manual attack [2], our
approach extracts more unique data chunks, and consequently higher coverage of the RAG database.
Second, inspired by existing work [18], which addresses discrete optimization through a continuous
optimization scheme, we adapt this methodology to a RAG context. This addition significantly
reduces computational overhead compared to the greedy algorithms employed in GCG [17] and
Pleak [1]. We then propose a novel approach to expand the framework of this optimization problem
to incorporate losses computed across multiple models with diverse architectures. This enhancement
ensures that the resulting adversarial string is optimized to retain semantics that transfer across
different models. Additionally, due to the long optimization targets we handle, we design a strategy
called primacy weighting to assign different weights to losses obtained on different tokens within
the targets, leveraging the autoregressive nature of LLMs. Our approach applies higher weights to
the initial tokens in the sequence to ensure the LLM prioritizes the starting portion of the target
RAG data. This technique allows us to achieves enhanced effectiveness in extracting the exact RAG

data verbatim. Third, to evade perplexity based defenses [15], we introduce perplexity-constrained
optimization, a strategy that generates lower-perplexity adversarial strings by incorporating the log
perplexity of the attack string itself into the loss function. We further perform probing tasks to
investigate how the model’s internal state is affected by the presence of our adversarial string and
to explain why MARAGE is more robust than other approaches.
To summarize, we make the following contributions:
• We propose a semantic-aware query selection technique for retrieving a broader range of unique
data from the RAG database. By integrating our optimized attack string with the selected
queries, MARAGE achieves superior performance over the baseline in an end-to-end RAG
pipeline.
• We addresses the discrete optimization problem for finding the adversarial string through a
continuous optimization scheme. Additionally, we extend this method to a multi-model setting
and integrate novel strategies like primacy weighting and perplexity-constrained optimization
to enhance the RAG data extraction capability of the adversarial string.
• We conducted probing tasks to investigate the impact of the adversarial string generated by
MARAGE on the model’s internal states and provided an explanation for why MARAGE is
more effective.

Chapter 2

Background
In this chapter, we establish the fundamental background required to understand the design principles and motivations underlying MARAGE. We first review the core components of large language
models, including tokenization strategies, model architectural, decoding methods, and post-training
approaches such as parameter-efficient fine-tuning. We then introduce the framework of RetrievalAugmented Generation (RAG) and discuss the key technical concepts of RAG systems necessary to
understand the operational mechanisms of MARAGE and its baseline methods.

2.1

Large Language Models

Large Language Models (LLMs) are neural architectures developed to understand and generate
natural language by learning probabilistic distributions from massive text corpora. Advances in
architectural design, optimization techniques, and training paradigms have substantially improved
their capability, enabling LLMs to perform competitively across a wide range of natural language
processing (NLP) tasks, such as open-ended text generation, logical reasoning, summarization, question answering, and code synthesis. Contemporary LLMs widely adopt Transformer-based architectures [19] and are trained under an autoregressive framework, where the model learns to predict the
next token conditioned on preceding contexts.
This section provides an overview of the fundamental building blocks of LLMs, covering tokenization strategies, Transformer architectures, learning objectives, inference-time decoding methods, and
parameter-efficient fine-tuning approaches for model adaptation.

2.1.1

Tokenization

Tokenization refers to the procedure of transforming raw textual input into a sequence of discrete
units, or tokens, that can be consumed by neural language models [20]. Because language models
operate over a fixed vocabulary, the choice of tokenization strategy directly influences computational
efficiency, representational capability, and the model’s ability to generalize across diverse inputs.
Early natural language processing systems typically employed word-level tokenization. While
conceptually simple, this approach leads to unavoidably large vocabularies and struggles to represent
rare or unseen words, resulting in limited robustness. To address these limitations, modern large
language models typically rely on subword-level tokenization [21, 22], which provides a compromise

4

CHAPTER 2. BACKGROUND

5

between vocabulary compactness and expressive power by decomposing words into smaller, reusable
units.
Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) [23] is among the most commonly adopted subword tokenization
techniques. BPE operates by iteratively merging the most frequently co-occurring pairs of symbols,
beginning from an initial character-based representation. This process continues until a target
vocabulary size is achieved. By constructing tokens from frequently occurring subword fragments,
BPE allows models to represent infrequent or novel words as combinations of known units.
Byte-level Byte Pair Encoding (BBPE) [24] further generalizes this approach by applying the
BPE algorithm directly to byte sequences rather than Unicode characters. This design choice eliminates the need for language-specific preprocessing and guarantees full coverage of all possible input
strings. As a result, byte-level tokenization is particularly capable for multilingual settings and codefocused language models, while also providing more consistent handling of whitespace, punctuations,
and special symbols.
Alternative tokenization methods, such as Unigram Language Model tokenization [22], adopt
similar subword-based methods but differ in their underlying optimization criteria. Regardless of
the specific method employed, tokenization defines the discrete vocab space over which a language
model learns to estimate probability distributions, making it a foundational component of large
language modeling.

2.1.2

Transformer Architecture

Most contemporary LLMs are based on the Transformer architecture, which replaces recurrent or
convolutional structures with the self-attention mechanisms [19]. By eliminating sequential recurrence, the Transformer enables efficient parallel computation over tokens and effective modeling of
long-range dependencies, making it more powerful for large-scale language modeling compared to
earlier recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [25] or long short-term memory networks (LSTMs) [26].
A typical Transformer-based LLM consists of three main components: an embedding layer, a
stack of Transformer blocks, and an output projection layer.
Embedding Layer.

The embedding layer maps each discrete input token to a dense vector repre-

sentation in a continuous latent space. Formally, given a token xt from a vocabulary V, it is mapped
to an embedding vector
et = E[xt ],

E ∈ R|V|×d

where d denotes the model or internal dimensionality.
Unlike RNNs or LSTMs, the Transformer architecture does not inherently encode the sequential
order of tokens and is therefore permutation-invariant. To provide the model with information about
token positions, positional encodings [27–29] are introduced. In decoder-only architectures, which
are widely used in large language models, these encodings are directly added to the token embeddings
to maintain the causal structure for autoregressive generation. Positional encodings often involves
sinusoidal functions [29] to provide encoding for relative distances, and are typically combined with

2.1.4

Decoding Schemes

During inference, LLMs generate text by iteratively sampling tokens from the predicted probability distribution. The choice of decoding strategy significantly affects the quality, diversity, and
determinism of generated text.
Greedy Decoding [39] selects the token with the highest probability at each step. While computationally efficient, it often leads to repetitive or suboptimal outputs.
Beam Search [39] maintains multiple candidate sequences at each generation step and selects
the most likely overall sequence. Although beam search improves likelihood-based metrics, it may
reduce output diversity and is less commonly used for open-ended generation.
Sampling-based methods [39] introduce stochasticity to improve diversity. Top-k sampling restricts sampling to the k most probable tokens. Top-p (nucleus) sampling samples from the smallest
set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds a threshold p. Temperature scaling controls the
sharpness of the probability distribution, balancing randomness and determinism.
Modern LLM deployments typically combine these techniques to achieve a trade-off between
quality and diversity, depending on the application.

2.1.5

Parameter-Efficient Fine Tuning

Fine-tuning large language models on downstream tasks can be prohibitively expensive due to their
scale. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods aim to adapt pre-trained LLMs while
updating only a smaller subset of parameters, thereby reducing memory and computational costs.
Adapter-based methods, such as LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) [40], insert lightweight trainable
modules into Transformer layers while keeping the original model parameters frozen. LoRA approximates weight updates using low-rank decompositions, achieving efficient task adaptation with
minimal overhead.

CHAPTER 2. BACKGROUND

9

Prompt Tuning [41] optimizes a small set of continuous prompt embeddings that are prepended
to the input sequence. The underlying model remains frozen, and task-specific behavior is induced
through learned prompts in the embedding space. P-Tuning [42] extends prompt tuning by introducing deep prompt embeddings that are injected into multiple layers of the Transformer. This
approach improves expressiveness and task performance, particularly for complex reasoning tasks.
PEFT techniques have become a standard practice for post-training LLMs in resource-constrained
settings and are especially relevant in scenarios involving rapid domain adaptation.

2.2

Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a framework designed to extend the capabilities of large
language models by leveraging external knowledge sources during text generation [43]. Rather than
depending exclusively on information encoded within the model’s parameters, RAG dynamically
accesses relevant documents or passages from external corpora and incorporates this context into
the generation process. By doing so, it addresses challenges such as model hallucination, outdated
knowledge, and limited domain coverage, while producing outputs that are both more accurate
and interpretable. A typical RAG pipeline consists of three core components: retrieval, reranking,
and generation. In this section, we focus primarily on the retrieval and reranking stages, and
further review recent advances that incorporate structured information through graph-based retrieval
mechanisms. In addition, we discuss several techniques that improve the effectiveness of RAG
systems, such as query rewriting to better align user inputs with retrievable content, and context
compression to reduce redundancy and noise in retrieved documents.

2.2.1

Retrieval

The retrieval stage aims to identify a set of candidate documents or passages that are relevant
to a given user query. These candidates are subsequently used to augment the input context to
the language model. Retrieval methods can be broadly categorized into sparse retrieval and dense
retrieval approaches.
Sparse Retrieval. Sparse retrieval methods represent text using high-dimensional sparse vectors, where each dimension corresponds to a term in the vocabulary. A widely used sparse retrieval
algorithm is BM25 [44], which ranks documents based on term frequency, inverse document frequency, and document length normalization. BM25 is computationally efficient and effective for
keyword-based matching, making it a strong baseline for many information retrieval tasks.
However, sparse methods rely heavily on lexical overlap between queries and documents, which
limits their ability to capture semantic similarity when different surface text forms express the same
meaning.
Dense Retrieval. Dense retrieval methods address this limitation by embedding queries and
documents into a shared continuous vector space. Relevance is computed using similarity metrics
such as cosine similarity. A common architecture for dense retrieval is the Dual-Path Encoder [45],
also known as a bi-encoder, where the query and document are encoded independently by neural
networks with shared or partially shared parameters.
Dense retrieval methods allow models to capture semantic similarity, enabling them to retrieve
documents that are paraphrased or contextually related to the query, rather than relying solely

CHAPTER 2. BACKGROUND

10

on exact lexical overlap. This capability makes dense retrieval particularly suitable for large-scale
information retrieval tasks, where efficiency and scalability are critical. In practice, the embeddings
of all candidate documents can be precomputed and stored in a vector database, such as FAISS [46] or
ChromaDB [47], which are optimized for fast similarity search. At query time, the system performs
Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) search to efficiently identify the most relevant embeddings.
Various ANN algorithms can be employed, such as Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW)
graphs [48], which provide logarithmic search complexity while maintaining high recall. By leveraging
these vector search structures and algorithms, dense retrieval systems can efficiently scale to millions
or even billions of documents without sacrificing retrieval quality.

2.2.2

Reranking

While the retrieval stage aims to maximize recall by selecting a broad set of candidate documents,
the reranking stage refines this set by more accurately estimating relevance. Reranking typically
operates on a smaller candidate pool produced by the retriever and prioritizes precision.
A common approach to reranking uses Cross-Encoders [49], which jointly encode the query
and each candidate document. Unlike dual-path encoders, cross-encoders allow full token-level
interaction between the query and document through self-attention, leading to more fine-grained
relevance modeling.
The reranker assigns a relevance score to each query-document pair, and the candidates are reordered accordingly. Although cross-encoders are computationally more expensive than bi-encoders,
their use is feasible at this stage due to the reduced number of candidates. In RAG pipelines,
reranking significantly improves the quality of retrieved context, which directly impacts downstream
generation performance.

2.2.3

Graph-RAG

Graph-based Retrieval-Augmented Generation, commonly referred to as Graph-RAG [50], extends
traditional RAG by incorporating structured knowledge in the form of graphs. Instead of retrieving
isolated text passages, Graph-RAG leverages relationships between entities, documents, or code
components to provide richer contextual information.
Knowledge Graph-Based Retrieval. In knowledge graph-based RAG [51], nodes represent
entities or concepts, and edges encode semantic relationships. Retrieval is performed by identifying relevant subgraphs based on the query and traversing connected nodes to collect contextual
information. This approach enables multi-hop reasoning and improves consistency when generating
responses that depend on the relational knowledge.
Call Graphs and Program Structure for Coding Agents. In code generation and software
engineering tasks, Graph-RAG can be instantiated using call graphs or dependency graphs, where
nodes represent functions or modules and edges capture invocation or dependency relationships [52].
By retrieving structurally related code components, Graph-RAG provides language models with
context that reflects program semantics rather than isolated code snippets.
Graph-RAG enhances retrieval by exploiting relational structure, achieving more context-aware
generation. This paradigm is particularly effective in complex reasoning scenarios, such as multi-step
question answering, knowledge-intensive tasks, and agent-based systems such as coding agents.

RAG Enhancement Techniques

Beyond the core retrieval and reranking components, a variety of enhancement techniques have been
proposed to further improve the effectiveness, robustness, and efficiency of Retrieval-Augmented
Generation systems. These techniques aim to address practical challenges such as ambiguous user
queries, noisy or redundant retrieved content, and limited context window size.
Query Rewriting. Query rewriting techniques [53] seek to transform the original user query
into a form that is more suitable for retrieval. User queries are often underspecified, ambiguous, or
conversational in nature, which can lead to suboptimal retrieval results. Query rewriting addresses
this issue by reformulating the input query to better align with the structure and vocabulary of the
underlying document corpus.
Recent approaches leverage large language models themselves to perform query expansion or decomposition, generating multiple semantically related sub-queries or clarifying implicit user intent.
These rewritten queries can improve recall by capturing alternative phrasings and relevant contextual details, and are particularly effective in multi-hop question answering and complex reasoning
scenarios. Query rewriting has become a common component in modern RAG systems deployed in
industry-scale applications.
Context Compression. Context compression techniques aim to reduce redundancy and noise in
retrieved documents before they are passed to the language model. Since LLMs are constrained by a
finite context window, naively concatenating retrieved passages may introduce irrelevant information
and dilute useful signals.
Context compression methods include passage filtering, extractive summarization, and tokenlevel relevance scoring, which selectively retain only the most informative segments of retrieved
documents. More recent approaches employ lightweight neural models or attention-based mechanisms to score and prune retrieved content conditioned on the query. By compressing context while
preserving salient information, these techniques improve generation quality and reduce computational overhead.
Recent Advances. Recent research and industrial systems have explored additional enhancements, such as adaptive retrieval strategies that dynamically adjust the number of retrieved documents, and iterative RAG pipelines that interleave retrieval and generation steps. Other emerging
directions include self-reflective retrieval, where the model evaluates the sufficiency of retrieved evidence, and hybrid systems that combine unstructured text retrieval with structured sources such as
databases or knowledge graphs.
Collectively, these enhancement techniques play a critical role in improving the reliability, scalability, and practical deployment of RAG systems, and are increasingly integrated into state-of-the-art
retrieval-augmented language modeling frameworks.

3.1

Optimization-based Attacks Against LLMs

In the family of optimization based attacks, there have been general jailbreaking attempts such as
GCG [17], which optimizes an adversarial suffix appended to the harmful request against a target
string that jailbreaks the LLMs, e.g., Sure, here’s how to make a bomb.. However, this attack does
not scale to longer RAG text targets, as discussed in Section 4.2.2. Therefore, this attack has
shown limited effectiveness in our evaluation, where we had to compromise by reducing the number
of candidate tokens from 512 to 16. A more relevant work is Pleak [1], which utilizes a similar
greedy optimization approach to that of GCG to manipulate the model to output its own system
prompts. Although this approach has demonstrated strong performance in system prompt leaking,
its effectiveness diminishes rapidly as the optimization target length increases. Specifically, Pleak
uses a stepping mechanism, which progressively expands the visible portion of the optimization
targets in discrete “steps”. This mechanism limits its effectiveness in extracting long targets. In our
evaluations, Pleak often reconstructs only the initial tokens of the target that correspond to the first
step. In comparison, our method adopts a different strategy that exposes the whole optimization
target at once while assigning different weights to the losses from tokens at different positions in
the targets. This strategy provides a smooth transition in the weights assigned to different tokens,
enabling the reconstruction of the complete RAG data.

3.2

Attacks on RAG Systems

The first category is knowledge corruption attacks, where the attacker manipulates the RAG database,
allowing them to control the content retrieved by the system. Zou et al. [54] proposed an attack
method involving injecting a small amount of malicious contents into the database. They defined
two key conditions, the retrieval and generation condition. Once these conditions are met, the injected contents will be retrieved from the database, guiding the LLM to generate outputs that the
attacker desires. The second category is membership inference attack that aims to infer if a piece of
data belongs to the database. Anderson et al [55] proposes a simple approach that directly prompts
the RAG system whether a specific piece of data is within the knowledge database. On the other
hand, Li et al. [56] proposes to use semantic similarity between the generated content and the target

determine if a specific sample is within the database. The third category is data extraction attack.
Jiang et al. [57] leverages manually crafted attack strings and a LLM to generate additional queries
based on previous responses. However, since their code is not publicly available and we find it hard
to reproduce their results, we did not include it in our evaluation.

3.3

Prompt stealing Attacks

There have been prompt stealing attacks in both text generation and multi-modal settings. Morris
et al. [58] proposed an approach that utilizes the unrolled logit values as input features to train an
encoder-decoder model. This model maps the sequence of logit values back to the corresponding
input data, effectively reconstructing the inputs. Although this approach achieves low exact matches,
it only requires black-box access to the model and relies solely on the output logits. Sha and
Zhang [59] utilize a parameter extractor to classify prompt types (direct, role-based, in-context) and
predict features like roles or context numbers. A prompt reconstructor then uses these features and
LLM outputs to recreate prompts. In the text-to-image domain, a related work by Wen et al. [18]
optimizes hard text prompts using gradients derived from continuous embeddings. This approach
mitigates the high computational cost associated with the discrete token space. While we were
inspired by their approach in solving the discrete optimization problem, their task is completely
different from our RAG database extraction. Therefore, their method fails to address extraction of
long targets or possible defenses in our case.

Methodology

The objective of the attack is to retrieve and extract as large a portion of the RAG database as

4.2.1

Semantic-aware Query Selection

To retrieve diverse data from the database, we propose a semantic-aware query selection strategy
that prioritizes queries with maximal semantic diversity. First, we construct an initial query pool
composed of random text segments, which could be obtained, for example, by sampling from the
random crawl [61] dataset. We then use this to generate queries to enable retrieval of the RAG
database’s contents. Conducting the attack with the entire query pool is unpractical due to the
prohibitively high cost and can lead to high collision rates, where the same data chunks are retrieved repeatedly. To efficiently retrieve a large portion of unique data chunks, the attack should
strategically select queries from the query pool that can retrieve distinct data. Thus, the selected
queries should maximize their semantic diversity. To do so, our approach maintains a dedicated
embedding database, Ec , which stores the embeddings of all previously retrieved data throughout
the attack process. For each subsequent query selection, we evaluate the query pool and select the
query q whose embedding exhibits the lowest cosine similarity to those in Ec . This query is then
used in the next attack iteration and removed from the pool.

Relaxing Discrete Optimization
Discrete optimization poses a significant challenge due to the discrete nature of tokens in the LLM’s
vocabulary. Specifically, not all instances of the continuous embedding values correspond to valid
tokens. An approach to this problem is the use of gradient-based greedy algorithms, as demonstrated
by GCG [17] and Pleak [1]. Appendix A examines the high memory and computational costs
associated with GCG and Pleak. Therefore, adapting these approaches to our scenario would require
us to significantly reduce the number of candidate tokens, resulting in compromised performance. To
address these limitations, we instead adopt a hybrid approach that relaxes the discrete optimization
process by combining the benefits of optimizing over both hard, discrete tokens and soft, continuous
embeddings.
Instead of greedily evaluating tokens to find the one that can reduce the loss the most, we adopt
the algorithm proposed by [18] as our optimization foundation, adapting from their multi-modal
setting to our adversarial objective of RAG extraction. In their optimization approach, gradients

CHAPTER 4. RETRIEVAL-AUGMENTED GENERATION DATABASE EXTRACTION ATTACK MECHANISM18

are directly computed on and used to update the embeddings EADV of the adversarial tokens in
ADV , which are continuous in nature. This technique avoids the performance bottleneck associated
with the large number of forward passes required in greedy algorithms. To handle the challenge that
optimized embeddings may not correspond to actual tokens, their method finds the tokens ADV ′
that have embeddings closest to EADV and uses them as inputs to compute the gradients on the
embeddings EADV ′ corresponding to ADV ′ . These gradients then update EADV so that in each
optimization step, the loss is calculated using authentic tokens to avoid accumulated deviations.

enhanced transferability of the optimized adversarial string across different LLMs embedded in the
RAG systems. We extend the optimization to minimize the aggregated loss over m models, fθ1:m .

We evaluate MARAGE against three representative defenses.

Adversarially crafted attacks often produce high-perplexity strings, which can serve as a defense [15].
Therefore, perplexity filters [15] can be used to identify the high-perplexity user inputs q ∥ ADV .
To circumvent the perplexity filter, we propose the perplexity-constrained attack by incorporating
the perplexity (PPL) of the attack string directly into the loss function, thereby constraining the
perplexity of the optimized adversarial example. Since PPL is computed as the exponential of the
sequence probability assigned by the LLM, its value can fluctuate drastically during optimization as
the attack string evolves. To ensure smoother loss computation and mitigate gradient instability, we
employ log perplexity instead. Thus, our perplexity-constrained loss function is defined as follows:
LPPLfθ (ADV ) = Lfθ (ADV ) + λ log P P L(ADV )

(4.10)

Where λ is the hyperparameter for scheduling the relative strength of the adversarial loss Lfθ and the
PPL. Additionally, due to the Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenization strategy [63], shorter tokens
occur more frequently and thus tend to exhibit lower perplexity across diverse contexts. To ensure
the optimized attack string consists of short tokens, we exclude tokens exceeding six characters in
length within the projection function defined in Equation 4.8.
Furthermore, perplexity is computed on the average log probability over a sequence, meaning a
longer low-perplexity segment can offset the impact of high-perplexity regions. To leverage this, we
not only optimize for low-perplexity attack strings but also increase the length of the query q by
concatenating two original queries together, thereby further reducing the overall perplexity of the
input.
Prompt-based Defenses.

The second defense involves enhancing the system prompt to explicitly

instruct the LLM not to reveal its contexts or to reject queries containing nonsensical strings.
We incorporated two types of such defenses shown in Appendix G. However, MARAGE remains

CHAPTER 4. RETRIEVAL-AUGMENTED GENERATION DATABASE EXTRACTION ATTACK MECHANISM20

largely resilient in our evaluations, as its optimized attack strings evade lexical detection heuristics
established by these defensive prompts.
Paraphrasing Defense.

The third defense leverages query enhancement techniques that are com-

monly adopted in modern RAG systems. To improve robustness and retrieval stability, productionlevel RAG pipelines often incorporate query rewriting mechanisms [53], which reformulate userprovided queries before retrieval. This design choice accounts for the fact that user inputs are
frequently noisy or out of distribution. By using an LLM to rewrite incoming queries into a more
standardized and retrieval-effective form, the system can significantly improve downstream retrieval
quality and overall pipeline robustness.
This query rewriting stage implicitly functions as a defense against MARAGE, as the injected
attack string may be altered or removed during reformulation, therefore reducing its effectiveness in
extracting the retrieved data. To prevent the attack string from being modified at this stage, we wrap
it within an explicit instruction that directs the rewriting model to preserve the enclosed content
exactly. We present MARAGE against this type of defense in Section 5.2 and the results demonstrate
that this straightforward prompt-based strategy successfully bypasses the query rewriting defense,
allowing the attack string to remain unchanged through the rewriting process.

4.2.4

Attack Failure Detection

In a real-world attack scenario, the attacker lacks knowledge of the ground-truth data chunks in the
database, necessitating a method to detect failure cases where the extracted data are not verbatim
repetition of the actual RAG data. We observe that these extraction failures exhibit consistent
patterns, the most prevalent being repetitive outputs, excessively short responses, and refusals.
These failure modes can be reliably identified using simple pattern-matching filters. Additionally,
when the topk retrieval setting exceeds 1, we must not only determine whether the attack successfully
extracted any data chunk but also identify which specific chunks were retrieved. To address this,
we leverage the RAG pipeline’s prompt engineering, which inserts identifiers such as ”Document
1:” into the LLM’s input. By searching for these keywords in the output, we can isolate individual
extracted chunks and assess whether any exhibit failure patterns (repetitions, brevity, or refusals).
We evaluate our failure case identifier on the attack results in Table 5.2, achieving an average true
positive rate of 0.914 (std = 0.059) and a false positive rate of 0.061 (std = 0.029). This indicates
that our method effectively distinguishes between successful and failed extractions.

evaluation samples are distinct, mitigating concerns about training data leakage.

5.1.2

Evaluation Metrics

To evaluate the performance of the attacks, we utilize metrics that measure the similarity between
the recovered and original RAG data, either at the textual level or the semantic level. The four
metrics we adopted are as follows:
• Exact Match (EM)(↑). We consider an attack attempt a successful EM only if d is strictly a
sub-string of the output of fθ , excluding punctuations.
• BLEU Score (BLEU)(↑). BLEU Score, which is between 0 and 1, evaluates the text similarity
between the output generated by fθ and the input RAG data d by comparing the overlap of
their n-grams.
• Extended Edit Distance (EED)(↓). EED, which is between 0 and 1, measures the minimum

CHAPTER 5. EVALUATION

23

number of operations needed to transform the output generated by fθ to the actual RAG data
d. The number of operations is normalized by the total number of characters.
• Semantic Similarity (SS)(↑). SS, which is between -1 and 1, measures the semantic gap between
the output generated by fθ and the input RAG data d. The semantic distance is interpreted as
the cosine similarity between the embedding vectors obtained through a sentence transformer
[68] as the encoder.

5.1.3

Baseline Methods

We compare the performance of MARAGE against three baseline methods: Manual attack [2],
GCG [17], and Pleak [1]. The settings that we adopt for evaluating them are as follows:
• Manual template-based attack: We use their original code to evaluate on our datasets.
• GCG: We change the optimization goals in their code to the RAG data, and then run their code
to obtain the adversarial string. Due to the computation cost imposed by the forward passes
and the long targets, we had to significantly lower the number of greedy token evaluations
from 512 adopted in its original jailbreaking task to 16 in our task.
• Pleak: Similar to GCG, we change the optimization goals to RAG data and obtain the adversarial string.

RQ3: Transferability

In this research question, we study the impact of different hyperparameters on the performance of

5.6.1

Decaying Rate

The primacy weighting mechanism is a critical component of our optimization strategy, with the
decaying mask value α playing a pivotal role in determining its success. As shown in Figure 5.4, the
absence of a decaying mask limits the generalization capability of the optimized ADV ′ , resulting in
an EM accuracy of only 0.293. In contrast, incorporating a decay rate of 0.9 more than doubles the
EM accuracy to 0.796.

Figure 5.4: Impact of the decay rate evaluated on LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct and Rag-12000.
The effectiveness of the primacy weighting mechanism is particularly evident when our optimization is performed on a Dp containing multiple targets, where the accumulated losses across these
targets make generalization challenging. When multiple models are included in the joint optimization, the number of accumulated losses multiplies, further complicating the optimization process.
Calculating losses on all tokens in d worsens the difficulty of finding an ADV ′ that generalizes across
all targets. The decaying mask addresses this challenge by concentrating the loss calculation on the
initial tokens of d while still accounting for the later tokens. Additionally, this mechanism does not
compromise the attack’s effectiveness in extracting the entire RAG data due to the autoregressive
nature of LLMs. By compelling the LLMs to generate the initial tokens in d precisely, the likelihood
of continuing to generate the remaining tokens in the sequence increases, ensuring the success of the
attack.

Layer and Token-wise Probing

In this section, we study the internal representations of the LLM under attack by conducting probing
tasks, which explains why MARAGE is effective in verbatim data extraction where the target strings
is very long. We then probe with the attack strings generated by Pleak [1] and the manual attack [2]
to study the differences. Probing [82] is one of the most prominent approaches to explain how the
internal states and representations of deep neural networks correlate with certain properties. It
usually involves a probing dataset Dprobe , and a probing classifier g which is trained to classify some
feature based on the model’s representations.
In our case, we aim to study how the model’s internal states will be affected when the attack
string ADV ′ is presented in the prompt. Therefore, we design our Dprobe to be a binary classification
dataset that includes either safe or attacked input data points. Safe data points contain only the
RAG data and the corresponding query: d ∥ q, while attacked ones further include the adversarial
string: d ∥ q ∥ ADV . We follow the methodology described in [83] to conduct per-layer probing
by training distinct probing classifiers g for the outputs of various LLM layers. Specifically, we
extract the attention layer representations corresponding to the i-th token generated by the model
fθ for a given input data point. This process is repeated for all the attention layers of layer n under
investigation. We denote the attention output on layer n and token i Oin . Oin will serve as the input
feature to g, while the linguistic property Z representing whether the model is being affected by the
attack for generating token i becomes the output label for g. Accordingly, the performance of the
classifier g in mapping Oin to Z reveals whether the attack string ADV imposes an influence that
lasts throughout the entire generation process.
According to the studies [82,83], the classifier’s fitting capability can exaggerate the test accuracy,
resulting in an overestimation of the model’s representation with respect to the property being
probed. Therefore, we adopt a linear classifier, and the V-usable information [83–85] as the metric
to minimize this impact. V-usable information (Vi) measures how effectively a model family V can
forecast the property Z based on a given input Oin :

Discussion
6.1

Agentic Systems

A limitation of the current study is that MARAGE is evaluated primarily in end-to-end RAG
pipelines, where retrieval-augmented generation is the core system behavior. In practice, modern
production systems increasingly adopt agentic architectures [86], where RAG serves as one component, alongside other modules like planning, tool call, and memory. Evaluating MARAGE in such
agentic systems represents an important and non-trivial direction for future work.
In agentic settings, the influence of the adversarial string may be diluted or transformed as they
propagate through multiple modules. For example, a planning module may break down user queries
to decompose tasks into sub-goals, or selectively invoke retrieval only at certain circumstances.
These intermediate transformations could weaken the effectiveness of attack tokens optimized under
simpler assumptions. Therefore, deploying MARAGE in such systems would require addressing
several challenges. First, MARAGE may need to influence the planning heuristics, or tool selection
so that the RAG module is guaranteed to be invoked. Another challenge is to extend MARAGE to
operate over trajectories rather than single-turn interactions.
Despite these challenges, agentic systems also present new opportunities for attack propagation.
If the adversarial string is successfully injected into an agent’s long-term memory or internal knowledge store, its impact may persist across tasks and sessions. Future work could investigate whether
MARAGE can be adapted to exploit such persistence, while also exploring defenses that prevent
untrusted contents from being permanently integrated into the agent state.

6.2

RAG Settings Extension

While MARAGE demonstrates strong attack effectiveness across a range of standard RAG configurations and techniques, there are yet more complex RAG settings, including larger backbone language
models, alternative retrieval architectures such as Graph-RAG, and multilingual environments.
First, evaluating MARAGE against larger language models remains an important direction. As
model size grows, the GPU resources required to optimize adversarial strings increase. Compared
to greedy-based methods such as GCG [17] and Pleak [1], MARAGE has demonstrated significantly higher resource efficiency. Therefore, extending the evaluation of MARAGE to larger model

37

CHAPTER 6. DISCUSSION

38

backbones and comparing its performance against these baselines may yield further insights and
potentially stronger empirical results.
Second, emerging variants such as Graph-RAG introduce structured retrieval mechanisms that
differ from flat document retrieval. Since MARAGE optimizes attack tokens to influence retrieval
and generation through embedding-based similarity, graph-structured retrieval could disrupt the
attack by constraining retrieval through explicit edges. Future work could explore adapting the
Semantic-Aware Query Selection technique to operate over graph nodes or paths, for example by
targeting entity representations rather than unstructured text embeddings.
Third, the current evaluation focuses primarily on English-language corpora. Because MARAGE
involves learning adversarial token representations, extending the attack to multilingual or nonEnglish settings raises additional challenges. Tokenization schemes, vocabulary distributions, and
semantic embedding spaces differ across languages, which may affect both attack transferability
and optimization stability. Evaluating MARAGE across multiple languages would provide a more
comprehensive understanding of its generalizability and reveal whether language-specific adaptations
are necessary.

6.3

More Effective Defenses

Finally, an important direction for future research concerns the development and evaluation of defenses against MARAGE style attacks. As RAG becomes increasingly central to language model
deployments, understanding and mitigating RAG related attacks will be critical for building trustworthy and resilient AI systems.
First, defenses at the training level may be explored. Adversarial training [87] introduces adversarial inputs during optimization, encouraging the model to reject high-perplexity or semantically
incoherent content, often with a DPO-style training framework [88]. While this approach can enhance robustness against adversarial attacks, it introduces an inherent trade-off. Overly strong
adversarial objectives may lead to catastrophic forgetting [89], degrading the model’s performance
on benign inputs. As a result, training-time defenses must carefully balance robustness and generalization to avoid sacrificing overall model utility.
Second possible defense involves post-generation verification, where generated responses are filtered or verified before being presented to users or propagated to downstream modules. One approach is to apply semantic or factual consistency checks to the generated output. Since MARAGE
aims to manipulate the generation process by injecting adversarial strings, the resulting outputs may
exhibit unnatural repetition, or distributional shifts in token. Statistical detectors or learned classifiers can be trained to identify such deviations by comparing outputs against a reference distribution
of benign generations. An alternative strategy is to measure the similarity between the generated
output and the input context, rejecting responses that mirror the prompt content too much. However, such filtering also faces limitations. Overly aggressive filtering can cause false positives due
to falsely suppressing legitimate responses. It also introduces latency, particularly in interactive or
real-time systems. Therefore, output filtering should be viewed as a complementary defense that
increases system robustness when combined with upstream protections such as adversarial training.

Chapter 7

Conclusion
RAG systems utilizing private or proprietary knowledge databases remain vulnerable to extraction
attacks. An adversary with only query access can extract a large portion of the RAG database
through carefully optimized inputs. In this work, we proposed MARAGE, an optimization-based
attack framework that addresses the two key challenges in RAG database Extraction: (1) retrieving
a broad and diverse subset of the database and (2) extracting verbatim reproduction of the long
retrieved data chunks.
By introducing the semantic-aware query selection and an optimized adversarial string generated
through a continuous optimization scheme leveraging multi-model optimization, primacy weighting,
and perplexity-constrained loss, MARAGE achieves significantly higher verbatim extraction rates
and broader database coverage than the baselines.
In end-to-end RAG pipelines, depending on database size and retrieval configuration, MARAGE
extracts between approximately 7% and 51% of the entire RAG database after only 100 queries,
outperforming the manual baseline by 2.6 to 6.5 times in extraction coverage and retrieving up to
29% more unique chunks. These findings indicate that even moderate query budgets can expose a
large portion of proprietary content in end-to-end RAG systems.
We also examine representative defenses. Perplexity-based filtering is ineffective against adaptive adversarial optimization, by leveraging the perplexity-constrained loss and query length manipulation, MARAGE maintains a near identical extraction success rate while significantly reducing
the perplexity signals. Prompt-based defensive instructions instructing the model not to reveal
its context fails to reduce MARAGE’s extraction success, while manual template attack is largely
neutralized. These results indicate that heuristic defenses are insufficient against MARAGE.
We further evaluated MARAGE under more advanced retrieval settings incorporating hybrid
search, reranking, and query rewriting. The attack’s performance varied by less than ±8% relative to
the standard pipeline, demonstrating robustness to implementation differences in retrieval strategy
and query processing. Extensive ablation studies further demonstrate MARAGE’s robustness to
variations in both prompt structure and decoding strategies employed in the target RAG pipeline.
This suggests that MARAGE does not depend on assumptions about retriever configurations and
can tolerate variations in different settings the target RAG system adopts.
To better understand MARAGE’s effectiveness, we conduct layer and token-wise probing analyses. Our results show that the optimized adversarial string induces a persistent and detectable shift

in the model’s internal representations across multiple layers and generated tokens. This “attacked
state” biases the model to verbatim reproduction of retrieved content. Compared to baseline attacks, MARAGE’s adversarial strings produce stronger and more sustained representational shifts,
helping explain its superior performance on long targets.
Finally, several directions can extend MARAGE beyond the current study. First, modern RAG
is increasingly embedded in agentic architectures that integrate planning, tool use, and memory.
Future work could extend MARAGE to trajectory-level optimization and influence planning or tool
selection to ensure retrieval is invoked. Additionally, adversarial content stored in long-term memory
may persist across sessions, creating new risks and motivating memory-level defenses. Second,
evaluating MARAGE on larger backbone models, graph-structured retrieval (e.g., Graph-RAG),
and multilingual corpora would improve understanding of scalability. Third, defenses could be
further explored. Promising directions include adversarial training that allows the model to learn to
reject adversarially crafted strings, and lightweight generation-phase verification without excessive
latency that detects overly similar outputs compared to the RAG chunks.

Cost of GCG, Pleak, and MARAGE

Gradient-based greedy algorithm has been adopted by GCG [17] and Pleak [1] for solving the discrete
optimization problem. This method involves leveraging gradient information to identify a set of
candidate tokens likely to reduce the objective loss, followed by evaluating these candidates through
actual forward passes to compute their losses. After the losses for all these candidates are obtained,
the one with the lowest actual loss will be adopted.

Figure 8.1: The GPU Ram consumption using LlaMA3-8B-Instruct versus the length of the optimization target. Both GCG and Pleak adopts 512 candidate tokens. For GCG and Pleak, the
section above 80 GB of Ram is estimated, as there is a roughly linear relationship between Ram
usage and target length.
However, both GCG and Pleak can become memory and compute intensive, especially under our
task where the optimization targets are long RAG data instead of short system prompts in Pleak
or initial affirmative responses in GCG. As shown in Figure 8.1, GCG can consume over 1000 GB
of GPU RAM when using 512 candidate tokens and targeting sequences longer than 300 words.
When running on a GPU with 80 GB of RAM, GCG supports 512 batched forward passes in its

47

CHAPTER 8. APPENDICES

48

original tasks, where the targets are short initial affirmative responses such as “Sure, here’s how
to make a bomb.”. However, this number decreases significantly to 8 to 16 in our task under the
same hardware conditions, depending on the model to optimize against. Additionally, the time for
generating all tokens in the target autoregressively is approximately proportional to the square of
the target length. This is because caching was disabled during inference to reduce memory usage.
As a result, the attack time increases significantly, as performing the forward passes to obtain losses
takes longer.
Conversely, Pleak sought to reduce memory consumption by employing 4-bit quantized models in
its optimization processes, achieving approximately a four-fold reduction in memory usage compared
to GCG. While this adaptation enabled optimization for their tasks with system prompts as targets
averaging around 50 tokens long, it proved inadequate for handling longer RAG data in our task,
where the targets average 830 tokens in length. Furthermore, using quantized models during the
optimization process negatively impacts the transferability of the resulting adversarial strings to
unseen, non-quantized models. In contrast, our method requires only about 48GB of GPU RAM,
even when the target sequences are approximately 600 words long, highlighting its superior efficiency
in memory usage.
