# Repair-verified critique credit

A judge that reviews a generated query is trained by reinforcement learning, and its reward has to say something about the reasoning as well as the verdict. In the RuCo-C paper the judge writes its check as a list of rubric questions and answers, and the process part of the reward scores those answers. Each answer is checked step by step by a strong LLM, and the process reward is "derived by verifying the correctness of the answer at each individual step via strong LLMs."[^c1] The authors also gate that process credit on the outcome: the process supervision is tested "conditional on a correct final judgment."[^c2]

The repair check is a further term, aimed at a case the outcome gate cannot handle. A judge can say the query is wrong when the dataset label says it is right. The outcome term then penalises a verdict that may be sound. The paper states the aim as combating "reward hacking" caused by noisy labels, where the model "is unjustly penalized for sound reasoning due to conflicting flawed labels."[^c3]

The mechanism is this. When the verdict disagrees with the label and the judge has flagged an error, the corrected query the judge proposes is compared with the predicted query by a test-suite check. The text says that "if an error is identified and the corrected SQL generated by the critique model passes verification, a small reward is given," and that if the correction fails, "a small penalty coefficient is applied."[^c4] The verifier is a comparison: "we use Test-Suite as the verifier by comparing between the predicted SQL and the corrected SQL."[^c5] The authors' reading is that "By validating the consistency of the generated correction SQL and the SQL being judged, we can provide positive feedback for sound reasoning despite label noise."[^c6]

Where the other cases go is set by the static coefficient. Read from the paper's equation, my reading and not a quoted statement: when the verdict agrees with the label, the process term is twice the rubric score; when it disagrees, the term is this repair credit; in all other cases it is zero. The repair credit is therefore paid only on disagreement, which is where a label-noise defence would matter.

The reported effect is an ablation, not a separate experiment. The variant with the verify-code term is compared with the variant without it under static coefficients, and "This effectiveness is particularly notable under static coefficients, with an average 4% improvement observed in the ACC metric."[^c7] The ablation uses one backbone: "all variants use Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruction as the backbone model."[^c8]

## Limits

- The direction of the comparison is not fully specified in the text. The paper speaks of "consistency" between the correction and the judged query, but it does not say whether a pass means the correction agrees with the predicted query on the suite or differs from it. Read as a repair test, the sign of the credit depends on that, and the text leaves it open (my reading).
- The term assumes that some flagged disagreements are label noise. The paper does not measure how many labels are wrong, or how often the term fires on a real error rather than a mislabelled case.
- The evidence is one ablation on one backbone across three text-to-SQL test sets, reported as an average. I did not find variance or seeds in the text I read.
- The check needs an executable correction. Outside a domain with a test suite, there is no analogue of this comparison; see [[executable-answer-verification]] for the execution-based check this term depends on. Whether a similar repair test works for prose answers is my inference, and untested here.
- The judge produces the verdict, the critique and the repair in one output. A policy could learn to flag errors whose repairs happen to pass the suite. The paper does not test for that exploitation of the repair term.
- The process term is weighted by a dynamic coefficient that depends on step count, on the stated ground that "questions of easy and medium difficulty typically involve around 5 reasoning steps, while those of hard and extra difficulty generally require 6 to 7 or more steps."[^c9] Step count is used as a difficulty proxy, and the ablation reports the static and dynamic coefficients together, so the repair term's effect is not separated from that weighting.
