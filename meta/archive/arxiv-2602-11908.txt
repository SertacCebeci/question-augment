A BSTRACT
LLMs are widely used, yet they remain prone
to factual errors that erode user trust and limit
adoption in high-risk settings. One approach to
mitigate this risk is to equip models with uncertainty estimation mechanisms that abstain when
confidence is low. However, this binary “allor-nothing” approach is excessively restrictive
in long-form settings, often discarding valuable
information. We introduce Selective Abstraction (SA), a framework that enables LLMs to
trade specificity for reliability by selectively reducing the detail of uncertain content. We first
formalize SA through the lenses of selective
risk and coverage. We then propose Atom-wise
Selective Abstraction, a claim-level instantiation that decomposes responses into atomic
claims (short, self-contained statements each
expressing a single fact) and replaces uncertain atoms with higher confidence, less specific
abstractions. To evaluate this framework, we
develop a novel end-to-end pipeline for openended generation that instantiates risk as factual correctness and measures coverage using
an information-theoretic measure of retained
information. Across six open-source models
on the FactScore and LongFact-Objects benchmarks, atom-wise SA consistently outperforms
existing baselines, improving the area under the
risk-coverage curve (AURC) by up to 27.73%
over claim removal, demonstrating that reducing specificity can boost accuracy and reliability
while preserving most of their original meaning.

specific in the wrong places can have dramatic consequences, while targeted abstraction, here removing just
two words, maintains usefulness without sacrificing reliability.

Related Work

Selective Prediction. We view selective prediction as a
starting point for formalizing Selective Abstraction. In
classic selective prediction [7, 12], a classifier may abstain when uncertain. Formally, a selective model is a pair
(f, g), where f is a classifier and g is a selection function deciding whether to predict or abstain. A common
choice defines g via a confidence score κ and threshold θ:
gθ (x|κ, f ) = 1[κ(x, ŷ|f ) > θ], where ŷ = f (x). Thus,
the model predicts only when its confidence exceeds θ.
Selective prediction induces a trade-off between risk and
coverage. Risk is defined with respect to a given loss,
e.g., the 0/1 loss, and is measured only over the samples
that are predicted. Coverage is the fraction of samples
that are not rejected. By rejecting uncertain samples,
the selective model aims to reduce risk at the cost of
lower coverage. Hierarchical selective classification [14]
extends selective classification to a hierarchical setting.
Under uncertainty, the model can retreat to a less specific ancestor label rather than abstaining (e.g.: predicting
“Dog” instead of “Golden Retriever”), thereby using the
hierarchy to guide a reduction in the information content
of predictions. This is conceptually aligned with our objective of trading specificity for reliability, rather than
relying on a binary abstention decision.

We introduce Selective Abstraction, a framework that enables LLMs to reduce the amount of information in longform generations when faced with uncertainty. Rather
than relying on binary all-or-nothing decisions, Selective
Abstraction retains informative content by reducing the
specificity of uncertain parts, yielding outputs that are
less specific but more reliable.

Selective Generation. Most existing work on uncertainty
estimation for text generation focuses on tasks where
the generated text is at most a few tokens long, such as
machine translation, text summarization, and question
answering (QA) [8, 24, 48]. Among these, QA is the
most relevant to our setting. However, even in openended QA benchmarks, the generated answers are typically short, e.g. TriviaQA [19] and CoQA [33]. This
allows for a binary decision: either accept or reject the
model’s output. This approach, known as selective generation [34], serves as the NLP analogue of selective
classification [8, 10, 25, 42, 43]. While these tasks are
considerably easier to evaluate, the uncertainty estimation
methods developed for them do not generalize well to
long-form text generation [50], which makes for a much
more realistic use case for LLMs.

The key contributions of this paper are as follows:
1. We introduce Selective Abstraction, a general framework for trading specificity for reliability in long-form
text generation, formalized through selective risk and coverage.
2. We propose atom-wise selective abstraction, a

than removing them entirely.
3. We develop an end-to-end evaluation pipeline for
selective abstraction in open-ended factual generation,
instantiating risk via factual correctness and coverage via
an information-theoretic measure of retained content.
4. We empirically demonstrate, across six open-source

RC Curves and AURC

Selective abstraction induces a trade-off between reliability and specificity, measured by risk and coverage.
We summarize this trade-off using Risk-Coverage (RC)
curves [7], which plot the risk of retained content as a
function of coverage. An example RC curve is shown in
Figure 1 (right).
Given a confidence scoring function κ(·) and a threshold
θ, SA produces an abstracted response T ′ (θ). For each θ,
we compute its coverage ϕ(T ′ (θ), T ) and risk R(T ′ (θ)),
as defined in the preceding subsections. Varying θ over
the range of κ traces an RC curve.
We summarize each RC curve using the area under the
R1
RC curve (AURC), defined as AURC = 0 R(ϕ) dϕ,
where R(ϕ) denotes the risk achieved at coverage ϕ. In
practice, since evaluation is performed on a finite set of
examples, we sweep over all distinct attainable thresholds {θk }K
k=1 , induced by the confidence scores in the
evaluation set, which yields a finite sequence of operating
points with coverages {ϕk } (sorted by increasing order).
If the minimum observed coverage is strictly positive,
we add an anchor point at ϕ = 0 with risk equal to the
risk at the minimum observed coverage. This prevents
methods that only attain a narrow, high-coverage range
from appearing artificially better due to integrating over a
smaller portion of [0, 1], and ensures AURC comparisons
reflect performance over a common coverage domain.
Lower AURC indicates a better risk-coverage trade-off
because it corresponds to lower risk on average across
the full coverage range.

I(c) increases as c narrows the set of compatible entities.
By construction, trivial claims (Ec = E, for instance: “X
is a person”) carry zero information.
Our notion of information aligns with prior work that
views information as uncertainty reduction [4, 5, 35].
This perspective also offers a useful intuition: our measure resembles information gain in decision-tree splits.
Each atomic claim narrows the set of compatible entities,
and I(c) quantifies the resulting uncertainty reduction.
We emphasize that this analogy is given for intuition only:
unlike decision trees on a fixed dataset, our splits are defined over an open-world entity set.
To estimate |Ec | and |E| automatically, we use Wikidata
[46], one of the largest publicly available knowledge
bases, which stores structured information about realworld entities and can be queried using SPARQL, a standard query language for structured knowledge bases. We
use the SPINACH agent [26], which translates naturallanguage questions into SPARQL queries and executes
them against the Wikidata Query Service to estimate the
sizes of the corresponding entity sets. Since obtaining
verified real-world counts is infeasible in an open-world
setting, we use Wikidata as a best-available proxy for
these set sizes.

Baselines. We compare our proposed abstraction method,
atom-wise Selective Abstraction, against the following
baselines (prompts are provided in Appendix F):
(1) Selective Redaction: a standard selective baseline
that removes all atoms whose confidence falls below a
threshold, without producing abstractions.
(2) Inline Abstraction: an adaptation of the aggregation
prompt baseline from Yona et al. [49]. This baseline reduces information during generation by instructing the
model to adjust the level of granularity in its response
according to its uncertainty.
(3) Self-Revision: a common approach to post-hoc refinement of generated text: we first generate T , and then
instruct the model to replace uncertain claims with less
specific but higher-confidence versions, or remove them
altogether.
(4) Additional θ-prompted variants: For Inline Abstraction and Self-Revision, we additionally report results for versions that explicitly include a value of θ in
the prompt, on a subset of settings.

confidence threshold θ̂ such that the atom-wise selective
abstraction achieves risk at most α + ϵ on new samples,
with confidence at least 1 − δ. Concretely, the algorithm
first queries M on each pi to obtain an initial response
Ti , and decomposes it into atomic claims A(Ti ),Sforming
a consolidated calibration set of atoms, Acal = i A(Ti ).
For each atom ak ∈ Acal , the algorithm computes a critical threshold θk , defined as the minimal confidence level
at which the abstraction selected for ak is correct. In
particular, if the original atom ak is already correct, then
θk = 0. Finally, the algorithm outputs θ̂, chosen as a
quantile of the calibration thresholds.

The Inline and Self-Revision baselines are implemented
via prompting. Evaluating them across the full range of
θ would require a separate prompt for each θ, which is
infeasible at our scale. Therefore, they appear as single operating points in our main comparison, and we do
not report AURC for these methods. Instead, to quantitatively compare against Inline and Self-Revision, we
report the risk difference at matched coverage (Table 2).
Across both datasets, atom-wise SA consistently achieves
markedly lower risk at matched coverage, with an average gap of about 10 percentage points. Observing the
RC Curves (Figures 7 and 8 in Appendix G), it is noticeable that the Inline and Self-Revision operating points lie
closer to the Redaction curve than to the atom-wise SA
curve, and in some cases even exceed Redaction, indicating behavior closer to atom deletion than to abstraction.
Finally, we report results for thresholded variants of the
Inline and Self-Revision baselines, that directly inject θ
into the prompts, but this did not produce a meaningful
trade-off (See Figure 9 in Appendix G.1).

is not a binary choice between answering and abstaining,
but a trade-off between specificity and correctness. We
introduce Selective Abstraction as a general framework

an atom-wise procedure that selectively reduces the information in uncertain claims. Our empirical evaluation
across six open-source LLMs and two open-ended factual

consistently outperforms standard baselines, including

Concluding Remarks

Threshold Selection for Target Risk

Thus far, we have studied the trade-off between risk and
coverage induced by atom-wise SA. However, practical deployment typically requires fixing a specific confidence threshold θ, which calls for a principled method
for choosing it. In this section, we propose an algorithm
for choosing θ to satisfy a user-specified target risk. Our
approach is inspired by split conformal prediction [45],
using a calibration set to select a confidence threshold
that, with high probability, guarantees that the risk on
unseen test samples does not exceed a desired target. The
full algorithm and its theoretical guarantees are provided
in Appendix H. Here, we describe the practical procedure
and present empirical results. The algorithm takes as
input a language model M, a target risk level α, and a
calibration set of prompts Dcal = {pi }ni=1 . It outputs a

Atomization Prompt
# Role and Objective
You are an Atom Extraction Assistant. Your task is to break down a given text into short, self-contained sentences
called atoms. Each atom should capture exactly one entity and one piece of information about that entity.
# Definition of an Atom
Each atom must capture exactly one entity and one piece of information about that entity. The atom must be in the
format: [entity] [information about the entity]. Both the entity and the information must be enclosed in square
brackets.
# Instructions
- Each atom must capture exactly one entity and one piece of information about that entity.
- Atoms must be as fine-grained as possible: each should express a **single, indivisible fact**.
- Do not merge multiple facts into one atom, even if they appear in the same clause or sentence.
- Ensure that each atom is independent and can be easily understood without relying on other atoms.
- If a sentence presents multiple facts—such as nationality, profession, or achievements—split them into separate
atoms.
- The entity must be a specific, concrete noun or noun phrase. Avoid vague terms like ”he”, ”she”, ”they”, or ”these”.
Entities should be simple and specific, such as ”Albert Einstein”, ”the Eiffel Tower”, ”the Mona Lisa”, ”Australia”,
”Apollo 11”, etc.
- These guidelines are crucial to ensure that each atom can be independently searched in knowledge bases to verify
its factual accuracy.
- Ensure that every piece of information given in the text is captured in the resulting atoms.
# Output Format
Output a numbered list of atoms:
1. [Entity] [Fact].
2. [Entity] [Fact].
...
# Examples
## Example 1
Input Text: Jennifer Coolidge is an American actress. Known for her work in the comedy genre, Coolidge is the
recipient of several accolades, including a Golden Globe Award and two Primetime Emmy Awards.
Atoms:
1. [Jennifer Coolidge] [is an American].
2. [Jennifer Coolidge] [is an actress].
3. [Jennifer Coolidge] [is known for her work in the comedy genre].
4. [Jennifer Coolidge] [has received several accolades].
5. [Jennifer Coolidge] [has received a Golden Globe Award].
6. [Jennifer Coolidge] [has received two Primetime Emmy Awards].

Abstraction Generation and Confidence Elicitation: Additional Discussion

This appendix expands on Section 3 (Selective Abstraction), focusing on Stage 3 (Abstraction) in the end-to-end
pipeline. In this stage, low-confidence atoms are abstracted into less specific alternatives in order to trade specificity for
reliability. We clarify the motivation behind Step (2), which constructs a sequence of abstraction candidates for each
atom and elicits confidence scores for these candidates conditioned on the atom’s original confidence.
Why generate a sequence of abstractions?
Selective abstraction requires choosing a minimally abstracted variant that satisfies the confidence threshold. Generating
a sequence of abstractions with gradually reduced specificity provides a discrete, ordered set of candidates for this
selection. This formulation makes thresholding well-defined: rather than relying on a single unconstrained rewrite,
the method can select the most specific abstraction whose confidence exceeds the threshold. In addition, eliciting
confidences jointly for related abstractions encourages a coherent progression in confidence as information is reduced,
aligning abstraction depth with reliability.
Why condition abstraction confidence on the atom’s confidence?
Conditioning abstraction confidence elicitation on the atom’s original confidence serves two purposes. First, it anchors
the abstraction confidences to the uncertainty of the underlying factual content, encouraging abstraction confidence to
reflect reduced specificity rather than a complete re-evaluation of factual correctness. Second, this conditioning helps
maintain comparability across atoms by evaluating each atom–abstraction group relative to its own baseline confidence,
reducing drift caused by prompt-level or statement-level variability. Together, these effects support a stable relationship
between abstraction depth and confidence, which is essential for consistent threshold-based selection.
Additionally, assigning confidence scores within the same elicitation call may help relatively calibrate abstraction
confidences across atoms by anchoring them to a common scale.

On the Importance of Choosing a Confidence Scoring Function

The choice of the confidence scoring function κ is critical, as it directly influences the quality of the downstream
abstractions. The confidence function determines which atoms are selected for abstraction and the degree to which they
are abstracted, thereby shaping the trade-off between information and reliability.
E.1

Comparison Metrics

RC Curves and AURC. We first compare confidence scoring functions directly by the risk-coverage trade-offs they
induce: a κ that achieves a better trade-off (lower AURC) is preferable [7].
Ranking (AUROC). Ranking captures the extent to which the confidence score induces a partial order on samples,
where correct samples are assigned higher confidence scores than incorrect samples [11]. In our setting, this property is
central to selective abstraction: a κ that ranks well will ensure that the atoms that are most likely to be incorrect are
abstracted first, since they will be assigned the lowest confidence values. Conversely, poor ranking leads to inefficient
abstraction, where correct atoms are unnecessarily abstracted while incorrect atoms are retained.
We quantify ranking quality using the area under the ROC curve (AUROC), which measures the probability that a
randomly chosen correct atom receives a higher confidence score than a randomly chosen incorrect atom. AUROC
therefore reflects how well κ separates correct from incorrect content across all possible thresholds. High AUROC
implies that abstraction can be focused on genuinely risky atoms, yielding substantial risk reduction for a given loss of
information.

the baseline. This saturation behavior is also visible in the corresponding RC curves, making P(True) ill-suited for
controlling abstraction across a broad coverage range.
Taken together, these results highlight that ranking quality is the primary driver of effective selective abstraction.
Verbalized confidence performs best on both AURC and AUROC, yielding the strongest risk-coverage trade-off and the
most reliable ordering of atoms by correctness likelihood. For this reason, we adopt verbal confidence as the default
confidence scoring function throughout this work.
