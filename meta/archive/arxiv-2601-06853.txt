Abstract

arXiv:2601.06853v3 [cs.CL] 1 Sep 2026

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting is widely
adopted for mathematical problem solving, including in low-resource languages, yet its robustness under irrelevant context - a known
vulnerability in English - remains unexamined in such settings. To systematically probe
this challenge, we introduce DISTRACTMATHBN, a Bangla benchmark that augments
MGSM and MSVAMP with semantically coherent but computationally irrelevant information. Evaluating seven models ranging from
3B to 12B parameters, we observe substantial performance degradation under distractors: standard models drop up to 41 points,
while reasoning-specialized models decline
14–20 points despite consuming 5× more tokens. We propose †DAGGER, which reformulates mathematical problem solving as executable computational graph generation with
dedicated modeling of distractor nodes. Finetuning Gemma-3 models using Supervised
Fine-Tuning followed by Group Relative Policy Optimization achieves comparable aggregate accuracy across original and distractoraugmented splits while using 89% fewer tokens than reasoning models. Importantly, this
robustness emerges without explicit training
on distractor-augmented examples. Our results suggest that enforcing structured intermediate representations can improve robustness
and inference efficiency in mathematical understanding compared to free-form reasoning,
particularly in noisy, low-resource settings.1

overhead of extended reasoning, particularly in
resource-constrained settings where both accuracy and eﬀiciency matter?
Research Questions Low-resource languages
like Bangla show greater vulnerability to irrelevant context during math reasoning (Sobhani
et al., 2025; Prama et al., 2025). While CoT
prompting improves English math reasoning (Wei
et al., 2022), it exhibits brittleness to distractors
(Shi et al., 2023). Reasoning models trained using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
achieve substantial gains in mathematical problemsolving (Guo et al., 2025) but require more tokens
(Snell et al., 2025), raising deployment concerns.
Recent works suggest reasoning traces produced
by reasoning models can be structured as graphs
(Minegishi et al., 2025), potentially enabling more
efficient reasoning (Yu et al., 2024). These observations motivate us to investigate the following research questions. RQ1: whether CoT-based
reasoning remains robust on distractor-augmented
Bangla MWPs; RQ2: whether structured reasoning frameworks can be distractor-aware without
targeted distractor-type training; and RQ3: the
computational trade-offs between robustness and
token efficiency.
Contributions We make the following contributions in this paper.
• We introduce DISTRACTMATH-BN, a novel
Bangla benchmark of 3,685 distractoraugmented problems derived from MGSM and
MSVAMP validated by GPT and human expert.

The benchmark systematically incorporates
three classes of interference - Related Entity
Distractors, Orthogonal Attribute Distractors,
and Null-Effect Event Distractors (details in
Section 3, examples in Appendix A.11).
• Thorough evaluation of seven open-weight
models ranging from 3B to 12B parameters, we
show that CoT-based reasoning degrades substantially in the presence of distractors.
• Our proposed framework †DAGGER reformulates mathematical problem solving as executable computational graph generation with explicit modeling of distractor nodes.
• We show substantial accuracy and token
efficiency over long-form CoT by fine-tuning
Gemma-3 (SFT+GRPO) using †DAGGER
framework for improved graph generation.
Overall, our findings indicate that robustness to
distractor information is not guaranteed by longer
reasoning traces alone. Instead, enforcing structured intermediate representations provides a practical mechanism for improving both robustness
and inference efficiency in mathematical reasoning, particularly in noisy, low-resource settings.

2 Related Work
Chain-of-Thought Reasoning CoT prompting (Wei et al., 2022) enables large language
models (LLMs) to generate intermediate reasoning steps, substantially improving performance
on mathematical word problems. Subsequent
variants such as self-consistency (Wang et al.,
2023), and least-to-most prompting (Zhou et al.,
2023) further enhance reasoning accuracy. In multilingual and low-resource settings, recent studies
report that CoT-based prompting remains a strong
baseline for Bangla mathematical reasoning
benchmarks (Ahmed et al., 2025).
Reasoning Robustness Prior work on mathematical problem solving has shown that, while
CoT prompting can improve accuracy on math
word problems, the resulting step-by-step rationales are often brittle and not reliably indicative
of the model’s underlying computation - models
may produce convincing but unfaithful explanations and still fail at exact or algorithmic calculation (Turpin et al., 2023; Sprague et al., 2024).
More closely related are studies showing that irrelevant or misleading context can significantly degrade model performance in question answering

and reasoning tasks (Shi et al., 2023; Roy Dipta
and Ferraro, 2025; Nazi et al., 2026).
Structured Reasoning Program-Aided Language Models (PAL) (Gao et al., 2023) and
Program-of-Thoughts (PoT) (Chen et al., 2022)
translate reasoning into executable programs,
offloading computation to external interpreters
such as Python. While effective for numerical
accuracy, these methods generate code sequentially and treat all input tokens uniformly, offering
limited mechanisms to isolate computationally
irrelevant context. Related work on graph-based
reasoning and planning (Yao et al., 2023; Besta
et al., 2024) focuses primarily on knowledge
graphs or abstract decision processes rather than
executable computational structures for math. In
particular, Graph-of-Thoughts (Besta et al., 2024)
models reasoning as a graph over alternative
thought strategies explored via multiple LLM
calls (∼6 per problem), whereas †DAGGER generates a single executable computational graph
in one call, with explicit distractor annotations
absent from all prior approaches. Particularly,
†DAGGER generates executable computational
graphs with dedicated modeling of distractor
nodes, enabling structural separation between
relevant computation and irrelevant information
prior to execution.
Bangla Mathematical Reasoning MGSM (Shi
et al., 2022) and MSVAMP (Patel et al., 2021)
provide multilingual benchmarks for mathematical reasoning, and recent Bangla work builds
instruction-tuned models (Zehady et al., 2026) and
a curriculum-GRPO math reasoner (Dipta et al.,
2026b) evaluated on their Bangla splits. We extend
these benchmarks with systematic distractor augmentation enabling controlled evaluation of reasoning robustness under semantically plausible but
computationally irrelevant context in low-resource
language (Bangla).

Related Entity Distractors (RED) augments
problems with numerical information about the
same object type as the query target, but associated
with different entities or contexts. These sentences

Orthogonal Attribute Distractors (OAD)
adds supplementary properties to objects already

queried attribute. This tests whether models can

the solution when multiple properties of identical

Null-Effect Event Distractors (NEED) introduces descriptions of events involving the problem’s main entities that have zero net impact on

Design Principles and Ecological Validity Our
distractors are structurally anchored to the original problem unlike prior work that evaluates robustness using randomly inserted or semantically
unrelated sentences (Shi et al., 2023). As illustrated in Table 1, each distractor sentence is
grounded in existing entities, subjects, or events
from the problem. This closely aligns with how
real-world math problems naturally contain plausible but computationally irrelevant information.
This ensures naturalness of the augmented problems: because distractors reference existing entities, events, and attributes rather than introducing unrelated context, they are semantically integrated into the problem and cannot be filtered
through surface-level heuristics, unlike randomly
appended irrelevant sentences. To preserve problem integrity, our generation pipeline uses structured JSON prompting (Apps. A.10) that instructs
the model to produce only candidate distractor
sentences with specified insertion positions, rather
than paraphrasing or rewriting the original problem. This ensures the core problem text and its
solution remain untouched while the augmented
problem reads as a natural, coherent Bangla math
problem. Full examples across both MGSM and
MSVAMP for all three distractor types are provided in Table A.11.

4.1 Computational Graph Framework
CoT prompting and code generation (PoT/PAL)
have no mechanism in their output to distinguish relevant quantities from distractors - distractors can be silently incorporated without the
model ever committing to their relevance (Section 6). Reasoning-specialized models reduce this
through extended computation, and their traces
have been shown to encode implicit graph-like
structures (Minegishi et al., 2025). We propose
making this structure explicit: the model constructs an executable directed acyclic graph that
assigns each identified quantity a relevance annotation, followed by deterministic execution over only
the relevant subgraph.
We reformulate mathematical word problem
solving as a structured graph generation task.
Given a problem statement P, our approach instructs a language model to construct a directed
acyclic graph G = (V, E) (prompt in A.10.5).
Each node v is assigned a unique identifier and
includes a boolean attribute distractor(v) that
indicates whether the node contributes to the solution path. Edges e ∈ E encode data dependencies,
where an edge from node u to v indicates that v
consumes the output of u as input. The graph structure enforces a topological ordering that enables
deterministic execution.
A distinguished node vfinal ∈ V is designated
as the final result, representing the problem’s solution. Graph execution proceeds through recursive computation: starting from vfinal , we traverse
backwards through dependencies, computing each
node’s value from its inputs until all constant
nodes are reached (Geh and Mauá, 2021). This
execution model provides verifiable correctness
since each computational step is explicitly represented and can be independently validated (A.8).
The graph representation offers several advantages over sequential reasoning chains. The explicit distractor annotation enables the model
to identify irrelevant numerical information during
construction, preventing error propagation. The
structured format separates problem understand-

4.2

Training Pipeline

We leverage GPT-4.1 as a teacher model to generate computational graph representations for training. For SFT, we curate 3K examples: 1.5K from
the SOMADHAN dataset and 1.5K from numinamath-cot-bn (Paul et al., 2025; LI et al., 2024; Rahman, 2024) without any distractor augmentation.
Each problem is processed by GPT-4.1 to generate structured graph outputs, which are subsequently executed using our graph interpreter (Algorithm A.8 in Apps.) to verify computational correctness. Only verified samples are retained for
training. For GRPO, we use 3K questions from
numina-math-cot-bn without pre-generated solutions, allowing the model to explore multiple solution paths during training. The GRPO reward function R(g, y) for a generated graph g and ground

truth y is defined as
R(g, y) = 0.5 Ifmt + 0.5 Iexec + Iacc (exec(g), y) ,
where Ifmt , Iexec , and Iacc are indicator functions
corresponding to (i) a valid JSON schema with at
least three nodes2 , (ii) successful topological execution, and (iii) an exact numerical match between
the executed graph output and y, respectively. A
natural concern with GRPO training is reward
hacking (Guo et al., 2025; Dipta et al., 2026a,c)
- whether the model can achieve high reward without genuine reasoning. Our reward design mitigates this: Iacc is computed by executing the generated graph, so the model cannot reach the correct
answer without first producing a structurally valid
and executable computational path. A model that
satisfies format and execution checks but produces
incorrect answers receives at most 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0
out of a maximum reward of 2.0, providing limited learning signal without genuine reasoning improvement. We verify that GRPO does not collapse graphs into trivial structures through reward
hacking in Appendix A.4.
2
This constraint prevents models from taking shortcuts by
answering the question with a single node.

We structure training as SFT followed by GRPO
because they address complementary bottlenecks.
SFT requires ground-truth graph annotations from
a teacher model, limiting scale to 3K examples,
but teaches the model to produce well-formed executable graphs. GRPO needs only final numerical
answers, allowing exploration of diverse solution
paths through multi-sample generation - but converges poorly without a strong initialization (Dipta
et al., 2026b). SFT therefore provides the structural initialization that allows GRPO to focus on
reasoning quality - an effect confirmed by the ablation in Appendix A.2, where SFT→GRPO consistently outperforms Base→GRPO.

split, accuracy drop (∆ = original − augmented) as

CoT Prompting Fails Catastrophically Standard 5-shot CoT prompting shows severe degradation under distractors: accuracy drops up to 40.7
points, with Qwen 2.5 (3B) falling from 53.7% to
13.0% on MSVAMP. LLaMA 3 8B shows a notably small drop of 3.2 points on MGSM, likely
attributable to its already low baseline (26.4%).
Figure 3 reveals vulnerabilities across all distractor categories. Related Entity Distractors (RED)
are most disruptive (46–94% error rate), indicating
persistent difficulties in entity-level disambiguation: models cannot reliably distinguish quantities
about the focal entity from those about peripheral
entities. Orthogonal Attribute (24–81%) and NullEffect (27–86%) distractors also cause substantial
errors, confirming that the vulnerability is systematic rather than category-specific.
Reasoning Models: Robust but Expensive
Reasoning-specialized models perform substantially better: Qwen 3-8B achieves 71.4% weighted
accuracy, the highest among all evaluated models.
However, they still degrade 14.2–19.9 points and
consume ∼5.2× more tokens than standard models (3,128 vs. ∼600 tokens per problem). This
establishes a key tension: achieving distractor robustness through extended reasoning comes at significant computational cost, motivating the search
for more efficient alternatives, particularly for lowresource languages.

Token Scaling with Computational
Complexity

We investigate whether output length in
†DAGGER scales with problem complexity
rather than explanation verbosity. We define
complexity as |Vop |, the number of operation
nodes (excluding const and identity) in the
ground-truth computational graph produced by
the teacher model. This directly reflects how many
arithmetic steps are needed to solve a problem.
We compare †DAGGER (Gemma-3-12B,
SFT→GRPO) against Qwen-3-8B Reasoning on
MSVAMP-BN, both on original and distractoraugmented splits. Each baseline output is assigned
the |Vop | of its corresponding ground-truth graph.

The analysis is restricted to correctly solved
instances (Figures 4a and 4b).
On the original split, †DAGGER output length
correlates well with |Vop | (R2 =0.600), staying in
the hundreds of tokens with low variance. Qwen3-8B shows almost no correlation (R2 =0.015),
spanning thousands of tokens regardless of complexity. Under distractor augmentation, the pattern persists: †DAGGER maintains meaningful
correlation (R2 =0.430) while the reasoning baseline remains near-zero (R2 =0.005). These results
show that graph-output length is associated with
the number of operation nodes in the same generated graph, whereas free-form reasoning length is
largely independent of problem complexity.

7 Discussion
Standard CoT prompting is brittle under distractors, dropping up to 40.7 points on Bangla MWPs
across all distractor categories (Figure 3). Reasoning models reduce this gap but still degrade 14–20
points at ∼9× the token cost.
†DAGGER addresses this by forcing explicit dependency commitments through the DAG structure, preventing distractors from entering the solution path, while distractor annotations allow acknowledging irrelevant quantities without incorporating them. This robustness emerges without distractor-augmented training (0.21 distractor nodes in training vs. 2.7 at evaluation), suggesting the graph structure itself induces filtering. Output length tracks computational complexity (R2 =0.600 vs. R2 =0.015 for free-form reasoning), yielding 89% fewer tokens at comparable accuracy.
Related Entity Distractors remain hardest across
all models (Figure 3), suggesting entity-level disambiguation may require richer representations beyond node-level annotations.

Conclusion

We introduce DISTRACTMATH-BN, revealing that
standard CoT prompting fails catastrophically under distractors while reasoning-specialized models
achieve robustness at prohibitive computational
cost. †DAGGER demonstrates that structured
computational graph generation enables comparable accuracy with substantially improved token efficiency. Critically, this robustness emerges without explicit distractor training. Our findings suggest structured representations provide a practical pathway for robust mathematical reasoning in
noisy low-resource settings, though entity-level semantic disambiguation remains an open challenge.

Limitations
Our systematic distractor generation employs
JSON-structured outputs to ensure semantic consistency, defining distractors operationally as numerical values not reached during backward graph
traversal. However, our benchmark is limited to
arithmetic word problems in Bangla with only
three distractor categories (Related Entity, Orthogonal Attribute, and Null-Effect Event) and does
not address non-numeric distractors such as irrelevant textual descriptions. The quality assurance
process relied on review by a single native Banglaspeaking PhD researcher, which may introduce individual judgment biases.
Our computational graph framework assumes
problems can be decomposed into directed acyclic
graphs with atomic operations, which may not
extend to geometric reasoning, algebraic manipulation, or mathematical proof. It is designed
for deterministic solutions and cannot represent
probabilistic reasoning or problems with multiple
valid solution paths. The complexity of generating
well-formed graphs creates practical capacity requirements, suggesting an empirical lower bound
on model size for effective performance, though
smaller models still show improvements over baselines.
Our approach demonstrates benefits even without supervised fine-tuning, though combining SFT
with GRPO yields optimal performance. The SFT
phase creates a dependency on constructing highquality initial training examples, which may limit
scalability to resource-intensive annotation scenarios. While we demonstrate improvements for frontier models like GPT-4.1 in few-shot settings, our
fine-tuning experiments are limited to the Gemma

model family and may require adaptation for other
architectures. The cross-lingual evaluation on four
languages used limited test sets without distractor augmentation, providing preliminary evidence
for transferability. Our evaluation relies on exact
numeric match, which may not capture reasoning
quality or penalize minor arithmetic errors proportionately. Finally, this work focuses on elementary
arithmetic word problems and should not be generalized to advanced mathematical reasoning including algebra, geometry, or calculus.

Ethics Statement
This work addresses the gap in NLP capabilities
for Bangla, a widely spoken but underrepresented
language in language technology. We have made
DISTRACTMATH-BN, code, and prompts publicly
available. Dataset construction relies in part on a
proprietary large language model, which may introduce biases or stylistic artifacts specific to that
model and should be considered when interpreting
results or extending the benchmark. Our method
is designed to improve mathematical reasoning in
educational technology and low-resource language
support tools. While the structured representation
improves interpretability, responsible deployment
requires human oversight, particularly in educational settings.

Reward Component Breakdown After SFT,

Reward Hacking Prevention The compositional reward design imposes several structural
constraints that limit reward exploitation. Format compliance requires valid JSON with at least
3 nodes, ruling out degenerate single-operation
graphs. Execution success requires all node dependencies to resolve during topological traversal
- referencing a nonexistent dependency causes execution failure and zero execution reward. The
accuracy reward evaluates the numerical output
of graph execution against ground truth, not
the graph structure itself: a graph that is wellformatted and executes successfully but computes
the wrong result receives zero accuracy reward.
Combined with the empirical evidence above - stable graph complexity and gains concentrated in accuracy - these constraints prevent reward hacking
and improve genuine reasoning and distractor identification capabilities.

length of 4096 tokens. During inference, we allow
up to 2048 output tokens to accommodate multistep reasoning traces. Unless otherwise specified,
all remaining decoding and runtime hyperparameters are kept at their default values.
For reasoning-specialized models, we employ a
larger context window to support longer reasoning traces. Specifically, models are loaded with a
maximum sequence length of 8192 tokens in BF16
precision. To prevent premature truncation of extended reasoning chains, we allow up to 7168 output tokens during generation. All other inferencetime settings follow the default configuration of the
respective models.
For DAGGER-style inference without training,
we use the same default inference setup as above

A.6.2 Group Relative Policy Optimization
We train the SFT checkpoints using GRPO with
a structured reward function that assigns +0.5
for valid JSON graph format, +0.5 for successful execution, and +1.0 for correct answers (maximum 2.0 per generation). We employ GRPO with
BNPO loss as it is specifically designed for tasks
with verifiable outputs and discrete reward signals,
making it well-suited for computational graph generation where correctness can be deterministically
evaluated. The group-based policy optimization
allows efficient comparison across multiple generated graphs per prompt, enabling the model to
learn from relative quality differences. BNPO’s binary normalization is particularly effective for our

structured three-tier reward system (format validation, execution success, and answer correctness).
For each prompt, we generate 8 completions using VLLM with top-p sampling (0.95) and top-k
(64). The training uses BNPO loss with β = 0.0,
epsilon clipping at 0.2-0.28, and temperature 1.0.
We employ LoRA rank 64 with global batch size
32 over 4 epochs. The learning rate follows cosine
scheduling from 1 × 10−5 to 1 × 10−6 with 20
warmup steps.
