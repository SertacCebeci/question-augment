Work to date has mainly relied on semantic
proximity to identify relevant content for lifelong personalization. However, situational relevance is often more important for determining which information is useful for a user’s
actual task and context. In this paper, we introduce the Proximity Advantage (PA) score,
a metric for quantifying semantic proximity
bias, and show that existing personalization
benchmarks largely conflate semantic and situational proximity, leaving it unclear whether
current systems truly capture situational relevance. To support this metric, we introduce
LUCid, a diagnostic benchmark of 1,936 user
queries paired with long interaction histories,
designed to isolate situational relevance from
semantic proximity. Our experiments across
different stages of the modern personalization
pipeline (retrieval, reranking, and generation)
reveal significant performance collapse: retrieval recall drops to near zero on the hardest
instances, and response alignment remains near
50% even for state-of-the-art models such as
Gemini-3-Flash, GPT-5.4, and Claude Haiku,
highlighting a fundamental mismatch between
the relevance encoded by current systems and

Given the cost and context-window limitations
of full-history conditioning (Wu et al., 2025; Zhao
et al., 2025), retrieval-based methods such as RAG
and its advanced variants (Peng et al., 2024; Tan
et al., 2025b; Tang et al., 2026) have become the
dominant paradigm for scalable lifelong personalization. At the core of these methods is the identification of relevant context, which they operationalize via semantic similarity scoring functions.
Information retrieval theory, however, establishes
relevance as multifaceted, extending beyond semantic similarity. In particular, situational relevance
defines information as relevant if it helps accomplish a user’s task given their goals and context,
regardless of its semantic or topical overlap with
the current query (Saracevic, 1975; Borlund, 2003).

Introduction

People vary widely in their goals, preferences, and
contexts, even within the same task or prompt
(Sorensen et al., 2024). As they interact with LLMpowered conversational systems, they generate rich,
evolving interaction histories that reflect these individual differences (Zhao et al., 2025; Wu et al.,
2025). Effective lifelong personalization therefore
requires models to reason over this history; without
it, conversational agents default to generic knowledge, producing responses that are not only unhelpful (Jandaghi et al., 2024) but potentially unsafe

view, lifelong personalization is fundamentally a
problem of identifying and conditioning on situationally relevant user context.
This perspective therefore prompts re-examining
lifelong personalization systems for the notion of
relevance they encode. Existing benchmarks, however, make this difficult: we show via a new metric,
Proximity Advantage (PA), that they largely conflate situational relevance with semantic proximity
(i.e., they mostly comprise evaluation instances
where the relevant context shares substantial semantic overlap with query relative to the rest of
the interaction history). Consequently, strong performance on these benchmarks does not necessarily indicate that systems have learned to retrieve
situationally relevant user context. To this end,
we operationalize situational relevance through latent user context—user-specific information embedded within semantically distant sessions yet
necessary to produce a correct personalized response—and introduce the Latent User Context
benchmark (LUCid), explicitly designed to decouple semantic proximity from situational relevance.
LUCid comprises 1,936 realistic user queries
whose correct responses hinge on user-specific
attributes – including domain affiliation, cultural/geographic context, age group, religion,
health constraints, and general preferences – shared
within semantically distant sessions of a lifelong interaction history of up to 500 sessions (620K tokens). Instances require either single-session inference, where a latent attribute must be inferred from
one session, or multi-session reasoning, where evidence must be aggregated across conversations.
By design, LUCid forces systems to identify and
reason over situationally relevant user context.
Using LUCid, we ask: What notion of relevance
do current lifelong personalization systems encode,
and can they reliably identify and utilize latent user
context? Our experiments across the key components of modern personalization systems (retrieval,
reranking, and generation) show that existing systems largely encode relevance as semantic proximity, suffering significant performance collapse
when user context must be inferred from semantically distant interaction history. Retrieval recall
drops to near zero on hard instances, and response
alignment fails to exceed 50% even for state-ofthe-art models such as Gemini-3-Flash, GPT-5.4,

and Claude Haiku. We further provide a theoretical
account that links the observed retrieval failures
to data-induced biases in learned representations.
These findings expose a fundamental gap between
the relevance current systems capture and what true
lifelong personalization demands.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
(1) Grounded in Information Retrieval theory, we
motivate situational relevance as the appropriate
notion of relevance for lifelong personalization and
operationalize it through latent user context; (2) We
introduce the Proximity Advantage (PA) score, a
simple reusable metric for quantifying proximity
bias in personalization benchmarks, and show that
existing benchmarks are largely solvable via semantic retrieval; (3) We introduce LUCid, a diagnostic
benchmark comprising 1,936 queries paired with
lifelong interaction histories of up to 500 sessions
(620K tokens), explicitly designed to evaluate situational relevance in lifelong personalization; and (4)
Our findings expose a fundamental gap between the
relevance encoded by current personalization systems and what lifelong personalization demands.

Related Work

Methods for Lifelong Personalization. Current
approaches fall into three paradigms. Long-context
methods directly condition on full interaction histories (Zhao et al., 2025; An et al., 2024; Fu et al.,
2024), but face computational bottlenecks and
needle-in-a-haystack failures (Hsieh et al., 2024).
RAG-based methods (Lewis et al., 2020) address
this by retrieving the top-k semantically similar
sessions, with extensions such as GraphRAG (Han
et al., 2025; Edge et al., 2025) enabling multi-hop
reasoning over structured entity-relation graphs.
Memory-augmented systems (Tan et al., 2025b;
Packer et al., 2024; Chhikara et al., 2025; Zhou
and Han, 2025; Tang et al., 2026) further compress
histories into queryable memory banks. We evaluate LUCid across a representative set of these approaches as well as provide a theoretical account of
their structural limitations (Section 6; Appendix B).
Benchmarks for Lifelong Personalization.
Benchmarks for lifelong personalization have
progressively expanded in scope and realism.
Early work such as LaMP (Salemi et al., 2024)
and LongLaMP (Kumar et al., 2024) introduced
personalized language modeling and long-form
generation, while LoCoMo (Maharana et al.,
2024) evaluates long-term conversational memory.
2

LoCoMo-plus (Li et al., 2026) further extends this
setting by introducing cognitive memory tasks
that require models to reason over constraints
established earlier in a conversation. More recent
benchmarks focus on dynamic user modeling over
extended interaction histories: LongMemEval
(Wu et al., 2025) probes multiple memory
abilities across sessions, PrefEval (Zhao et al.,
2025) evaluates explicit and implicit preference
following, PersonaMem (Jiang et al., 2025b,a)
studies evolving user profiles, PersonaBench (Tan
et al., 2025a) assesses understanding of private
user information, and ImplexConv (Li et al., 2025)
introduces semantically diverse multi-session
reasoning. These benchmarks have advanced
lifelong personalization by evaluating complementary memory and personalization capabilities.
However, they do not explicitly evaluate the notion
of relevance underlying successful personalization.
In contrast, LUCid is a diagnostic benchmark that
isolates the notion of relevance encoded by lifelong
personalization systems (see Appendix A for an
extended discussion and benchmark comparison).

Lifelong Personalization: Problem
Setting

Let H = {s1 , s2 , . . . , sn } be a lifelong interaction
history consisting of n sessions, and let qn+1 be a
new user query. A personalization system aims to
generate a response y by conditioning on a subset
of the history: P (y | qn+1 , ϕ(H)), where ϕ is a
selection or compression function. In modern personalization systems, at the core of ϕ is a retrieval
module that selects a subset of historical context
with respect to the current query via a similarity
scoring function over their embedding representations. The retrieved context is often further ranked
by a reranking module before being passed to a
language model for generation (Tan et al., 2025b).

reason about situational relevance that goes beyond semantic proximity. Following Section 3,
each evaluation instance in LUCid is defined as
(H, qn+1 , A∗ ), where A∗ denotes the latent user
attribute that must be inferred from the interaction history to produce the correct personalized

Constructing LUCid

We construct LUCid through a multi-stage pipeline
that mirrors everyday conversational dynamics.
Our pipeline consists of four stages: (1) Query
synthesis, (2) Relevant session construction, (3)
Irrelevant session curation, and (4) Interaction history aggregation.

For each dimension-value pair (e.g., age-group:
teen), we construct a group of sessions spanning
diverse topics, each revealing the same latent attribute through indirect signals: a user’s age, for
instance, may surface across sessions about generational events. Some instances require multi-session
reasoning, where no single session suffices: a user
who consistently resists formal responses signals a
preference for casual communication; one whose
sessions span compilers, data mining, and tokenization plausibly indicates a computer science affiliation. This mirrors organic personalization, where
user identity emerges gradually across a history
rather than through isolated disclosures.

Query Synthesis. We first identify a set of
user-specific dimensions along which a correct
personalized response varies meaningfully (Table 2). Guided by these dimensions, we synthesize queries using three complementary strategies:
(1) extraction and adaptation of naturally occurring user queries from real-world conversational
logs such as UltraChat and ShareGPT (Ding et al.,
2023; Ouyang et al., 2023) (N=125); (2) adoption or light modification of queries from existing datasets aligned to specific dimensions, including SituatedQA (Zhang and Choi, 2021) for geographic and location-aware queries (N=273), PersonaMem (Jiang et al., 2025b) for health-related
queries (N=561), PrefEval (Zhao et al., 2025)
for movie recommendation queries (N=75), and
SafeBench (Ying et al., 2024) and CASE-Bench
(Sun et al., 2025) for safety-critical age-sensitive
queries (N=43); and (3) manual writing of queries
for dimensions not covered by existing sources
(N=859). All queries are manually inspected to ensure (i) clarity, (ii) naturalness, and (iii) presence of
personalization cues that signal a user’s expectation
of a tailored response.

Irrelevant Session Construction. Irrelevant sessions serve as distractors: they contain no personally attributable user information and neither contribute latent context nor interact with or contradict
signals from relevant sessions. We manually inspect over 10,000 sessions from UltraChat (Ding
et al., 2023) and ShareGPT (Ouyang et al., 2023)
and retain approximately 700 that satisfy this criterion, predominantly general knowledge-seeking
or curiosity-driven conversations spanning diverse
topics (e.g., wildlife behavior, social phenomena).

Relevant Session Construction. Relevant sessions are those comprising the latent user context
CL for a given query (Section 4). Drawing on
conversational logs from WildChat (Zhao et al.,
2024), UltraChat (Ding et al., 2023) and ShareGPT
(Ouyang et al., 2023) to inform structure and linguistic style, sessions are sourced via three methods: (1) direct extraction from logs where a session
naturally surfaces a target attribute; (2) dimensionvalue translation, where an extracted session is

Final Benchmark and Release. To accommodate varying computational constraints and research objectives, we introduce four benchmark
variants: LUCid-C (controlled reranking probe,
30 sessions, ∼47K tokens), LUCid-S (Small, 50
sessions, ∼64K tokens), LUCid-B (Base, 200 sessions, ∼270K tokens), and LUCid-L (Large, 500
sessions, ∼620K tokens), with the latter providing
a high-difficulty setting to push the boundaries of
long-context reasoning. Across all variants, the
benchmark consists of 1,936 evaluation queries,

reflect the dynamics of conversational logs and a
ground-truth latent attribute A∗ . We further define

Conclusion

Grounded in information retrieval theory, we show
that situational relevance, not semantic similarity,
is the appropriate notion of relevance for lifelong
personalization. We show that existing benchmarks largely conflate the two, motivating LUCid,
a benchmark that explicitly decouples situational
relevance from semantic proximity. Our experiments reveal a two-stage bottleneck: at retrieval,
all systems—spanning RAG, memory-augmented
architectures, and rerankers—collapse to near-zero
recall on hard instances; at generation, state-ofthe-art models (Gemini, GPT, Claude, Qwen) still
struggle to condition on latent attributes even when
the relevant session is directly provided. These findings expose a fundamental mismatch between the
relevance current systems encode and what lifelong
personalization demands, motivating a shift toward
situational relevance at both retrieval and generation. We offer LUCid as a step toward that realignment, and open-source our curation pipeline, data,
and code to facilitate future research in this area
(https://github.com/MichiganNLP/LUCid).

Extended Related Work

A.1

Current Methods for Lifelong
Personalization

Current methods for lifelong personalization can
be broadly categorized into three paradigms: long
context methods, Retrieval-Augmented Generation
(RAG), and augmented memory methods. Long
context methods directly input extensive user histories into models’ context windows (Zhao et al.,
2025; An et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024); however,
scaling user history introduces significant computational bottlenecks and leads to well-documented
“needle-in-a-haystack” failures (Hsieh et al., 2024).
To mitigate context limitations, RAG (Lewis et al.,
2020) methods condition the model’s responses
on only the top-k most semantically relevant user
sessions. Extensions such as GraphRAG (Han
et al., 2025; Edge et al., 2025; Han et al., 2026;
Xiang et al., 2026) organize interaction histories
into structured entity–relation graphs to enable
multi-hop reasoning across sessions. Memoryaugmented systems (Tan et al., 2025b; Packer et al.,
2024; Chhikara et al., 2025; Zhou and Han, 2025;
Tang et al., 2026) further consolidate histories into
structured, queryable memory units via memory
agents and hierarchical compression. However,
both RAG and GraphRAG approximate relevance
as semantic proximity at the retrieval frontier: flat
RAG scores sessions by embedding-space similarity to the query; GraphRAG encodes proximity
bias into both entry-point selection and in some
implementations graph traversal (Edge et al., 2025;
Peng et al., 2024), causing it to compound at each
hop; and memory-augmented systems surface their
compressed units using same backbone (flat RAG,
GraphRAG, or a dual-stage retrieve-then-rerank
pipeline) thus, preserving the bottleneck on a compressed index rather than eliminating it. We evaluate LUCid across a representative set of these
approaches as well as provide a theoretical account
of their structural limitations in Section 6.1, Appendix B.

Benchmarks for Evaluating Lifelong
Personalization

Benchmarks for lifelong personalization have progressively expanded in scope and realism. Early
work such as LaMP (Salemi et al., 2024) introduced personalized language modeling across diverse NLP tasks, while LongLaMP (Kumar et al.,
2024) extended this to long-form generation. Lo12

CoMo (Maharana et al., 2024) evaluates long-term
conversational memory, and LoCoMo-plus extends
this setting with cognitive memory tasks requiring
reasoning over constraints established earlier in a
dialogue. PersoBench (Afzoon et al., 2026) evaluates response-level personalization using personaaware datasets. More recent benchmarks target
long-horizon and dynamic user modeling: LongMemEval (Wu et al., 2025) probes five core memory abilities across multi-session histories; PrefEval (Zhao et al., 2025) evaluates explicit and implicit preference following in contexts up to 100K
tokens; PersonaMem (Jiang et al., 2025b,a) emphasizes tracking evolving user profiles across temporally grounded sessions; and PersonaBench (Tan
et al., 2025a) tests understanding of personal information derived from synthetic private user documents; and ImplexConv (Li et al., 2025) introduces
implicit reasoning over semantically diverse multisession histories. Collectively, these benchmarks
have substantially advanced lifelong personalization by evaluating complementary memory and
personalization capabilities. Although some deliberately reduce semantic similarity between the
query and the relevant evidence (Zhao et al., 2025;
Li et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025b,a), they do not
control the relative similarity of the remaining interaction history. As a result, strong performance
may still arise from semantic shortcuts, leaving
the notion of relevance underlying successful personalization untested. LUCid therefore serves as
a diagnostic benchmark that explicitly decouples
semantic proximity from situational relevance, enabling direct evaluation of the notion of relevance
encoded by personalization systems. We summarize a detailed comparison of existing benchmarks
and LUCid in Table 6, including history length,

Beyond Standard RAG

Several works augment standard RAG to address
its granularity and structural limitations. Memoryaugmented systems (Packer et al., 2024; Chhikara
et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2025b) compress interaction histories into structured, queryable memory
units; however, these units are still surfaced at
query time via embedding-space similarity search
using stardard retrievers such as Contriever, preserving the proximity identified in Section 6.1 bottleneck on a compressed index rather than eliminating it. Graph-augmented systems (Han et al.,
2025; Edge et al., 2025; Han et al., 2026; Xiang
et al., 2026) organize history into entity-relation
graphs to enable multi-hop reasoning across sessions. However, both entry-point node selection
and, in some implementations, graph traversal are
guided by similarity-based scoring function (Edge
et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2024), causing proximity
bias to compound at each hop. These augmentations thus share the same structural failure: without
a richer notion of relevance at the retrieval frontier,
latent user context remains systematically inaccessible.

Section 6.1 uses Contriever as an illustrative example to motivate how retrieval training data and sampling strategies can operationalize relevance primarily through semantic proximity. However, the
empirical results in Table 9 show that stronger and
supervised dense retrievers exhibit similar degradation on LUCid. We briefly discuss why this behavior may persist despite differences in architecture,
supervision, and instruction tuning.
Many modern dense retrieval pipelines continue
to rely on training data where relevance is strongly
correlated with semantic similarity. In supervised
datasets such as MS MARCO, positive passages
are typically highly semantically related to the
query, encouraging models to associate semantic
similarity with relevance. Furthermore, because
true hard negatives are relatively scarce, many retrievers—including models trained with synthetic
positives such as E5-Mistral—still rely heavily on
in-batch negatives, which are predominantly semantically unrelated to the query. Although training procedures differ substantially across retrievers,
these design choices encourage embedding spaces
in which semantic proximity remains the dominant
retrieval signal.
Beyond the training objective itself, dense retrievers are also known to generalize poorly outside
the distributions on which they are trained (Thakur
et al., 2021; Izacard et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022).
LUCid intentionally evaluates a setting where situational relevance is decoupled from topical similarity, placing it outside the assumptions encoded
by many existing retrieval benchmarks. We hypothesize that these factors together help explain
why stronger retrievers continue to exhibit similar
failure modes on LUCid despite improvements on
conventional retrieval benchmarks.

under Oracle conditioning to 0.475 on LUCidB, approaching the no-context baseline of 0.461,
suggesting performance convergence towards nocontext baseline as scale increases. Notably, the
sharp drop from Oracle → LUCid-5 (0.744 →
0.603) suggests that even a relatively small number
of distractor contexts can already shift the model’s
focus away from the relevant latent user information.

WEIRD Assumptions Under Ambiguity

In the absence of user-specific context (No Retrieval setting), we analyze how models respond to
location-sensitive queries. As shown in Figure 12,
models consistently produce U.S.- or Westerncentric responses rather than acknowledging geographic ambiguity or eliciting missing context.
Across all models, the majority of responses assume a Western context, with non-Western locations accounting for a small fraction of outputs
despite representing a significant portion of the
world’s population.

biases documented in LLMs (Mihalcea et al.,

Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic assumptions
