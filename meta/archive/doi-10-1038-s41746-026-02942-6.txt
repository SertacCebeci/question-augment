Abstract

A

TI

C

S

While multimodal large language models (LLMs) demonstrate significant potential in
healthcare applications, their clinical utility is difficult to appraise. Current evaluations of
medical-assisting LLMs are often limited by sparse human expertise, narrow specialty scope,
and reliance on multiple-choice benchmarks or synthetic vignettes, which can inflate
performance and obscure clinical utility. We conducted a multicenter, multidisciplinary
study in which more than 400 physicians—spanning seven specialties, varied experience
levels, and multiple geographic settings—evaluated LLM-generated free-text responses to
real, de-identified clinical cases. In a matched-control design, we also deployed an equivalent
number of AI agents configured to mirror physician characteristics to examine whether
automated evaluators can supplement or replace human assessment. Our results
demonstrated that physician assessments exhibited substantial heterogeneity by clinical
seniority and practice environment, leading to notable shifts in relative model rankings
across cohorts. While AI agents delivered highly efficient, directionally aligned assessments,
they did not fully capture the nuances of human clinical judgment and could not substitute
for physician-centered evaluation. Instead, they promise assistive tools that can triage or prescreen outputs to reduce human burden.

Introduction

IN

Generative artificial intelligence (AI), particularly multimodal large language models (LLMs)
capable of processing and generating text, image, and other data modalities, has accelerated
adoption of AI in healthcare1,2, moving beyond the traditional predictive analytics toward assistantlike systems that interact with clinicians and patients3. These models hold great promise as
transformative tools in the medical field4,5, ranging from automating administrative tasks to
augmenting clinical decision making6–8. However, rigorous evaluation has revealed fundamental
limitations that must be addressed before their widespread and safe clinical deployment9–13. To
date, medical evaluation of LLMs has centered largely on medical question answering (MQA),
leveraging standardized multiple-choice benchmarks such as MedQA (USMLE) and PubMedQA
to quantify knowledge14,15. Remarkably, 84.2% of the research in this field is dedicated to refining
QA capabilities13. This paradigm enables rapid, low-cost model comparisons but departs from
clinical reality. Multiple-choice tests do not reflect how clinicians synthesize unstructured
information into free-text assessments, differentials, and management plans. They can overstate
performance, obscure reasoning deficits, and miss safety-critical errors16–20. As LLMs approach
high-stakes use, evaluations should prioritize free-response performance on real, de-identified
clinical cases.

Concurrently, there is growing recognition that evaluations should be human-centric. Physicians
bring experiential knowledge, contextual judgment, and risk calculus that current automated
metrics and proxy evaluators often fail to capture6,21–25. Yet large-scale human evaluation faces
practical barriers: constrained clinician availability, the diversity of specialties and care settings,
and heterogeneity in training and clinical priorities26–30. This leaves the crucial task of selecting
and standardizing expert evaluators an unresolved challenge.
To address the scalability constraints, some have proposed using autonomous AI agents as proxy
evaluators31. While agents can be efficient and have shown utility in other domains32,33, it remains
unclear whether they can reliably emulate the nuanced assessments of practicing clinicians—
especially for free-text clinical reasoning, safety appraisal, and contextual appropriateness.
Overreliance on automated judges’ risks mischaracterizing model readiness and overlooking
safety-relevant failure modes.
In this study, we aimed to determine whether AI agents can approximate physician-centered,
multidimensional evaluation of large language model (LLM)-generated clinical case
interpretations under realistic clinical conditions (see Fig. 1). To address this question, we
conducted a multicenter evaluation involving more than 400 physicians across seven specialties
and diverse practice environments. In parallel, we deployed a matched cohort of AI agents
configured to simulate physician roles. Secondary exploratory analyses examined whether
evaluation patterns varied across practice environments and assessed comparative time efficiency
between human and agent evaluators. Instead of multiple-choice questions, we use real, de-

To characterize physician-centered evaluation under realistic clinical conditions, we first analyzed
physician rankings of five LLM-generated interpretations across six predefined quality dimensions.
Physicians exhibited systematic, structured evaluation patterns, with preferences that varied by
clinical seniority (Fig. 2). This experience-related variability indicates that physician evaluation is
not monolithic and underscores why approximating physician-centered assessment is a non-trivial
target for automated evaluators. Across the six dimensions, junior physicians tended to favor
Qwen-Max (the highest mean score, 51.8, 95% CI 44.5-59.0) consistently, whereas senior
physicians demonstrated more differentiated preferences, frequently assigning higher relative
rankings to GPT-4o and DeepSeek-R1 in dimensions related to coherence, accuracy, and clinical
usefulness (Fig. 2). Notably, the identity of the top-ranked model was not stable across seniority
strata, suggesting that evaluative emphasis shifts with clinical experience rather than reflecting
random dispersion. In contrast, Claude 3.5 and OpenAI o1 were consistently ranked lower across
experience levels, with OpenAI o1 (with mean scores typically below 45) demonstrating
persistently reduced relative scores across multiple dimensions. Collectively, these findings
indicate that clinical seniority shapes multidimensional evaluation priorities and that physiciancentered judgment exhibits structured heterogeneity rather than consensus uniformity. Unless
otherwise specified, analyses reflect n = 421 physicians evaluating 7 cases, with 5 model outputs
per case across six evaluation dimensions.

Discussion
Our study provides evidence on the human-centric evaluation of LLM-generated clinical case
interpretations, revealing two principal findings. First, physician assessments are not monolithic
but are shaped by clinical seniority and institutional context, producing structured heterogeneity
in model rankings. Second, while AI-agent evaluators show broad directional alignment with
physician rankings, they do not replicate the nuanced variation of human clinical judgment and
cannot currently substitute for physician-centered evaluation.
The variability in model rankings across physician seniority groups underscores the complexity of
evaluating LLM-generated clinical case interpretations. Our findings demonstrate that clinical
experience shapes how physicians rank LLM outputs in this case-note evaluation setting. Junior
and senior physicians produced distinct model-ranking patterns across the six evaluation

any physician subgroup should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of alignment with superior
evaluation quality.

that current AI-agent evaluators, configured as single-model instances with identical prompts and
contextual parameters, do not reproduce the structured heterogeneity of physician judgment. The
procedural homogeneity required for experimental control may have further contributed to
convergent agent outputs, although the relative contributions of model architecture, training data,
and prompt design to this consistency were not directly assessed.

simplistic benchmarks. While MedFound34 established a critical precedent for in-depth qualitative
assessment with a panel of six physicians, our study’s scale (N > 400) allows us to characterize a

Within this specific case-note evaluation task, the observed global rank concordance suggests that
AI-agent evaluators may provide a preliminary signal about broad relative model performance.
Whether this directional alignment translates into practical utility for preliminary screening of
LLM-generated case interpretations remains untested and would require prospective, task-specific
validation with safeguards against missing clinically important errors. These findings do not
establish suitability for patient triage, clinical decision-making, treatment recommendation, or
autonomous quality assurance. More broadly, our results underscore that meaningful validation of
LLM-generated clinical case interpretations requires evaluation frameworks that are not only
human-centric but also intentionally diverse, capturing the perspectives of clinicians across
specialties, experience levels, and practice settings.

This study has several limitations. First, although the physician cohort was large (N = 421), the
evaluation was based on seven real-world clinical cases, each corresponding to a specific specialty.
Because each case was evaluated exclusively by physicians within the matching specialty, the
effects of case content and evaluator specialty are confounded and cannot be disentangled without
cross-specialty evaluation of the same case. The observed ranking patterns may therefore be casesensitive and specialty-sensitive, and should be interpreted as evidence from a multicenter,
multidisciplinary case-note evaluation setting rather than as a comprehensive assessment across
all clinical tasks. Second, all cases and evaluations were conducted in Chinese; generalizability to
other languages and clinical documentation systems requires independent validation. Third, the
study compared evaluation patterns across physician seniority groups but did not establish which
group provided the most clinically valid assessments. Senior clinicians may be better positioned
to evaluate certain dimensions whereas junior clinicians may apply different criteria. Closer
alignment between AI-agent evaluators and any physician subgroup should not be interpreted as
evidence of alignment with superior evaluation quality. Future studies should incorporate expert
adjudication, predefined reference standards, or outcome-linked assessments to distinguish
evaluator preference from evaluation quality. Fourth, the ordinal ranking-based scoring captures
relative preference but does not measure absolute quality differences between closely ranked
models. Fifth, all AI agents were powered by a single underlying LLM architecture; agentevaluation findings may not generalize to other agent frameworks or base models. Finally, all

Methods

This study employed a multi-agent, multicenter evaluation framework to compare human
physician and AI agent assessments of LLM-generated medical case interpretations. The design
featured two parallel evaluation arms: a human cohort of 450 physicians, with 421 valid responses
collected, and an AI cohort of 421 evaluator agents. Participation was voluntary and
uncompensated. No financial or material incentives were provided. Both cohorts were tasked with
assessing the quality of medical case reports generated by a standardized set of five highperforming LLMs. The physicians’ collective judgments served as the benchmark for evaluating
the performance and alignment of the AI agents. To generate the medical interpretations for
evaluation, five LLMs were selected, including GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-11-20), Claude 3.5 (claude3-5-sonnet-20240620), Qwen-Max (qwen-max-0919), DeepSeek-R1 (deepseek-r1), and OpenAI
o1 (o1-mini-2024-09-12), all of which consistently exhibited strong performance. To ensure
comparability across models, all LLMs were evaluated under identical input conditions. A
standardized prompt template was applied uniformly across all five models. Each model received
the same case materials in an identical sequence. No model-specific prompt tuning or adaptive
modification was introduced.

We retrospectively compiled a multicenter cohort comprising seven real-world clinical cases
collected between November and December 2024 in China. All case materials were presented in
Chinese, consistent with the primary clinical documentation language of the participating
institutions, thereby preserving ecological validity in evaluator interpretation. Rigorous deidentification protocols were implemented, and patient data contamination was prevented by
excluding any cases previously disseminated online or at academic conferences. For each case, the
definitive diagnosis and reference treatment strategy were established through consensus by a
panel of senior specialists, including multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussions where appropriate.
The final case material provided to human doctors and agents included summarized clinical
records and definitive diagnosis (ground truth) but omitted the established treatment plan to allow
for unbiased assessment of the LLM-generated therapeutic suggestions. For each case, a definitive
diagnosis and reference treatment strategy were established through consensus among senior
specialists to ensure internal clinical validity of the case material. The definitive diagnosis was
provided to both physician and AI-agent evaluators to standardize the clinical context and anchor
assessment of reasoning quality relative to a confirmed ground truth. The reference treatment
strategy, however, was intentionally withheld from evaluators. This design choice was made to
prevent evaluators from anchoring their judgments to a predefined therapeutic plan or assessing
outputs based solely on concordance with that plan. Instead, evaluators were instructed to assess
the quality, coherence, accuracy, and clinical appropriateness of the LLM-generated interpretations
independently. Evaluators were not tasked with generating independent diagnoses but with
assessing the quality of model-generated interpretations relative to a confirmed diagnostic context.

We recruited physicians from eight medical institutions in this evaluation, and they were divided
into different groups based on medical experience: Junior (1-3 years of clinical experience, n=252),
Middle career (4-14 years of clinical experience, n=127), and Senior (over 15 years of clinical
experience, n=42) for comparison. Physicians were recruited from eight medical institutions across
China through institutional collaborations and established professional clinical networks.
Invitations were distributed via departmental communications and specialty-based networks to
licensed physicians actively practicing in relevant clinical disciplines. Eligibility requires active
clinical engagement within the corresponding specialty. Participation was open during the
designated recruitment period and was entirely voluntary. The recruitment strategy was designed
to capture heterogeneous real-world clinical perspectives rather than a selectively incentivized
sample. Before the experiment began, each doctor completed an anonymous questionnaire,
including their background details such as medical title, years of practice in the corresponding
specialty, and the highest academic degree obtained. Participants were assigned to evaluate cases
relevant to their medical specialty. For each clinical case, five LLM-generated interpretations were
produced under identical prompt conditions. Participating physicians evaluated all five
interpretations corresponding to the case within their clinical specialty. To minimize positional
bias, the order of the five model outputs was randomized independently for each evaluator.
Evaluations were completed within a single structured session, during which physicians ranked all
five model outputs across six predefined quality dimensions. Because the five interpretations
referred to the same underlying case and were presented concurrently, judgments were made
comparatively within-case rather than sequentially across separate time points. Case allocation
followed specialty alignment rather than cross-specialty randomization, ensuring that physicians
evaluated cases relevant to their clinical expertise while preserving ecological validity.

A

Physician assessments were conducted in designated evaluation rooms at participating institutions
using a standardized digital interface developed for this study. Evaluations were completed in
person rather than through remote or online submission. Internet access was not available in the
evaluation setting. Participants were instructed not to communicate with other evaluators or
consult external resources during assessment. Each evaluation session was limited to a maximum
duration of 120 minutes, and all model outputs corresponding to a case were assessed
consecutively within the same session. Submissions were finalized at the end of the session and
could not be revised thereafter. Although continuous physical supervision was not implemented,
adherence to instructions relied on professional compliance.
As a parallel evaluation arm, we constructed an agentic evaluation system (see Fig. S1) based on
the CAMEL architecture39. Specifically, each agent participating in the evaluation was assigned a
unified task, analogous to the task of physicians evaluating AI-assisted case interpretations. AI

finalized assessment without subsequent revision. And each agent was provided with configurable

across six dimensions, which were developed with medical specialists following discussion with
reference to previous literature13,34. For each case and for each of the six predefined evaluation
dimensions (comprehensiveness, coherence, accuracy, helpfulness, harmlessness, and structural
quality), evaluators were instructed to rank the five LLM-generated interpretations from highest
to lowest quality. These ordinal rankings were then converted into standardized scores of 100 (rank
1), 75 (rank 2), 50 (rank 3), 25 (rank 4), and 0 (rank 5) to enable quantitative comparison across
evaluators. In addition to mean converted ranking scores, we report the frequency with which each
LLM was assigned first rank within each subgroup (“mentions”), defined as the count of first-rank
assignments across evaluators.
A structured evaluation rubric was provided to physicians, describing the intended meaning of
each dimension to promote consistent interpretation across participants. AI-agent evaluators were
configured to follow the identical ranking framework and score conversion rules. A centralized AI
agent outside the evaluation system then consolidated the results, which were recorded in a
structured spreadsheet for subsequent analysis. Complete information of a case (referred to as
‘Case Information’ in Fig. S1) consists of clinical records, diagnosis, and corresponding LLMgenerated diagnostic reports. Both human and AI evaluators assessed the LLM-generated reports
across six predefined dimensions and results from all evaluators were consolidated into a unified
dataset for analysis. To better resolve the fine details of AI models preference comparison between
AI Agents and human doctors, a higher-magnification image of the area marked in Fig. S1 is
presented in Fig. S2.
To ensure analytical rigor and transparency, prespecified criteria were applied to define a valid
response. A submission was considered valid if it met all of the following conditions:
1. Completeness: all mandatory evaluation fields were fully completed without missing primary
data;
2. Internal Consistency: absence of logically contradictory scoring patterns;
3. Technical Integrity: successful finalization through the study interface without duplication,
corruption, or partial saving.
Responses that were incomplete, structurally inconsistent, or duplicate entries were excluded prior
to analysis. Of 450 physician submissions, 421 met these criteria and were retained for final
statistical analysis.

To evaluate the overall agreement in the rank-ordering of the LLMs between human physicians
and AI agent evaluators, we calculated Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W). This nonparametric statistical test was selected to quantify the degree of global rank concordance across all
five evaluated LLMs and the six assessment dimensions, independent of absolute score magnitudes.
Kendall's Wvalues range from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates no agreement and 1 indicates perfect,
unanimous agreement in rankings between the evaluator groups.
