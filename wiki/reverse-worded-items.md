# Reverse-worded items

Questionnaire designers often flip the direction of about half the items (fitness items among fatigue items) to defeat response bias. Van Sonderen, Sanderman and Coyne (2013) argue the device mostly fails and test it on the 20-item Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI-20), ten items of which are reverse worded, with 700 patients.[^c1]

## Three response styles, not one

The paper separates response set (bias tied to item content, such as social desirability) from response style (answering regardless of content). Three styles matter for reversal: acquiescence (a tendency to agree), inattention (not reading carefully, the satisficing of Krosnick), and confusion (the item is too hard to verify).[^c2]

## Why reversal does not do the job

- Acquiescence: a careful acquiescent reader still agrees with both "I am tired" and "I am fit", leaving an uninterpretable pattern. With all items keyed one way, an acquiescent person who is truly fatigued gets a nearly correct score and one who is not gets a biased one; reversing half the items just moves the bias to the other kind of respondent. Reversal can at best detect acquiescence, and agreeing with everything may equally be inattention or confusion.[^c3]
- Inattention: respondents can miss a negation, miss that an item switched direction, or answer the first item and copy it down the page. Only the last kind (missing content differences) can be caught by reversal, and only if the respondent notices the reversal.[^c4]
- Confusion: items using negative particles or affixes are harder to verify, and an item describing a state the respondent is not in is harder than one describing their own state.[^c5]
- Method artifact: reversal commonly produces a second factor, which is then debated as a real concept and later often shown to be a method factor. Woods found that carelessness in 10% of respondents is enough to create an artificial factor and wrongly reject unidimensionality.[^c6]

## The MFI-20 test

Within each four-item subscale the authors paired items by content with opposite direction, and compared them with same-direction pairs differing in content. If reversal reduced bias, same-content opposite-direction pairs should correlate most strongly. They did not: same-direction correlations were not consistently higher or lower in the predicted way, and the authors read this as an adverse effect of reversal.[^c7] Large discrepancies (3 or more points on a 5-point scale) between answers on same-content pairs were not rarer than between differing-content pairs, again read as inattention or confusion.[^c8]

Combined 20-item subscales had lower mean inter-item correlation than subscales with one direction; overall alpha was .95 (mean r .47) for all items, .90 for fitness-only and .91 for fatigue-only (.46 and .49). Higher alpha for the long scale is expected from length alone.[^c9] The authors conclude a 10-item, one-direction instrument makes fewer mistakes, and prefer the negatively formulated (fatigue) items for populations that are fatigued.[^c10] If both poles matter, they suggest presenting ordinary and reversed items separately rather than mixed.[^c11]

## Limits

- One instrument, one clinical sample (inflammatory bowel disease patients, who are more fatigued than the general population); no experiment randomizing wording.
- The three-point discrepancy criterion is, in the authors' words, "a bit arbitrary"; "suspicious" patterns are inferred, not independently verified as errors.
- The authors treated all fatigue and fitness items as measuring nearly the same thing; real two-pole constructs were not ruled out, and they grant some validity to unipolarity claims.
- The argument that reversal cannot prevent acquiescence is largely conceptual; the empirical part shows no benefit, not proof of harm for every scale.
- Whether the result carries to LLM prompts or to questions asked one at a time is untested.
