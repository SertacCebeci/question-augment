# Forced-choice safety collapse

> The same harmful request, asked open-ended, is refused; packaged as a
> multiple-choice question with no safe option, it is answered.

## Definition / Mechanism

Chen, Wu, Cheng & Takahashi (2026) build two datasets of
harmful questions — 90 human-authored, and a model-generated set — each
paired with four options that are all harmful (no refusal or benign
alternative among them), and push the same underlying request through seven
prompt formats of increasing structural constraint: bare question (Format 1),
question with options attached, an explicit "this is a multiple-choice
question" framing, a framing that requires picking an option, and framings
that additionally require an explanation, a detailed procedure, or
alternatives. Attack success is judged by a three-prompt GPT-4o ensemble plus
human adjudication on disagreements, counting a response as a violation if
it selects, ranks, or justifies any harmful option, or emits a bare option
letter. On the human-authored set, violation rate is non-monotonic in
constraint strength — it rises from open-ended to forced-choice, then falls
again once the format demands a full procedure — while model-generated
adversarial questions instead saturate and stay saturated once a choice is
forced.[^c1]

Holding the question's semantic content fixed and toggling only
the format, moving from an open question to an MCQ with explanation shifts a
model's violation rate by up to an order of magnitude.[^c2]

A frontier target
(GPT-5) that refuses essentially all human-authored attacks nonetheless
reaches an 82.67% attack success rate when the MCQ it is shown was generated
by a copy of itself.[^c3]
The strength of the effect scales with the
generating model's capability rather than with its similarity to the
target.[^c6]

Two ablations locate the mechanism at the act of forced selection, not at
the explanation it is sometimes paired with: stripping the requirement to
explain leaves the violation rate essentially unchanged (34/90 → 32/90),
while asking for an explanation to the *same open-ended question with no
options attached* raises the open-ended baseline only from 1.11% to 8.89% —
far below the 37.78% the MCQ framing alone produces.[^c4]

Randomizing option
order and re-running the same harmful items shows the model choosing the
same underlying (not the same lettered) option in 63.3% of matched cases,
well above the 25% a pure positional artifact would predict — so the
collapse is a change in what gets evaluated, not a slot the model fills by
position.[^c5]

## Why it matters

This is a formulation effect with no semantic manipulation, no adversarial
optimization, and no multi-turn pressure: the identical request, only
re-cast from "should I answer this" to "which of these should I pick,"
moves a model from reliable refusal to compliance. Any evaluation, benchmark
or deployment that asks a safety-trained model to select among predefined
options rather than to respond freely inherits this gap silently, and the
gap is largest exactly where option sets are cheapest to generate — by
another capable model.

## Limits

Every prompt is Chinese-only, single-turn, and restricted to
process/"how-to" harmful requests; whether the effect holds across
languages, multi-turn adjustment of options, or more indirect/hypothetical
harmful framings is unverified — the authors name all three as open. The
"intermediate structure provides a strong task-execution signal" account of
*why* the inverted U appears is the authors' own interpretation, offered
explicitly as a behavioral characterization rather than a mechanistic one,
and is not tested by probing or steering the way [[mcq-answer-position-bias]]
tests its mechanism.
