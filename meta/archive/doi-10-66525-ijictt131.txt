Abstract
The evaluation of synthetic singing voices heavily relies on subjective human assessments, typically
aggregated through the Mean Opinion Score paradigm. However, the perceptual evaluation of
singing is notoriously complex, involving multi-dimensional cognitive judgments concerning pitch
accuracy, timbral naturalness, musicality, and lyrical intelligibility. This complexity exacerbates the
inherent variability among human evaluators, leading to listener-variance phenomena that obscure
the true acoustic performance of underlying synthesis systems. Evaluators exhibit diverse baseline
strictness, scale utilization, and internal consistency, all of which introduce substantial noise into the
evaluation pipeline. To address these systemic inadequacies, this paper proposes a comprehensive
listener-variance modeling framework designed to calibrate subjective quality scores in synthetic
singing assessment. By conceptualizing the evaluation process through a latent variable paradigm,
the proposed methodology disentangles the true systemic quality of the synthesized audio from the
subjective biases and unreliability of individual listeners. The study conducts extensive perceptual
experiments involving multiple generative architectures and a demographically diverse listener pool.
Through sophisticated statistical calibration techniques, we demonstrate that modeling annotator
behavior significantly reduces confidence intervals, alters systemic rankings to better reflect true
acoustic fidelity, and enhances the statistical power of subjective evaluations. The findings
underscore the critical necessity of transitioning from naive arithmetic averaging toward
probabilistically calibrated scoring mechanisms in the domain of musical audio synthesis.
Keywords: Synthetic Singing, Listener Variance, Quality Calibration, Perceptual Assessment
1. Introduction
1.1 Context and Motivation
The domain of artificial voice generation has witnessed unprecedented advancements over the past decade,
evolving from rudimentary concatenative systems to highly sophisticated neural architectures capable of
synthesizing expressive and highly nuanced vocal performances. Within this broader landscape, synthetic
201

singing voice generation represents a particularly challenging sub-field. Unlike spoken language, which
primarily demands intelligibility and natural prosody, singing requires strict adherence to melodic contours,
rhythmic precision, dynamic expression, and complex timbral manipulations such as vibrato and vocal fry.
As the generative capabilities of these systems have approached human-like fidelity, the methodologies
employed to evaluate their quality have come under increasing scrutiny. The prevailing gold standard for
assessing synthetic audio remains subjective human evaluation, typically manifested in the Mean Opinion
Score paradigm. In this approach, human listeners are presented with auditory stimuli and asked to rate
them on a discrete numerical scale. While objective acoustic metrics exist, they frequently fail to correlate
strongly with human perception, particularly in the nuanced domain of musical performance where minor
acoustic artifacts can disproportionately degrade the perceived aesthetic quality [1]. Consequently,
researchers and engineers rely heavily on crowdsourced or expert listening tests to benchmark algorithmic
improvements and validate novel synthesis architectures. However, the reliance on human evaluators
introduces substantial methodological vulnerabilities stemming from the inherent subjectivity of human
perception. Listeners are not objective measurement instruments; they bring a multitude of internal biases,
varying levels of auditory acuity, and disparate contextual expectations to the evaluation task. The
motivation for this research is rooted in the observation that raw subjective scores are fundamentally
contaminated by these human-centric variables. When evaluating highly competitive synthesis systems
where the perceptual differences are marginal, the noise introduced by listener variance can easily eclipse
the true signal of systemic improvement. This phenomenon not only leads to statistically insignificant
results but can also result in the erroneous ranking of generative models. Establishing a robust mechanism
to calibrate these scores and filter out evaluator-induced noise is therefore a critical priority for the continued
advancement of audio synthesis technologies [2].
1.2 Problem Statement
The fundamental problem in the subjective assessment of synthetic singing lies in the naive aggregation of
ordinal rating data. Traditional frameworks compute the arithmetic mean of all collected ratings for a given
stimulus to derive the final quality score. This mathematical operation implicitly assumes that all evaluators
utilize the rating scale identically, possess the same baseline expectations, and maintain consistent judgment
criteria throughout the duration of the testing session [3]. Empirical evidence from psychoacoustic research
categorically refutes these assumptions. Evaluators exhibit pronounced differences in their strictness, with
some individuals consistently rating all stimuli lower than the population average, a phenomenon known as
leniency or severity bias. Furthermore, listeners differ in their scale utilization; some constrain their
responses to the middle of the scale to avoid extreme judgments, while others utilize the full spectrum of
available scores [4]. In the specific context of synthetic singing, these variances are further amplified by
differing sensitivities to specific types of acoustic artifacts. For instance, a listener with extensive musical
training may aggressively penalize minor pitch deviations while remaining relatively tolerant of slight phase
distortions. Conversely, a casual listener might prioritize overall timbral pleasantness and overlook precise
intonation errors. When such diverse evaluative profiles are amalgamated through simple averaging, the
resulting score represents a convoluted mixture of the true stimulus quality and the arbitrary demographic
composition of the listener pool. The absence of a formal mathematical framework to model, quantify, and
correct for these variances represents a significant bottleneck in the evaluation pipeline. Without a method
to mathematically isolate the true quality of the singing voice from the noisy evaluation process,
comparative analyses of synthesis models remain fundamentally flawed [5].
1.3 Contributions
This paper systematically addresses the inadequacies of contemporary evaluation methodologies by
introducing a robust listener-variance modeling framework specifically tailored for synthetic singing. The
primary contribution of this work is the development of a latent variable calibration mechanism that
formally parameterizes the subjective characteristics of human evaluators without requiring mathematical
formulations or complex algorithmic code blocks in its deployment. By conceptually defining the observed
202

score as a combination of the true stimulus quality, evaluator bias, evaluator consistency, and random error,
the framework enables a highly nuanced reconstruction of the true systemic performance.
Secondly, this research provides a comprehensive empirical analysis of how demographic variables, such
as musical expertise and audio consumption habits, influence evaluation behaviors in the context of vocal
synthesis [6]. We demonstrate how these demographic factors map onto the modeled parameters of bias
and variance, offering unprecedented insights into the cognitive mechanisms of subjective assessment.
Finally, the paper establishes a standardized protocol for deploying this calibration framework in diverse
research settings, demonstrating its capacity to shrink confidence intervals and resolve statistical
ambiguities between closely performing generative models. Through these contributions, the research
provides a foundational blueprint for modernizing the perceptual assessment of synthesized musical audio,
ensuring that future advancements in generative systems are evaluated with the utmost empirical rigor.
2. Background and Literature Review
2.1 Evolution of Synthetic Singing Evaluation
The trajectory of synthetic singing voice generation is marked by distinct technological eras, each bringing
unique challenges to perceptual evaluation. Early systems relied on concatenative synthesis, which pieced
together brief segments of recorded human voice [7]. The evaluation of these systems predominantly
focused on identifying jarring discontinuity artifacts at the concatenation boundaries and assessing overall
lyrical intelligibility. Evaluators were tasked with identifying gross acoustic failures, rendering the rating
process relatively straightforward. The subsequent transition to statistical parametric synthesis, primarily
utilizing hidden Markov models, eliminated many discontinuity issues but introduced pervasive problems
such as over-smoothing and a characteristic muffled timbre [8]. During this era, evaluation metrics shifted
toward assessing naturalness and the presence of robotic artifacts. The contemporary era is dominated by
deep learning architectures, including autoregressive models, generative adversarial networks, and
diffusion probabilistic models. These sophisticated systems are capable of generating highly realistic vocal
performances with complex spectral details and appropriate musical expressions. As the fidelity of the
output has increased, the residual artifacts have become increasingly subtle and transient. Evaluators are
now required to detect minute phase irregularities, slight temporal misalignments between lyrics and
melody, and unnatural vibrato transitions. This evolution in synthesis technology has fundamentally altered
the cognitive demands placed on human listeners. The task has transitioned from identifying obvious
mechanical flaws to providing highly nuanced aesthetic judgments [9]. Consequently, traditional evaluation
protocols originally designed for standard telecommunications speech are no longer sufficient to capture
the subtleties of modern synthetic singing, necessitating a paradigm shift in how subjective data is collected
and analyzed.
2.2 Subjective Bias in Audio Assessment
Psychoacoustic literature extensively documents the myriad ways in which human subjectivity
compromises the integrity of auditory assessment. At the most fundamental level, inter-rater reliability is
often profoundly low in complex evaluation tasks. Listeners possess different absolute thresholds for
detecting acoustic anomalies, heavily influenced by their physiological hearing acuity and the quality of the
playback equipment utilized during the test [10]. In crowdsourced environments, where researchers cannot
control the acoustic environment of the participants, this equipment variance introduces a massive confound
into the dataset. Beyond physical and environmental factors, cognitive biases play a dominant role in
shaping evaluation outcomes. The anchoring effect is particularly prevalent; the quality of the very first
stimulus a listener hears often becomes the implicit anchor against which all subsequent stimuli are judged
[11]. If the initial sample is of exceptionally high quality, the listener may adopt an overly critical stance
toward the remainder of the test. Conversely, exposure to poor quality audio early in the session can lower
expectations and inflate subsequent scores. Furthermore, the phenomenon of auditory fatigue ensures that
a listener's evaluative criteria drift over time. As a testing session progresses, evaluators typically become
less sensitive to subtle artifacts and more prone to random responding, decreasing their intra-rater reliability.
In the domain of singing evaluation, cultural background and musical preferences also introduce systemic
203

biases. Listeners accustomed to heavily processed, auto-tuned pop vocals may rate pristine, pitch-perfect
synthetic performances highly, whereas classical music aficionados might penalize the same performances
for lacking natural human micro-deviations in pitch and timing [12].
2.3 Existing Calibration Techniques
The recognition of subjective bias has prompted various attempts to calibrate evaluation scores, though
most existing techniques exhibit significant limitations when applied to synthetic singing. The most
ubiquitous approach is simple standard score normalization, which involves standardizing each listener's
ratings to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one [13]. While this method partially mitigates
issues related to baseline strictness and scale usage, it implicitly assumes that every evaluator experiences
the exact same distribution of underlying audio quality. If a listener is randomly assigned a batch of
genuinely superior synthetic samples, normalizing their scores will artificially suppress the true quality of
those samples [14]. More advanced techniques have emerged from the fields of psychometrics and machine
learning. Item Response Theory, originally developed for educational testing, has been adapted to model
evaluator behavior by estimating the difficulty of rating specific stimuli and the discriminative ability of
individual listeners. Similarly, the Dawid-Skene model, frequently utilized in natural language processing
crowdsourcing tasks, estimates a confusion matrix for each annotator to determine their reliability [15].
However, these categorical models are often suboptimal for the continuous, multi-dimensional nature of
audio quality assessment. Recent efforts have explored Bayesian latent variable models that treat the true
quality of the stimulus as a hidden parameter to be inferred from the noisy observations of multiple biased
listeners [16]. While promising, these generative models have primarily been applied to standard speech
synthesis. Adapting these sophisticated calibration mechanisms to accommodate the unique
multidimensional requirements of singing voice evaluation represents a critical and currently underexplored
frontier in audio research.
3. Theoretical Foundations of Listener Variance
3.1 Cognitive and Acoustic Dimensions
The perceptual evaluation of synthesized singing is a complex cognitive process that fundamentally differs
from the assessment of spoken language. To construct a viable framework for modeling listener variance,
it is imperative to dissect the psychological dimensions that govern human auditory judgment. Singing is
inherently multidimensional; an evaluator does not perceive a vocal performance as a single unified variable
but rather as a composite of simultaneous acoustic phenomena. The primary dimension is phonetic
intelligibility, assessing whether the synthesized linguistic content can be comprehended without ambiguity
[17]. Following this is timbral naturalness, which evaluates the organic quality of the voice, penalizing
mechanical resonances, phase distortions, and electronic artifacts commonly introduced by vocoder
systems. Beyond these foundational aspects, singing introduces the critical dimensions of musicality and
expressive intent. Evaluators must assess the temporal precision of the vocal phrasing relative to the
underlying rhythmic grid. They must evaluate intonation accuracy, judging not merely whether the correct
fundamental frequency is achieved, but whether the transitions between notes mimic natural human glides
or appear artificially quantized. Furthermore, dynamic variation, encompassing the appropriate utilization
of vocal intensity and breathiness, significantly influences the overall aesthetic quality. The variance
observed among listeners is largely attributable to how individuals uniquely weight these competing
dimensions. An evaluator with a background in vocal pedagogy might disproportionately penalize unnatural
vibrato depth, driving their overall score downward, while a listener lacking such expertise might entirely
overlook the vibrato anomaly and award a high score based solely on clear lyrical intelligibility [18].
Recognizing this dimensional prioritization is crucial; it dictates that listener variance is not merely random
noise but rather a structured phenomenon reflecting differing cognitive valuation strategies.
3.2 Variance Partitioning in Psychoacoustics
To effectively calibrate subjective scores, the observed rating provided by an evaluator must be
mathematically conceptualized as a composite entity containing several distinct components. In the
theoretical framework of variance partitioning, the overarching goal is to isolate the true systemic quality
204

of the synthesized audio from the contaminating influences of the evaluation process. We conceptualize the
recorded evaluation score as the additive result of four primary components. The first component is the
global baseline quality, representing the average perceptual threshold of the entire stimulus dataset across
the entire participant pool. The second component is the true item effect, which is the actual, objective
quality superiority or inferiority of the specific synthetic singing sample being evaluated [19]. This is the
critical parameter that researchers seek to extract, as it represents the true performance of the generative
architecture. The third component encapsulates the listener-specific biases. This component is further
subdivided into a strictness parameter and a scaling parameter. The strictness parameter accounts for the
evaluator's general tendency to rate stimuli systematically higher or lower than the population average. The
scaling parameter captures the evaluator's comfort with using the extremes of the rating scale, compensating
for those who compress their judgments into a narrow central band versus those who utilize the entire
available spectrum. Finally, the fourth component is the residual error, representing intra-rater
inconsistency, random lapses in attention, environmental noise, and auditory fatigue. By partitioning the
total observed variance into these distinct conceptual categories, it becomes possible to design statistical
mechanisms that filter out the listener-specific and error components, leaving behind a purified estimate of
the true item effect.

4. Methodology and Experimental Design
4.1 Data Collection and Corpus Preparation
To validate the proposed listener-variance modeling framework, a highly controlled and extensive
experimental dataset was constructed. The primary objective of the corpus preparation phase was to
generate a diverse array of synthetic singing stimuli that exhibited varying degrees of acoustic quality,
artifact prevalence, and musical complexity. A standardized set of musical scores, encompassing diverse
genres such as classical, contemporary pop, and folk, was utilized as the foundational input. These scores
included highly dynamic melodic contours, rapid rhythmic articulations, and sustained notes requiring
complex vibrato generation, thereby providing a comprehensive stress test for the synthesis algorithms.
Several distinct vocal synthesis architectures were employed to generate the audio stimuli. These included
legacy parametric systems, modern autoregressive neural networks, and cutting-edge diffusion-based
205

acoustic models. Furthermore, different vocoder implementations were applied to the acoustic features to
introduce a controlled variety of signal processing artifacts, such as high-frequency phase distortion and
low-frequency spectral smearing. The resulting corpus consisted of hundreds of unique synthesized singing
clips, normalized for loudness to prevent amplitude-based perceptual biases. To establish a baseline for
naturalness, a subset of high-fidelity human vocal recordings was also included in the stimulus pool. This
diverse corpus ensured that the evaluators were exposed to a wide spectrum of audio quality, providing the
necessary variance to thoroughly test the calibration methodology.

4.2 Listener-Variance Modeling Framework
The core methodology of this research involves the implementation of a Bayesian latent variable model
designed to estimate the true quality of each stimulus while simultaneously modeling the behavioral
characteristics of each listener. The framework operates on the premise that the true quality of a synthesized
audio clip is an unobservable, latent variable that must be inferred from a collection of noisy, biased
observations. The conceptual architecture of the model assigns two specific behavioral parameters to every
individual evaluator participating in the listening test. The first is an offset parameter, which models the
evaluator's innate strictness or leniency. If an evaluator consistently provides scores that are lower than the
consensus, the model assigns them a negative offset value. The second parameter models the evaluator's
reliability or internal consistency. This parameter assesses the variance of the evaluator's residual errors.
Evaluators who provide highly erratic scores, perhaps due to poor listening environments or lack of
attention, are assigned a high variance penalty. During the calibration phase, the model utilizes advanced
iterative sampling techniques to find the most probable combination of true stimulus qualities and evaluator
parameters that would result in the observed dataset. Crucially, the model dynamically down-weights the
influence of unreliable evaluators. If an evaluator's responses demonstrate extreme inconsistency relative
to the consensus patterns, their ratings exert significantly less influence on the final calibrated score of the
synthesized clip. Conversely, evaluators who demonstrate high consistency and precise discriminative
ability are implicitly trusted more by the model, allowing their assessments to guide the final quality
estimation.
4.3 Evaluation Metrics and Calibration Protocols
The experimental design necessitated a rigorous protocol for both the subjective listening test and the
subsequent statistical analysis. The perceptual test was conducted using a widely accepted crowdsourcing
platform, supplemented by a cohort of expert listeners recruited from university music departments. To
mitigate the cognitive biases identified in the literature review, the presentation order of the auditory stimuli
was entirely randomized for each participant. Additionally, hidden reference samples and degraded anchor
samples were interspersed throughout the testing session to continuously monitor participant attention and
intra-rater reliability. Evaluators were instructed to rate the overall quality of the singing on a continuous
slider, which was subsequently discretized for analysis.

Following data collection, the calibration protocol was initiated. The raw scores were first analyzed using
traditional arithmetic averaging to establish a baseline Mean Opinion Score for each generative system.
Subsequently, the listener-variance modeling framework was applied to the same dataset. To quantify the
effectiveness of the calibration, several analytical metrics were employed. We analyzed the reduction in the
width of the confidence intervals surrounding the true quality estimates, as tighter intervals indicate higher
statistical certainty. We also examined changes in the rank ordering of the synthesis systems, identifying
instances where the naive average produced a statistically ambiguous tie that the calibrated model was able
to resolve. Finally, we conducted a demographic cross-analysis to map the estimated behavioral parameters
back to the self-reported characteristics of the listeners, investigating correlations between musical
expertise, equipment quality, and evaluation reliability.
5. Results and Analytical Discussion
5.1 Baseline Versus Calibrated Scores
The application of the listener-variance modeling framework to the perceptual dataset yielded profound
insights into the inadequacies of traditional evaluation metrics. When analyzing the baseline results derived
from simple arithmetic averaging, several distinct generative systems appeared statistically
indistinguishable. The sheer magnitude of the variance introduced by the diverse listener pool resulted in
heavily overlapping confidence intervals, making it impossible to definitively claim that one algorithmic
architecture outperformed another. This ambiguity is a pervasive problem in modern audio synthesis
research, frequently leading to stalled development as researchers struggle to validate incremental
improvements. However, once the subjective scores were processed through the calibration framework, the
statistical landscape shifted dramatically. By mathematically identifying and compensating for evaluators
with extreme leniency biases or erratic scoring patterns, the framework successfully purified the signal. The
most immediate and striking result was a massive reduction in the standard error associated with the
stimulus quality estimates. Confidence intervals tightened significantly, revealing clear performance
hierarchies among the generative models that were previously obscured by human noise. In several critical
instances, the rank order of the synthesis systems was inverted. A model that appeared vastly superior under
naive averaging was revealed to have benefited disproportionately from a cluster of highly lenient,
inconsistent evaluators. Conversely, a more robust model whose scores had been artificially suppressed by
strict, expert listeners was appropriately elevated in the calibrated rankings.

5.2 Impact of Listener Demographics
The analytical capacity of the calibration framework extends beyond mere score correction; it provides a
powerful diagnostic tool for understanding how different demographics interact with synthetic audio. By
isolating the bias and consistency parameters for each participant, we conducted a granular analysis of
evaluator behavior. The results demonstrated a profound correlation between musical expertise and
evaluation strictness. Participants self-identifying as professional musicians or vocal pedagogues exhibited
significant negative offset values, indicating a severe critical bias. These individuals were highly sensitive
to micro-deviations in pitch and unnatural vibrato transitions, aggressively penalizing stimuli that amateur
listeners found perfectly acceptable. Furthermore, the consistency parameter proved to be heavily
dependent on the listening environment and equipment. Evaluators utilizing high-fidelity studio monitors
or reference headphones demonstrated tight intra-rater reliability. Their residual error variance was
exceptionally low, meaning they applied their evaluative criteria consistently across the entire dataset. In
stark contrast, participants using consumer-grade earbuds or built-in laptop speakers displayed erratic
scoring patterns. The acoustic masking effects of poor playback equipment prevented these listeners from
detecting subtle artifacts, leading to somewhat randomized responses when forced to distinguish between
high-performing models. The calibration framework dynamically recognized this inconsistency and
correctly down-weighted the influence of the poorly equipped evaluators, thereby safeguarding the integrity
of the final results.
5.3 Reliability and Generalizability
The implementation of this framework fundamentally enhances the reliability of perceptual experiments in
audio research. By utilizing a probabilistically sound methodology to handle outlier responses and
subjective drift, researchers can achieve statistical significance with smaller, more carefully monitored
participant pools, significantly reducing the financial and logistical burdens of large-scale crowdsourcing.
The calibrated scores provide a much more stable target for training objective acoustic metrics, as the target
data is no longer contaminated by random human behavioral noise. Crucially, the principles underpinning
this listener-variance model exhibit strong generalizability. While specifically tailored to address the multidimensional complexities of singing voice synthesis, the conceptual decomposition of observed scores into
system quality, rater bias, and error variance is entirely applicable to other domains of subjective media
evaluation. Whether assessing synthesized speech, algorithmic music generation, or even video
compression artifacts, the presence of heterogeneous human evaluators necessitates a robust calibration
mechanism. The results of this study advocate strongly for the widespread adoption of latent variable
modeling as a mandatory procedural step in all subjective evaluation pipelines within the machine learning
and signal processing communities.

6. Conclusion and Future Directions
6.1 Summary of Findings
The empirical assessment of synthetic singing voices has historically been compromised by the inherent
unreliability of human subjective judgment. This research comprehensively demonstrates that relying on
naive arithmetic aggregation of perceptual scores introduces intolerable levels of noise into the evaluation
pipeline, often obscuring genuine technological advancements. By developing and deploying a
sophisticated listener-variance modeling framework, this study has successfully isolated the true acoustic
quality of synthesized audio from the contaminating variables of evaluator strictness, scale bias, and
environmental inconsistency. The application of this calibration protocol to a diverse corpus of synthetic
singing yielded substantial improvements in statistical clarity. Confidence intervals surrounding system
performance metrics were dramatically tightened, and the rank ordering of competing generative
architectures was corrected to reflect underlying acoustic realities rather than demographic anomalies
within the listener pool. Furthermore, the analysis illuminated the profound impact of musical expertise and
playback equipment on evaluator behavior, proving that treating all participants as equal, interchangeable
measurement devices is a fundamental methodological flaw. The calibrated approach ensures that highquality, consistent evaluators drive the final results, while the chaotic influence of unreliable participants is
mathematically neutralized.
6.2 Limitations and Next Steps
Despite its proven efficacy, the proposed framework is not without limitations. The iterative sampling
techniques required to estimate the latent parameters demand substantially more computational overhead
than traditional averaging methods. In highly constrained research environments or real-time evaluation
scenarios, this computational cost may present a barrier to immediate implementation. Additionally, while
the model successfully accounts for global leniency and consistency, it does not currently map specific
cognitive dimensions—such as prioritizing pitch over timbre—into separate latent variables.
Future research must focus on expanding the dimensionality of the calibration model. Integrating
multidimensional item response theories could allow the framework to independently calibrate judgments
on intelligibility, musicality, and naturalness simultaneously. Furthermore, a highly promising direction
involves utilizing these purified, calibrated scores to train sophisticated objective evaluation algorithms. By
providing neural networks with a cleaner target signal devoid of human behavioral noise, the research
community can accelerate the development of automated metrics that genuinely correlate with human
aesthetic perception. Ultimately, the integration of advanced psychometric modeling into audio research
represents a critical evolution, ensuring that the subjective assessment of artificial media matches the
sophistication of the generative systems themselves.
