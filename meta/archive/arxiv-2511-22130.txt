Abstract
To navigate ever-shifting real-world environments, agents must grapple with incomplete knowledge and adapt their strategies through experience. However, current
evaluations of LLM-based agents largely overlook this capability. Crucially, we
stress not just the ability to contend with uncertainty within a task (episode), as
episode-specific information is progressively revealed across turns, but also the
refinement of such adaptive ability across similar episodes, as agents accumulate experiences and infer their shared latent structure. Repeated product recommendation
offers a natural setting to isolate this need for experiential learning: within each
interaction, an intelligent recommender must elicit unknown customer preferences
through questions; across multiple interactions, it should tailor its questioning
strategy to the observed customer and product distributions. We instantiate the
Benchmark for Experiential Learning and Active exploration (BELA) by combining (1) a rich catalog of real-world products from Amazon, (2) a diverse collection
of synthetic customer personas aimed to capture heterogeneous latent preferences,
and (3) an LLM-based customer simulator framework that emulate preferencerevealing interactions. Rather than aiming to faithfully replicate real consumer
behavior, BELA provides a controlled and scalable testbed for whether agents
can exploit consistent latent preferences across episodes. Benchmarking current
models reveals that they can learn across turns, but struggle to improve across
episodes. This underscore the need for frontier models to advance in experiential
learning capabilities.

Introduction

The ability to acquire and to learn from experience is a hallmark of intelligence. Real-world
environments involve uncertainty arising from unobserved information, and intelligent agents must
deliberately act to reduce unknowns and to learn from acquired observations. Recently, several
benchmarks have introduced tasks with unknowns that agents should strategically resolve in a multiturn task [1–8]. In addition to learning within a task, real-world agents have the opportunity to refine
their strategy from inferring the shared uncertainty structure in observed tasks. However, measuring
and advancing this ability has received far less attention.
∗ Equal contribution.

To motivate this ability, consider a sales agent recommending products to a stream of incoming
unknown customers. Early on, the agent must probe broadly to identify each customer’s taste. But
after repeated recommendations, the agent should recognize that its user base concentrates on a few
dominant preference clusters, and that a small number of well-chosen questions reliably disambiguate
among them. An agent deployed on a niche marketplace should instead learn the opposite: as
preferences are idiosyncratic, the same broad questions are uninformative. The structure of the latent
preference distribution dictates how the agent should act under uncertainty, and since this structure is
rarely known a priori, agents must learn to infer and exploit it from experience. Following Silver and
Sutton [9], we refer to this ability as experiential learning. Without it, large language model (LLM)
agents cannot tailor their information-gathering to the observed task distribution at deployment. We
stress the need to examine this capability in-context, since the optimal strategy depends on distribution
of tasks revealed at test time.
We construct the Benchmark for Experiential Learning and Active exploration (BELA) that isolates
this capacity for in-context experiential learning in product recommendation setting. BELA consists
of two main modules: (1) a choice set module with numerous sets of similar products that a customer
might choose from, and (2) a customer module that admits varying latent preferences and provide
interactive responses to recommenders. The customer module is designed for learnability rather
than realism: it exposes consistent latent structure that the recommender can learn from interactions.
By combining real amazon products [10] and diverse synthetic LLM persona [11], BELA supports
up to 2 billion unique choice set–persona pairs. Each pairing constitutes a single task with natural
uncertainty in the customer’s general preferences and their reactions to the available products. By
exposing agents on a stream of such pairings, combinatorially composable from this space of 2 billion
pairs, BELA offers an uniquely scalable environments for measuring agent’s experiential learning
capability under recommendation uncertainty. See Figure 2 for an example of our setting.
Our main contributions are as follows:
• We formulate in-context experiential learning as a centerpiece to intelligence. Agents must
strategically resolve uncertainty within a task (across turns), and refine their strategies by leveraging
past task experiences (across episodes). See Figure 1.
• To measure in-context experiential learning capability, we develop a Benchmark for Experiential
Learning and Active exploration (BELA), consisting of diverse and scalable product categories
with 71K products and 2K choice sets. Coupled with 1M scalably generated personas from Li et al.
[11], the benchmark supports up to 2B multi-turn product recommendation environments on which
one can combinatorially scale experiential learning environments.
• We observe that while state-of-the-art models exhibit learning across turns, they generally fail to
exhibit meaningful experiential learning across episodes, highlighting their inability to extract and
utilize high level information from past experiences.

Recommendation As A Testbed for Experiential Learning

Product recommendation provides a rich setting for testing the experiential learning capabilities of
agentic systems. Consider a common shopping scenario: a customer (c) enters a store in seek of
a product to satisfy a specific need. Many products fulfill the same functional purpose, but they
differ in style, aesthetics, or price, leading to preferences unique to the customer. We refer to this
group of functionally equivalent products as a choice set (S). The recommender agent must discover
customer’s preferences over S by engaging in multiple turns of queries: asking targeted questions
and synthesizing the customer’s responses. This entire interaction, from initial inquiry to final
recommendation, constitutes an episode.
Following each recommendation, the agent typically receives feedback, such as a purchase decision or
free-form text opinions about the suggested product. This feedback, combined with the intermediate
responses, encodes rich information about the latent factor (θ) underlying a given episode. This latent
factor may include the customer’s preferences, the dynamics of the question-answering process, how
the customer perceives the structure of the choice set, and so forth. Compared to a typical sequential
decision-making setup (e.g., POMDPs), the “reward" based on which the agent should optimize
its strategy is encoded primarily in text as part of the observations [13]. A capable agent must
interpret these text-based signals to sharpen its belief of the latent θ to improve its recommendations
in subsequent episodes.
A performant recommender must learn across users and products and improve based on experience.
Thus, we are not merely interested in the recommender agent’s performance within a single episode,
but rather its ability to improve as its experience accrues. For instance, consider a personalized
recommendation setting where the same customer interacts with the agent across multiple episodes.
In this case, the agent should focus on uncovering the customer’s underlying preferences to improve
future recommendations. In a scenario where the agent repeatedly sells a fixed choice set of products
to a stream of new customers, the goal shifts to identifying how these products compare relative
to one another across the diverse distribution of customers. In the most difficult setting, both the
customer and the choice set distributions can evolve over time.

Formally, the agent encounters a stream of episodes over its lifetime, where each e-th episode is
characterized by a tuple (ce , S e ) that induces a corresponding latent factor θ e . Each episode, indexed
by e ∈ {1, ..., E}, represents a single shopping session defined by a tuple (ce , S e ), representing a
customer and a choice set of products. In an episode (shopping session), customer ce is interested
in buying one product from S e according to their preference. Each episode contains a sequence
of turns where each turn consists of the recommender’s question or recommendation ae,t , and the
customer’s response oe,t . If the action ae,t is a question, then the response oe,t is the customer’s
answer; if ae,t is a recommendation, then oe,t is a feedback provided by the customer, encoding the
customer’s preference over the recommended product. Naturally, the e-th episode concludes after a
recommendation is made, and the agent moves on to the next episode. We denote this final feedback
as fe for convenience.

adapt to the dynamics of Θ in an online fashion. At each turn, the response oe,t = g(θ e , ae,t ) is a
function of both the action and the latent factor of the episode. At episode e and turn t, the entire

BELA: A Benchmark for Experiential Learning and Active Exploration

To instantiate the aforementioned benchmark for experiential learning, our product recommendation
dataset consists of two main modules: (1) a set of C = {ci } of hypothetical customers that can
respond interactively to the recommender, and (2) a set S = {S j } of choice sets.
4

A few desiderata are essential for a meaningful evaluation of experiential learning capability. First, the
underlying sets of both modules (C and S) need to be scalable and diverse to support a wide variety
of Θ, customer preferences, and question-answering dynamics. Second, the customer module must
maintain consistency between θ e and (oe,t , fe ); naturally, since the latter constitutes the experiences
accessible to the agents, it must reliably inform the agents about θ e . In particular, note that whether C
reflect the distribution of real-world customers’ preferences is not a key requirement for the primary
purpose of evaluating experiential learning. For use cases where one wishes to assess the quality
of an agent’s recommendation for customers from a particular industry or company, our framework
easily supports customized instantiations of C.
3.1

Products and Choice Sets Curation

To form the choice sets module, we curate a pool of products that are correctly categorized into
reasonable choice sets. For the products, we first sample 100K products from the Amazon Reviews
dataset [10]. We then applied filters to remove products missing essential fields such as product
descriptions. We match each product to a predefined category from ASINSpotlight [14]. Through a
rigorous matching process, we were able to assign 70% of the sampled products to the categories of
ASINSpotlight [14]; see Appendix F for additional details.
Then, we extract S as a subset of predefined categories, leaving only categories that could reasonably
be treated as choice sets. We first truncate the top two levels of this category hierarchy as these levels
contained overly broad classifications ill-suited as choice sets (e.g., Home & Kitchen). We also
filter categories that contain too few products to support nontrivial recommendation settings. See
Figure 3 for an illustration of the process.
Through the novel filtering schemes, we construct a large collection of products and choice sets,
consisting of 71K products and 2K choice sets. Since some categories share products, the dataset
contains in total 100K product-category pairs.
On scalability of available choice sets, our pipeline naturally supports expansion by sampling
additional products from Hou et al. [10] or other large-scale datasets. Moreover, our framework is
designed to be broadly compatible, allowing it to incorporate any choice set that contains a sufficient
number of products with descriptions. More sophisticated or specialized data collection pipelines
could be developed to gather richer and more diverse choice sets, further extending the scope of our
benchmark.
3.2

Persona-Based Customer Simulation

To power the customer module, we first need to curate a pool of persona descriptions that define the
simulated customers. Next, to enable the evaluation of recommendation quality in regret, we generate
each persona’s preference over a given choice set (ya = h(pa , c, S)). Finally, we have a choice over
the types of feedback fe to be provided at the end of a produce recommendation episode.
Persona Curation. To power the customer module, we use the 1M persona specifications in Li
et al. [11] to provide diversity in C. We then utilize LLMs to simulate the customer’s response
corresponding to a persona file to enable interactive question-answering. The persona therein are
generated by LLMs, and hence are as scalable and diverse as what modern LLMs encode.
Preference Generation. For preference generation (ya = h(pa , c, S)), the scoring function h(·) was
an average of the scores generated using two models: GPT-4o and Gemini-1.5-Pro. Each model
received the full description of the current persona encoding c, along with details of the products
in the set S under evaluation. The models were instructed to assign a score (0–100) reflecting how
much the persona would enjoy or appreciate each product within S. The consistency of the scoring
process is crucial to the integrity of the benchmark. To evaluate the consistency, we randomly selected
five categories and measure the average variation in the scores across three seeds. We found that
the averaged score variation is 4.1, much smaller than the standard deviation in scores within each
category (25.4), which suggests that the scoring process is consistent. For instance, in Figure 3, the
persona is described as having curly brown hair. Consistent with this trait, products intended for curl
enhancement are assigned high scores, while products designed for hair straightening receive lower
scores.

3.3

Dataset Robustness

To prevent unrealistic scenarios (e.g., an individual with no musical interests shopping for pianos), we
exclude choice set-persona pair in which the highest product score is below 60. Using this filtering
procedure, an average of 3.75 scenarios were skipped across 10 randomly drawn choice set-persona
pairs.
We further verify that the customer module forms a consistent and learnable environment through two
additional checks: (a) faithfulness of simulator responses to persona descriptions, and (b) stability
of simulator responses under repeated queries. For (a), we sample 10 multi-episode trajectories and
use Claude Sonnet 4 as an LLM-as-judge to label each persona-agent answer as Aligned, Conflict, or
Neutral relative to the persona description. Across 327 answers, 74.6% are labeled Aligned, 19.3%
Neutral, and 6.1% Conflict. Manual review shows that all labeled conflicts are false positives, arising
from missing persona details, unwarranted assumptions by the judge, or differences in specificity
than genuine contradictions. For (b), we resample the simulator’s answer ten times for each question
and measure agreement with the per-question majority response. Across 1,090 responses, 94.4%
agree with the majority position, suggesting high response stability. Full setups and results are in
Appendix E.
To further demonstrate learnability of the environments, we conducted a manual, human-curated
questioning run as seen in Appendix C. The questions were deliberately reasonable in that they (i)
targeted concrete, product-determining attributes, (ii) prompted the persona to reveal personality
details, and (iii) were answerable without specialized knowledge. Using the information elicited by
expert questionings, the agent identified the best scoring product (regret = 0) when it had otherwise
consistently chosen a product with regret 37.5. This demonstrates that, given a well-planned query
policy, the information required to reach the best item is accessible, and the environment is solvable.

Experiments

We consider three sequential recommendation settings that exemplify experiential learning: (a) same
customer across episodes with different choice sets each episode, (b) same category across episodes
with different customers each episode, and (c) different choice sets and customers each episode. Of
course, the rich sets of personas and choice set available in BELA can support many more settings
than the ones considered in this study.
To evaluate performance, aside from the aforementioned regret metrics, we monitor the number
of questions asked. In our experiments, recommender agents are instructed to identify the best
product while asking as few questions as they deem necessary. This setup requires agents to balance
uncertainty reduction with interaction efficiency: they should ask enough questions to make an
informed recommendation, but avoid unnecessary questions. We consider three baselines throughout.
R ANDOM recommends products uniformly at random, and P OPULARITY selects the product with
the highest original rating, breaking ties by choosing the cheaper option. Finally, O RACLE is a
Claude-Sonnet-4 with access to the full persona description and prompted to directly select a product
from the category. Since Claude is not used during the initial scoring of products, this setup ensures
a clean separation and serves as a lower bound on regret in cases where the recommender has full
access to persona information.

4.3

Ideal Learning Trajectories

The negative results above admit a trivial alternative explanation: the customer interactions may carry
little transferable information for models to learn across episodes. We rule this out by constructing
ideal learning trajectories that exploit cross-episode information in the same experimental setting as
Figure 4. Their existence serves as a witness to shared information that agents could have leveraged
but did not. To find the ideal trajectories, for each k-th episode, we run the experiment 5 times
with GPT-5.4 as the recommender. Out of the 5 repetitions, we pick one high-quality trajectory that
contains questions that target meaningful aspects of the persona. We then freeze this accepted episode
and use the questions and information obtained therein as the starting context for the next episode.
We repeat this process for all 10 episodes to find an ideal trajectory, and in total find 10 trajectories
depicted in Figure 7. The decreasing regret across episodes illustrates how a capable agent should
inquire transferable questions to acquire experiences that help recommend products in later episodes.
In contrast, without this deliberate process, current agents on their own appears incapable of this
learning.
Qualitatively, these trajectories show a shift in focus from surface-level, episode-specific questions
that focus on specific features, to higher-level modeling of user preferences that may be transferable.
Figure 5 gives one representative example from Episodes 1, 6, and 10. In Episode 1, the agent
asks shallow feature-comparison questions such as “solar-powered or low voltage?”, which captures
surface-level preferences helpful to the recommendation in the current trajectories, but miss deeper
latent preferences of the user. By Episode 6, the agent begins to model higher-level attributes such as
the user’s value hierarchy (e.g., sustainability vs. price), signaling a transition from inquiring specific
attributes to modeling implicit preferences. By Episode 10, the agent has internalized the persona’s

Related Work

Reinforcement learning for LLM. Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) [15–17]
has proven highly effective for aligning models with objectives that lack well-defined, differentiable
reward functions, improve models on dimensions such as translation quality [18, 19], helpfulness
[20, 21], and factual accuracy [22–25]. However, these settings are fundamentally limited as RL
environments, corresponding to fully observable single-step environments, where the model takes
one action (i.e. generating a response) with all necessary information available, and immediately
receives a scalar reward. While this paradigm has been remarkably successful for training performant
zero-shot models, it fails to capture the interactive, dynamic nature of real-world environments, where
actions and feedback over multiple turns and episodes can aid the models’ decisions. In contrast, our
work focuses on evaluating agents ability to contend with uncertainty in multi-turn and multi-episode
settings, and highlights the insufficiency of current state-of-the-art models to adapt over the latter.
Multi-turn and Multi-episode RL. Recent years have witnessed a surge of interest in agentic
models powered by LLMs [12, 26, 27]. These agents are expected to autonomously plan, act and
adapt through iterative interactions with their environment, requiring models to engage in multi-turn
interactions within an episode of task. As a result, prior works have explored multi-turn settings in
text-based games [28, 29], medical question-answering [1], and numerous other tasks [30, 31]. Some
other works have explored multi-episode settings [32, 33], but they primarily focus on environments
devoid of uncertainties, and therefore do not require agents to actively resolve uncertainties in the
environment. In contrast, our setup focuses on assessing the ability of the models to reason through
uncertainties of the latent preference of the customers in context.
Recommendation System and LLM Personalization. There has been a long line of work on
recommendation systems [34, 35]. In the era of foundation models, LLMs have demonstrated
remarkable zero-shot performance in recommendation tasks [36–38]. However, the classical yet
crucial setting of sequential recommendation [39, 40] remains underexplored in this era, and the
even more realistic problem of modeling interactions with customers has received little attention.
Our dataset addresses this gap by enabling the study of these settings through an interactive user
simulator powered by LLMs. Separate but relatedly, the rise of LLMs has sparked growing interest in
personalized LLMs [41–43]. Compared to these post-hoc heuristic approaches, we propose a more
principled way to instill agents with the ability to learn to personalize. Our dataset is designed to
directly evaluate and help advance this capability.
9

6

Conclusion

We argue for a shift in focus toward measuring whether agents can leverage past episodes to refine
how they resolve uncertainty. To this end, we introduced BELA: a flexible interactive recommendation benchmark designed to evaluate in-context experiential learning capabilities. The framework
we employ provides a uniquely scalable dataset, supporting up to 2B multi-turn product recommendation environments, the combinations of which can form numerous diverse environments to
study experiential learning behaviours. Our experiments reveal that while frontier models can learn
across turns within an episode, they still fail to learn effectively from experiences accrued across
episodes. In addition, they struggle to calibrate their uncertainties and, as a result, fail to proactively
ask informative questions. By uncovering these limitations, we hope to motivate future research on
experiential learning, which is essential for building agents capable of reliable decision-making under
constantly shifting real-world uncertainties. In terms of limitation, BELA deliberately prioritizes
evaluation of experiential learning over realism, and we do not expect the simulated customers to
reflect real users. This work also does not explore training methods to instill experiential learning
capability, which we leave as interesting direction for future work.

Varying Prompts

We investigate whether explicit prompting can induce more deliberate planning during recommendation. Using GPT-4o, we compare three prompting variants: (1) No Strategy, which simply instructs
the agent to ask questions for T turns; (2) Greedy, which instructs the agent to choose actions that
maximize immediate recommendation quality; and (3) DP, which frames the task as planning in a
POMDP and instructs the agent to act as a DP-optimal planner. The full prompts are provided in
Appendix D.4.
We first evaluate whether these prompting strategies improve within-episode behavior across turns.
In this setting, the agent is forced to ask 10 questions and to provide a recommendation after each
question. However, the agent receives no feedback between turns and is not given memory of its
previous recommendations until the final turn. We evaluate this setup in Episodes 1, 5, and 10. As
shown in Figure 9, explicit planning prompts do not reliably improve the agent’s ability to learn

Planning Agents

In addition to the baseline recommender prompts described above, we introduce prompts designed to
explicitly encourage planning behavior. These variants aim to test whether models can adopt more
deliberate strategies for information gathering rather than defaulting to shallow heuristics.
The Greedy prompt (Figure 23) directs the agent to internally enumerate plausible candidate products
and then select the single most informative clarifying question that would best differentiate among
them.
The POMDP prompt (Figure 24) frames the interaction as a planning problem under uncertainty.
Here, the agent is instructed to maintain a belief state over possible user preferences, evaluate the
expected value of different candidate questions, and select the one with the highest information
gain—even if its benefits only materialize in later turns.

E

Additional Consistency Checks for the Customer Module

A central requirement of our benchmark is that the customer simulator behaves consistently: it should
remain faithful to its persona specification and provide stable answers under repeated queries. We
evaluate these properties through two complementary tests.
24

E.1

Faithfulness to Persona Descriptions

We first test whether simulator responses remain consistent with the underlying persona descriptions.
For each of the 327 agent questions from 10 multi-episode trajectories, Claude Sonnet 4 labels the
simulator’s answer as Aligned, Neutral, or Conflict with respect to the persona description. The raw
breakdown is:
Aligned: 244 (74.6%),
Neutral: 63 (19.3%),
Conflict: 20 (6.1%).
However, manual review shows that all 20 Conflict labels are false positives. These cases typically
arise when the judge infers contradictions from missing persona details, makes unwarranted assumptions about what activities a persona should enjoy, or treats differences in specificity as logical conflict.
We identify no genuine contradictions between simulator responses and persona profiles.
E.2

Response Stability Under Resampling

We next measure whether the simulator gives semantically stable answers when asked the same
question multiple times. Across 10 trajectories with 5 episodes each, we pause whenever the
recommender asks a question and query the persona simulator independently 10 times, refreshing
the GPT-4o persona agent for each run. We then present all 10 responses to Claude Sonnet 4, which
identifies the majority position and labels each response as either aligned or misaligned with that
position. Across 109 questions, this yields 1,090 total responses:
Aligned: 1,029 (94.4%),
Misaligned: 61 (5.6%).
Manual inspection indicates that most misaligned cases are not substantive preference changes, but
minor numeric or stylistic variations. For example, in response to a budget question, answers such as
“under $50,” “$30 to $50,” “moderately priced,” and “reasonably priced” were sometimes separated by
the evaluator despite expressing the same underlying value-conscious preference. Thus, the measured
94.4% agreement rate should be interpreted as a conservative lower bound on semantic consistency.

F

Dataset Collection

Collecting Products. The Amazon Reviews dataset [10], consisting of 34 product categories, is set
up such that one has to pick one of the 34 categories to sample products. Therefore, to preserve the
original distribution of categories, we performed weighted random sampling over the categories. We
provided the necessary scripts to draw more products in our codebase.
Matching Product Categories. The first challenge is inconsistent naming conventions; for example,
a product’s category path might be ...→Women → Shoes, Sandals, whereas our standardized path
is ... → Women’s Fashion → Women’s Shoes → Women’s Sandals. The second challenge is
structural discrepancy, where a product’s path omits an intermediate level, such as CDs&Vinyl →
Blues, while the canonical path is CDs&Vinyl → Music Styles → Blues. To solve these issues,
our algorithm employs a two-pronged approach at each step of the path traversal:
• Fuzzy Matching for Naming Inconsistencies: To resolve differing names, we apply a series of
prioritized fuzzy matching heuristics. For instance, the substring detection heuristic is precisely
what allows the algorithm to map a product’s simpler category Sandals to the more descriptive
canonical term Women’s Sandals. Other heuristics, like word subset validation (mapping Action
Figures to Action & Toy Figures) and normalization (handling case and suffix differences), ensure
robustness against a wide range of naming variations.
• Subtree Traversal for Structural Gaps: To handle missing intermediate levels, our algorithm does
not merely search the immediate children of the last matched node. Instead, it performs a breadthfirst search through the entire subtree of descendants. This is how it bridges structural gaps. In
the example CDs & Vinyl → Blues, once the algorithm successfully matches the CDs & Vinyl
node, it then searches all descendants for a Blues node. It will find the correct Blues node even
though it is a grandchild (nested under Music Styles), effectively “skipping over" the missing
level in the product’s path data.
This matching enables us to successfully map about 70% of the sampled products to a valid path
within the target hierarchy. Despite the flexibility of these heuristics, the overall assignment criteria
remain strict: a product is considered successfully matched only when its entire category sequence
corresponds to a valid, continuous path from the root.

G

Additional Models’ Failure Modes

In this section, we discuss some notable failure modes of the considered models.
Repetitive Questions. A recurring issue with some models was an endless loop of repetitive
questions. At times, the model would repeatedly ask the same queries despite having access to the
full conversation history, continuing until reaching a 20-turn limit. This was particularly evident with
GPT-4o in all three experiments. Both Gemini models did not have this issue, while both Claude
models showed repeated questions only under the variable setting experiment mode.
Failure to Memorize Requirements. Occasionally, explicit preferences stated by the customer are
ignored. The final product chosen by the recommender agent can go against the stated preferences
directly. This shows that SOTA models still struggle to respect specifications provided in context.
Model Specific Questions. Models from the Gemini series exhibit their own specific rigid patterns.
They frequently ask questions like,“Are you looking for a free option?” and then often proceed
immediately to a recommendation. This behavior suggests the model is following a pre-determined
conversational script rather than dynamically adapting to the user’s needs, showing no interest in
asking more questions and prematurely ending the conversation.
