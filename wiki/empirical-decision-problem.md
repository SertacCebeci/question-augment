# Empirical decision problem

A decision problem can be posed without naming the states of the world. The
model's primitive is a **protocol**: a finite record of pairs saying which
act was taken and what consequence followed. "We propose a decision model that
retains the appeal and simplicity of the original theory, but completely
overcomes the need to specify the states of the world explicitly."[^c1]

The acts are replaced by *action descriptions*, plain statements of what was
done, such as "choose the blue card to clock out". A description "does neither
require specifying a state space S nor a consequence space C: It is a pure
description of the action the decision maker is taking".[^c2] Each description
still has an unknown consequence, a random variable on a latent state space,
and the only observable part is the consequence itself: "Just follow some
action description and observe what happens!"

## Mechanism

An **empirical choice function** (ECF) takes a sub-protocol, meaning the
pairs for some subset of the descriptions, and returns a subset of those
descriptions. It uses only the protocol: "empirical choice functions refer
solely to the available protocol."[^c3] The paper's running example has five
coloured clock-out cards, each tried five times with a money outcome. The
empirical expected-utility rule (the sample mean of each card's outcomes, then
the maximum) returns Red alone: "In this example, out of all cards, this ECF
selects only" Red.[^c4] The empirical first-order-dominance rule keeps Red,
Blue and Green, and drops Yellow and Black.[^c5]

The point of the construction is that the choice set moves with the protocol.
"Optimal empirical choice sets are dependent on the observed protocol" and "may
change after further experimentation".[^c6] Convergence is the claim that
makes this more than a heuristic. With consequences drawn i.i.d. for each
description and protocols growing, the EU-based ECF "acts as a consistent
approximator of the population variant of the choice function under certain
conditions".[^c7] The dominance-based rule does not stabilise by itself: in
the paper's binomial simulation its sets "do not appear to stabilize", and the
fix is that the naive variant "must be replaced by a regularized version".[^c8]
Both are reported as illustrations; the proofs of the convergence theorem sit
in the appendix and were not checked here.

The condition that carries the whole approach is the randomness of the
mechanism: "the analysis of EDPs is usually only useful if" the states are
generated by something that can be treated as random.[^c9] The paper shows
the failure case: if the states follow a deterministic switching rule, the
protocol keeps selecting the red card, although it is "strictly inadmissible
in the underlying decision problem: every other card" gives more profit in
every state.[^c10]

*My inference, not the paper's claim:* the transferable move is that a choice
can be scored from logged attempts and their outcomes, without first listing
every way the world could be. Whether that log is informative depends on the
randomness condition above, which the logged attempts cannot themselves test.

## Limits

- The guarantees hold "only in frequentist settings".[^c11] Under a suspected
  deterministic or adversarial mechanism the theory "remains silent", and the
  paper proposes learning that mechanism as a different problem.
- Each description's consequences must be i.i.d.: "under each individual action
  description can be interpreted as an i.i.d. sample".[^c12] A modified test
  tolerates slight deviations, but the authors say it "will only very rarely
  produce interesting statements" for designs far from i.i.d.[^c13]
- Dropping the state space is paid for in purely empirical claims: optimality
  statements are "purely empirical ones, not directly generalizing to the
  underlying true decision problem".[^c14]
- The evidence is a proof-of-concept on comparing prompting strategies in
  generative AI models (abstract). No empirical comparison against classical
  decision analysis is reported in the parts read.
