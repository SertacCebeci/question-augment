# Self-administered interview

The Self-Administered Interview (SAI) is a tool that replaces the interviewer with a structured set of elicitation prompts that the witness works through alone. It was designed because statements should be obtained as soon as possible after an incident, which police resources often prevent.[^c1] Its purpose is to "elicit a comprehensive initial statement": the questioning structure, not the questioner, carries the work of getting memory out.[^c2]

## What was measured

Two studies are reported in the abstract. In Study 1, SAI participants reported more correct details than participants who gave a free recall account. They performed at the same level as participants given a Cognitive Interview, an interviewer-led technique.[^c3] So a fixed prompt structure recovered the interviewer's gain over unstructured recall without the interviewer.

In Study 2, participants viewed a simulated crime and half recorded their statement using the SAI. After a delay of one week, all completed a free recall test. SAI participants recalled more correct details in that delayed test than control participants.[^c4] The early structured elicitation therefore did more than collect a statement: it improved what the witness could still produce later. This is consistent with the first retrieval shaping the later one, but the abstract does not say why. That mechanism is my inference, not a finding reported here.

## Why it matters for asking

The result separates two things that are usually bundled in an interview: the question structure (which cues are offered, in what order) and the presence of a person asking. In this setting, the structure alone reached the level of the interviewer-led method on correct details. It also suggests that when the answerer is the only available source of information and the asker cannot be present, a pre-built sequence of prompts can stand in for live questioning.

## Limits

- The only text available is the abstract and bibliographic record. Sample sizes, effect sizes, the SAI's actual sections, error or incorrect-detail rates, and the control condition's exact form are not in it, so none are claimed.
- "Correct details" is the reported outcome; whether accuracy (correct over total) or confabulation changed is not stated.
- Both studies used simulated events with student-type participants as far as the abstract indicates; real witnesses, emotional events and longer delays are untested here.
- Equal performance to the Cognitive Interview in Study 1 is a null difference; the abstract does not report an equivalence test.
- The delayed-recall benefit was tested against control participants, not against a Cognitive Interview group.
