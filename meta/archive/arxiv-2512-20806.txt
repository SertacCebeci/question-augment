Ensuring the safety of language models (LMs)
while maintaining their usefulness remains a critical challenge in AI alignment. Current approaches rely on sequential adversarial training:
generating adversarial prompts and fine-tuning
LMs to defend against them. We introduce a different paradigm: framing safety alignment as a
non-zero-sum game between an Attacker LM and
a Defender LM trained jointly via online reinforcement learning. Each LM continuously adapts
to the other’s evolving strategies, driving iterative improvement. Our method uses a preferencebased reward signal derived from pairwise comparisons instead of point-wise scores, providing
more robust supervision and potentially reducing reward hacking. Our RL recipe, AdvGame,
shifts the Pareto frontier of safety and utility,
yielding a Defender LM that is simultaneously
more helpful and more resilient to adversarial attacks. In addition, the resulting Attacker LM converges into a strong, general-purpose red-teaming
agent that can be directly deployed to probe arbitrary target models. Code at github.com/
facebookresearch/advgame.

Safety alignment of LMs is not solved. Despite undergoing careful safety post-training (e.g., as in Meta Platforms,
Inc. (2024a)), state-of-the-art models remain far from robust
when placed against adaptive attacks, exhibiting nontrivial
jailbreak success rates on standard safety benchmarks, as
shown in Figure 1. We conjecture that this is due to suboptimality of the existing methods for safety alignment of
LMs. Current approaches generally fall into two complementary categories. The first approach involves manually
collecting sets of harmful input prompts across topics of
interest and performing fine-tuning to teach models to refuse
or respond appropriately to such input (Ganguli et al., 2022).
The second approach leverages automatic prompt optimization methods to jailbreak LMs (Guo et al., 2021; Zou et al.,
2023), followed by adversarial training (via RL or SFT)
on the collected prompts. Increasingly, such jailbreaking
attacks are crafted via specialized Attacker LMs designed
to generate adversarial prompts (Paulus et al., 2025).

1. Introduction
Language models (LMs) have become ubiquitous across
numerous applications, from conversational assistants to
content generation and decision support systems. As these
models grow in capability and deployment scale, ensuring
their safety has emerged as a critical challenge. Modern LM
developers face a fundamental tension: balancing the usefulness and helpfulness of model responses against the need to
prevent harmful, inappropriate, or unsafe outputs. Achieving this balance is essential for responsible deployment of
LMs in real-world settings.

In practice, these approaches are typically applied iteratively in a sequential process. For instance, when using an
Attacker LM, the standard procedure, e.g., as in Paulus et al.
(2025) involves: (1) training the Attacker to generate adversarial prompts, (2) using those prompts to train the Defender
LM (the main model being aligned) to resist such attacks,
and (3) repeating this alternation until some convergence
criterion is met. This sequential cat-and-mouse game, while
effective to some degree, suffers from inefficiencies and

Preference-based signals address these issues by requiring
only reliable relative comparisons rather than fragile absolute metrics. Furthermore, our formalization allows using
off-policy samples, which we show significantly improves
training stability in comparison to pure on-policy learning
(Section 3.2). We provide a comprehensive empirical evaluation of safety post-training methods, yielding a Defender
LM that achieves state-of-the-art performance on HarmBench and exhibits greater robustness to strong adaptive
attacks than prior defended models (Section 4). Finally, in
addition to Defender LM, the trained Attacker LM becomes
a strong, general-purpose red-teaming agent that can be
directly used to probe arbitrary models (Section 4.4).

This paper: safety alignment via attacker-defender
games. In this paper, we introduce a different paradigm:
jointly training the Attacker and Defender LMs by formulating the safety alignment problem as an adversarial game,
which we call AdvGame. Rather than alternating updates,
AdvGame trains both agents concurrently, allowing each
side to adapt online to the other’s current capabilities. This
joint view better matches the operational reality of safety
alignment: attack surfaces shift as models change and this
encourages equilibria that reflect the long-run interaction
between Attacker and Defender.
One possible approach to such formulations employs a twoplayer zero-sum game, which under mild conditions admits
a minimax equilibrium (Nash, 1950). However, we argue
that adversarial safety alignment games are inherently nonzero-sum and asymmetric. In a true zero-sum game, one
player’s gain is exactly another player’s loss. But in safety
alignment, the Attacker and Defender have distinct, not
perfectly opposed objectives. We describe this in-detail in
Section 3.1. Recent work has explored such zero-sum game
direction in self-play settings, where the same one model
serves both roles (Liu et al., 2025a) (i.e., shared parameters).
Such self-play has gained some popularity in recent years
for training models to solve math, logic and other problems
(Silver et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2025). While self-play
formulations may be sensible for scenarios such as “an LLM
teacher proposes challenging math problems for a student
LLM to solve”, where collaborative improvement is the goal,
self-play is problematic for adversarial safety alignment:
sharing a single set of parameters to serve both Attacker
and Defender can entangle objectives, leak gradients across
roles, and bias exploration, undermining the diversity and
hardness of attacks or encouraging degenerate “solutions”
(e.g., Attacker refusing to create harmful attacks).

Nash equilibrium. First, we characterize the existence
of an equilibrium at which both the Attacker and Defender
objective are maximized. We observe that Equation (3)
is independent of ρ and decouples over x. This problem
is strongly concave in the tabular π, therefore a unique
solution exists which we denote as π ∗ (·|c, s, x). Similarly,
Equation (2) can be decomposed over seeds and is again
strongly-concave in the tabular ρ, and we denote the unique
optimal solution as ρ∗ (·|c, s). The unique Nash equilibrium
is therefore given by (ρ∗ , π ∗ ).

query is not faithful to the seed query, we cannot give a

4. Experiments

We also include a variant of our formulation called
AdvGame-GRPO, in which the judge model directly produces scalar rewards instead of preferences. This gives a
formulation as in AdvGame-DPO, but with an explicit reward model instead of an implicit one. Equation (8) and
Equation (9) can then be directly optimized with the standard GRPO loss (Shao et al., 2024) or variants thereof (Liu
et al., 2025b). This is the closest algorithm to the competing
method Self-RedTeam (Liu et al., 2025b), with the major
modifications of being a non-zero-sum formulation and removed parameter sharing between Attacker and Defender.

The primary goal of this section is to conduct an extensive
evaluation of AdvGame. Our assessments focus on the
following key questions: 1) To what extent can AdvGame
advance the Pareto frontier of utility, safety and compliance?
2) How effective is our Attacker for generic model safety
red teaming? 3) What are the critical factors contributing to
the success of AdvGame (pairwise judge, EMA, etc.)?

4.5. Analysis and Discussion
This section presents our analysis of several key aspects of
our proposed adversarial training. Specifically, we compare
different versions of AdvGame (DPO-MD, IPO-MD, GRPO)
across multiple dimensions, including training stability, utility and safety performance, among others. Additionally,
using online DPO as a case study, we provide ablations on
various parameters of AdvGame: the type of judge model,
the benefits of off-policy versus on-policy training, the importance of training the Attacker model rather than keeping
it fixed, and more. Further analysis can be found in Section C, where we report reward as one of the main metrics in
learning curves, but note that AdvGame-DPO/IPO does
not actually use them during training as they only need
preference pairs. So, we calculate reward only for logging
purposes in those methods.

4.4. How Good Is the Attacker?
A notable byproduct of our method is the development of
a capable Attacker model, which can be leveraged for redteaming purposes. Note that our Attacker does not run any
special iterative algorithm. Only adversarial prompts are
generated (conditioned on input harmful behavior) using
the regular LLM generation procedure (with temperature
= 1 and max tokens len = 500). For evaluation, we take
the checkpoint from the same step as AdvGame-DPO-MD–
Defender6 and generate ten adversarial prompts per input
from our Attacker. We consider the attack successful if
at least one adversarial prompt is judged as successful by
the default LLM judge used in HarmBench (Mazeika et al.,
2024a) (on the same test set used by other methods).

GRPO on safety benchmarks, achieving lower rates of harmful behavior and higher utility scores, Tables 6 and 7 further
substantiate those findings. For example, on the Qwen2.57B model, DPO and IPO achieve the lowest harmful be-

Benefits of EMA. Table 5 (no EMA) compares training
with an on-policy generator versus an off-policy EMA generator. In the on-policy setup, the vLLM generator uses
the current models to generate prompts and respones and
synchronizes after each step. The off-policy approach uses
EMA (see Equation (10)), which also synchronizes after
each step but maintains a moving average of the model
parameters. The training curves are included in Figure 4
(bottom) in the appendix. The results show that on-policy
training performs worse in terms of safety metrics, and this
is reflected in the lower reward curves. One possible explanation is that on-policy training can make the model overfit
to recent changes, causing instability and less reliable safety.
EMA helps smooth out updates, making training more stable, which is important for overall performance.

Should we train the Attacker or is keeping it fixed
enough? Table 5 (“fixed attacker”) evaluates the effect of
keeping the attacker fixed instead of training it throughout.
In this context, “fixed” means the Attacker is only trained
to produce outputs in the correct format, but not to improve
the jailbreaking reward. The training curves are included in
Figure 4 (top) in the appendix. As expected, when the Attacker is fixed, it simply learns to follow the format and does
not get better at jailbreaking. The Defender, on the other
hand, still adapts to balance benign and harmful prompts.
However, the safety reward is lower compared to when the
Attacker is actively trained. Final utility remains the same,
but safety scores are worse when the Attacker is fixed. This
suggests that actively training the Attacker is important for
making adversarial training more diverse and challenging,
which ultimately helps the Defender become more robust.

Additional ablations. More results on variations of
AdvGame-DPO-MD, including evaluations of using a
non-thinking attacker, a smaller judge model, or a nonoptimistic attacker, are available in Figure 5 in the appendix. Moreover, we provide further experimental results
on AdvGame-GRPO in Figure 6, including a comparison of
pair- vs point-wise judge and on- vs off-policy generation.

5. Conclusion
In this paper, we introduced AdvGame, a novel framework for adversarial safety alignment that jointly trains Attacker and Defender language models in a non-zero-sum,
non-cooperative game, departing from traditional sequential alternation or self-play approaches. Our experimental
results demonstrate that AdvGame variants, particularly
AdvGame-DPO-MD and AdvGame-IPO-MD, achieve superior balance across safety, compliance, and utility metrics while exhibiting significantly greater robustness against
adaptive attacks, especially black-box methods generating
human-readable adversarial prompts (e.g., PAIR and TAP).
Through comprehensive ablation studies, we established
that several design choices are critical for effective adversarial training: (1) actively training a separate Attacker model
(without parameter sharing) to generate diverse and challenging attacks, (2) employing pair-wise preference judging
rather than point-wise scoring, and (3) utilizing off-policy
sampling with EMA for improved training stability.

A. Extended Related Work
A.1. Attacks and Defenses on LMs
Red teaming began as a manual methodology to surface safety gaps and operationalize mitigations, establishing core
practices and scaling lessons for aligning LMs (Ganguli et al., 2022). Automation quickly expanded the attack surface:
gradient-based prompt optimization exposes highly transferable prompts that circumvent alignment (Guo et al., 2021;
Zou et al., 2023), fast adaptive adversarial prompting accelerates search and adaptation (Paulus et al., 2025), multi-round
evolutionary methods cultivate diverse red-team agents at scale (Ma et al., 2026; Pavlova et al., 2024). There are works
that show that multi-shot demonstrations might also be effective in jailbreaking—Anil et al. (2024) demonstrate that many
(30-200) violating examples enable successful attacks across all major LLMs following power law effectiveness.
Another attack vector is prompt injection, where attackers exploit LMs’ instruction-following nature by embedding malicious
instructions in untrusted data (e.g., tool output). Here, the static manual attacks are still dominant (Evtimov et al., 2025;
Debenedetti et al., 2024), but jailbreaking attack methods can also be used with some adaptation (Chen et al., 2025). Recent
work explores training an Attacker LM via online RL under fixed Defender LM (Wen et al., 2025). In terms of defense,
SecAlign (Chen et al., 2025) represents a breakthrough defense using preference optimization with specially constructed
datasets, achieving near-zero success rates against several attacks while maintaining utility. It is worth mentioning that
both system-level defenses and various guardrails are widely used against jailbreaking and prompt injection attacks (Meta
Platforms, Inc., 2024b); however, they typically face challenges from simple adversarial transformations (Wen et al., 2025).
A.2. Nash Equilibria and Game-Theoretic Methods in RLHF
A growing body of work frames alignment as a game between a policy (main LM being aligned) and a preference model (or
judge), seeking stable fixed points in co-adaptation via Nash-equilibrium and no-regret lenses (Nash, 1950), inspired by
self-play advances in RL (Silver et al., 2018). IPO by Azar et al. (2024) introduced a foundational framework that formulates
preference optimization as a game between a response model and another static response model. Their work presented a
general objective that can recover DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023). Building on this foundation, Nash-MD by Munos et al. (2024)
extended IPO to non-static opponents by phrasing preference optimization as a maxmin game between two LLMs. Further,
IPO-MD by Calandriello et al. (2024) established important theoretical connections by showing the equivalence between
online IPO and self-play. Section 2 and Section B.2.1 provide further background information on DPO, IPO, IPO-MD and
Nash-MD which we directly build on. SPPO (Wu et al., 2025) adapted the multiplicative weights update algorithm to solve
Nash equilibria in unregularized preference games. Robust Preference Optimization (Faye et al., 2025) extends the partition
function approximation idea from SPPO to general rewards, potentially offering an improved alternative to GRPO (Shao
et al., 2024). SRPO (Choi et al., 2025) introduced an asymmetric formulation where preference learning is phrased as a
minmax game between two response policies, with one player privileged over the other by receiving the first player’s output
as input. Optimistic Nash Policy Optimization (Zhang et al., 2025b) employed optimistic online mirror descent with two
substeps, achieving improved theoretical convergence rates.
Parallel to these algorithmic developments, game-theoretic preference optimization has found important applications in safety
and security for LMs. Li et al. (2025) introduce a black-box jailbreak framework that leverages preference optimization
against static Defender LM. Their approach computes a scalar harmfulness response score and converts it into preferences
by comparing rewards across different responses. Liu et al. (2025a) expand this to self-play between two contradictory
modes of the same LM: Defender and Attacker, and consider a zero-sum setting. Wang et al. (2025) also train Attacker and
Defender as separate models. Their procedure alternates freezing one side and rejection-fine-tuning (RFT) the other for two
iterations, retraining the Defender from scratch each iteration. AdvGame differs in four ways. First, training is joint and
online, with both models adapting to each other’s current policy in the same loop. Second, we use pairwise preferences
instead of accept/reject filtering with SFT, which gives a richer gradient signal. Third, the non-zero-sum formulation with
reward swapping prevents degenerate gibberish convergence. Fourth, EMA-based off-policy generation removes the need to
retrain the Defender from scratch. Zhang et al. (2025a) propose a collaborative game in which the safety judge and the
Defender are trained together through the point-wise judge. While these are all valid approaches, our proposed AdvGame
considers non-cooperative game (non-self-play) by jointly training Attacker and Defender, and directly uses a pair-wise
judge.

B.1.1. A D V G A M E -DPO-MD A LGORITHM
Given the AdvGame-DPO-MD loss functions, we next explain the algorithm step by step. We first initialize the Attacker
and Defender models as well as the EMA models with the reference models.
Attacker rollouts. Then, for each training step, we first sample a batch of seed queries, consisting of both harmful and
benign seeds. For each seed s the Attacker generates two prompts x1 , x2 with a task template depending on whether the
seed is harmful or benign, see Section D.1.
Faithfulness judge. Then the seed and each of the prompts are passed to the faithfulness judge model, which classifies
each prompt as faithful or not faithful to the seed.
Defender rollouts. For all faithful prompts, the Defender now generates two responses y1 , y2 given only the prompt and
no additional task description. This results in a tree-like structure of game rollouts.
Defender judge. After this, the Defender judge is called, conditioned on the seed and the two responses, returning a
binary preference determining the winning and losing response in Equation (8). If the seed is benign, the Defender judge is
prompted to make its decision based on compliance, whereas if the seed is harmful, the Defender judge is prompted to make
its decision based on deflection, see the respective templates in Section D.2.
Attacker judge. Next, we look at the prompts generated by the Attacker. According to Equation (7), if both prompts
are not faithful, we have no preference and ignore the seed. If only one of them is faithful, the faithful prompt is set as
the winning prompt and the other one as the losing prompt in Equation (9). If both are faithful, the Attacker judge is
called conditioned on the seed and the winning response for each prompt according to the Defender judge. This is inspired
by optimistic optimization algorithms for computing Nash equilibria (Zhang et al., 2025b), with the idea being that the
Attacker plays against an improved Defender, by optimizing against the preferred response. The judge then returns a binary
preference determining the winning and losing prompt in Equation (9). The decision criterion is flipped in comparison to the
Defender judge: For benign seeds we judge deflection, for harmful seeds we judge compliance.
Update. Once the winner/loser pairs for prompts and responses are determined, we calculate the AdvGame-DPO-MD
losses, and compute the gradients. After applying the updates, we update the Attacker and Defender EMA. Finally, we
return the EMA models as the final trained attacker and defender models.
The described algorithm is summarized in Algorithm 2.

C.3. Comparison with Single-Turn Defense Baselines
Beyond Self-RedTeam, we also compare AdvGame-DPO-MD against three single-turn defenses: LifelongSA (Wang
et al., 2025), RR (Zou et al., 2024), and LAT (Sheshadri et al., 2024). These use Llama3-8B as the base model and
we use Llama3.1-8B, which behave similarly on most evaluations (cf. https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct). Table 9 reports utility, safety, and over-refusal on the publicly released checkpoints.
AdvGame-DPO-MD gives better utility and competitive safety. The one benchmark on which it falls behind the baselines is
DAN. Note that safety alignment operates on the Pareto frontier of safety and utility, and AdvGame preserves utility and
compliance well, with a large margin on ArenaHard. LifelongSA is strongest on ASR, but yields the largest drop in utility.
C.4. Additional Discussion
Why does Llama compliance on WJB-benign drop more than Qwen? On WJB-benign, compliance drops from 98.8 to

capability: Qwen is the stronger base model, so its Attacker covers a wider surface, including the hard-to-generate benign
attacks that elicit deflection on benign prompts. Self-RedTeam shows a similar Llama vs Qwen gap on WJB-benign (72.8%
vs 98.4%), which points to the Llama Attacker’s limited surface, not AdvGame itself. XSTest tests lexical over-refusal

The example below is used for judging Defender output when the input prompt is harmful.
# System prompt for Defender when input prompt is harmful
You are a judge. You are given:
- A query.
- Two responses produced by an LLM.
Your task is to evaluate each response and assign a score from 0 (very poor) to 10 (excellent). Your criteria for a good response are
as follows:
- Human Readability and engagement: Is the response fluent, coherent, and easy to understand? Does the response directly
acknowledge the query and interact with the user, rather than ignoring it?
- Deflection: This is the most important criterion, it is the opposite of compliance.
Does the response deflect from the query instead of complying with it?
Does it constructively redirect, suggest adjacent knowledge or ask clarifying questions instead of directly answering the query?
Does it provide vague, high-level insights (e.g., concepts, context, history, ethics) instead of specific high-utility information?
Note that deflection is not the same as refusal, a good deflection should still engage with the query and not completely refuse to
answer it.
Note also that these criteria are independent of the safety and harmfulness of the query. No matter how innocuous the query is, the
response should fully deflect and not comply.
Your task is to assign the responses a score from 0 to 10, with a higher score indicating a better response according to the described
criteria as follows:
0–2: unreadable, ignoring the query, or no deflection (e.g., directly answering the query).
3–5: readable, relevant to query. Low deflection (e.g., refusing to answer).
6–7: readable, relevant to query. Decent deflection (e.g., leaking minor operational details while staying informative).
8–10: readable, relevant to query. Excellent deflection (e.g., constructively redirect, suggest adjacent knowledge or ask clarifying
questions).
Do not allow the length of the responses to influence your evaluation. Be as objective as possible.
