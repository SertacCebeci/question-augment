Systematic reviews – which requires comprehensive evidence collection and synthesis from large document corpora in response to targeted research questions –
are foundational in finance, social sciences, and other technical fields. Manual
construction of evidence tables is labor-intensive, and recent LLM-based assistants
relying on embedding or keyword based search often fail to meet the coverage
standards of systematic reviews. We introduce SLIDERS, a novel LLM-based
methodology for systematic reviews, by automatically assembling evidence tables
tailored to research questions. In addition to extracting structured data from documents, SLIDERS can extract full-text excerpts that serve as direct evidence or
as provenance for structured data. Core to SLIDERS is an automated evidence
reconciliation agent that writes code to analyze and reconcile extracted evidence,
bringing together information fragmented across documents, resolving inconsistencies across excerpts, and synthesizing overlapping findings into a coherent evidence
table. In addition, SLIDERS allows users to ask follow-up questions in natural
language to further explore the assembled evidence. We evaluate SLIDERS on
three systematic-review-style tasks over large document collections. SLIDERS
outperforms the best-performing baseline across benchmarks, remains near 90% accuracy across 6M-11M-token corpora. On two new follow-up analysis benchmarks
SLIDERS can answer 77.9% and 58.3% followup questions accurately.

Introduction

Systematic reviews are a cornerstone of evidence-based research across medicine, social sciences,
policy analysis, finance, law, and other technical domains [1–5]. These reviews answer pre-specified
research questions by collecting and synthesizing evidence from eligible documents [6–10]. The
process includes defining a question, selecting relevant documents, extracting evidence variables,
reconciling evidence across multiple sources, and producing a transparent synthesis with supporting
evidence and caveats [10–14]. Because these steps require sustained expert judgment, manual
systematic review production is highly labor-intensive. One PROSPERO-based analysis estimates a
mean of 67.3 weeks to complete and publish a review [15–17].
We study the problem of automating evidence synthesis for systematic-review-style workflows. Given
a research question and a corpus of eligible documents, the goal is to identify relevant evidence,
preserve enough context to support tractable reasoning, compare findings across sources, and answer
both the original question and follow-up questions. This setting creates two technical challenges: how
to maintain broad evidence coverage across a large corpus, and how to synthesize accurate, detailed
answers from evidence that may be distributed, redundant, incomplete, or conflicting.
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in research, with recent deep research systems [18, 19] surfacing references that manual searches overlook. However, these systems primarily
rely on RAG [20–22], where embedding-similarity search is used to select passages for downstream
synthesis. This retrieval-first approach is poorly matched to systematic reviews. Technical evidence
is often distributed across many paragraphs and documents, and systematic reviews require comprehensive evidence tracking with provenance, not merely a small set of semantically similar passages.
As a result, RAG systems can either miss relevant evidence or retrieve an unmanageable volume
of passages, limiting both transparency and efficiency. Thus, enabling systematic reviews in deep
research calls for a fundamentally new approach to evidence tracking and synthesis.
Chunk-based extraction pipelines[23–25], such as DocETL[24], process documents in segments
and fill structured extraction forms based on user-defined schemas. However, extraction alone does
not solve the evidence-management problem. The resulting records can be large, noisy, duplicated,
fragmented, or conflicting, and they often require source context to interpret. The common approach
is to provide all the collected text to LLMs and prompt them for answers, but this approach does not
scale well to complex questions over large document sets; the relevant evidence may exceed model
context windows, and even when it fits, answer accuracy and reliability can degrade.

Figure 2: SLIDERS for systematic-review-style question answering. Starting from a research question
and document corpus, the system extracts evidence variables into a raw evidence table, reconciles
related records, and answers from the resulting table and supports follow-up analysis.
To address these challenges, we introduce SLIDERS, the Systematic Literature Deep Evidence Reconciling System, an approach for scaling systematic reviews across large document sets. SLIDERS
separates evidence synthesis into four stages: evidence-variable discovery, exhaustive extraction
into the evidence table, evidence reconciliation, and evidence table-grounded answer synthesis, as
shown in Fig. 2. First, given a research question and a document corpus, SLIDERS samples the
documents to identify evidence variables, or data items [10], relevant to the given question. The
evidence variables are not limited to structured data. To handle free-form questions, SLIDERS also
includes free-text variables to preserve all excerpts relevant to the questions.
SLIDERS then fills the evidence table by processing each document chunk by chunk rather than
relying on embedding or keyword search based retrieval. However, evidence in the original documents
2

is often distributed across sections, repeated across sources, partially reported, or inconsistent across
documents. SLIDERS therefore uses an evidence reconciliation agent as its core component, that
prepares the evidence table for question answering. The agent derives canonical keys and groups
related records, producing manageable evidence cohorts that fit within its context windows. These
cohorts allow SLIDERS to compare, merge, and reconcile evidence across the corpus before final
synthesis. The evidence table is then used to answer the original and the follow-up questions.
We evaluate SLIDERS primarily on three systematic review tasks. On the deep-research benchmark,
Browsecomp+ [26], SLIDERS outperforms the best performing baseline by 10 points. On our two
new benchmarks, 100 Wikipedia documents (3.9M tokens) and SEC filings (36M tokens), where
additional related follow-up questions are derived from a high-level question, SLIDERS achieves a
performance of 77.9% and 58.3%, respectively, outperforming the next best baseline. To compare
with methods that cannot handle the scale, we run SLIDERS on benchmarks that fit in the context
window of frontier LMs, SLIDERS outperforms all evaluated baselines up to 16.58 points.

Related Works

Retrieval-augmented generation. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds LLM outputs
by retrieving passages from an external corpus before synthesis [27–32]. Long-context, adaptive,
iterative, graph-based, and hierarchy-based variants improve retrieval scope or organization [20, 22,
33–41]. However, their working state remains primarily retrieved passages, summaries, entities,
or partial generations rather than an explicit, question-specific evidence representation. This is
limiting for systematic-review-style synthesis, where the relevant evidence dimensions may include
temporal regimes, outcomes, caveats, comparison attributes, stated reasons, and source provenance.
Simply expanding context also does not ensure reliable use of all relevant evidence [42]. In contrast,
SLIDERS derives evidence variables from the research question and preserves source-grounded
excerpts for later selection, grouping, comparison, reconciliation, and cited synthesis.
Chunk-then-aggregate pipelines. Chunk-then-aggregate pipelines scale long-document reasoning
by decomposing inputs into chunks, processing each chunk locally, and aggregating the outputs.
This pattern appears in divide-and-conquer summarization [43], LLM MapReduce pipelines [44],
optimized document-processing workflows [24], domain-specific extraction systems [45, 46], and
hierarchical or hybrid retrieval methods [39, 47]. While these approaches make local processing
tractable, they usually reduce chunk outputs directly into a final answer or summary. SLIDERS
instead maintains chunk-level extractions as persistent evidence tables for later selection, grouping,
reconciliation, and cited synthesis.
Long context modeling. Training-based approaches expand model capacity via position-agnostic
training [48], multi-document instruction tuning [49], or representation-level compression into
fixed working memory [50, 51]. Agentic systems instead divide work across roles or recursive
sub-tasks [23, 25, 52–54]. SLIDERS is complementary to these efforts.

The SLIDERS Method

The following first formulates the problem, then describes how SLIDERS generates the variables in
the evidence table, extracts the table, cleans the table, and answers questions using the table.
Task Formulation. Given a research question q0 and a set of documents D, SLIDERS constructs
a reusable evidence table Iq for answering both the initial question and later follow-up questions.
The table has question-relevant evidence variables as columns and document-grounded evidence
records as rows, with each record linked to its document, supporting text span, and provenance. These
variables may contain typed values, such as dates, categories, or measurements, as well as free-text
entries, such as claims, rationales, or descriptions. We implement this evidence table as a database
table, since synthesis often requires operations such as filtering, grouping, aggregation, and joins.
The table is reusable across related research questions. At answer time, a coding agent operates over
the table to reorganize evidence from different sources, reconcile duplicate or conflicting records, and
construct the evidence view needed for the current question.

Generating Evidence Variables for Evidence Table

SLIDERS generates a set of evidence variables from the given question and a sample of the documents.
For example, in financial research, given a question about long-term borrowing, useful evidence
variables may include company, sector, borrowing level, stated reason, debt instrument, and time
period. In healthcare settings, given a question about clinical outcomes, evidence variables may
include intervention, population, outcome, effect direction, measurement, and caveat.
Definition 1. The Evidence Variable Generation Task takes a question q and document metadata M
and produces a set of evidence variables for the evidence table.

vn is the evidence variable name; d is a semantic description; τ is the data type, u is an optional unit

Evidence variables are strongly typed to ensure that the values of each variable are amenable to select,
aggregate, filter, and compare operations. The normalization rules are needed to make independently
extracted excerpts comparable (e.g., currency conversion or date formatting). In addition, for each
variable, we preserve source-grounded text, rationales, and provenance, allowing the synthesis to
inspect the original language upon generating answers.

3.3

Evidence Reconciliation Agent

Once the evidence table has been constructed, the system must turn a high-recall collection of
document-grounded evidence into an answer-ready representation. This is necessary for two reasons.
First, answer-time scale remains a problem: for large document sets, the evidence table may contain
too many records to pass directly to an LLM, either exceeding the context window or degrading
reasoning reliability. Second, systematic-review evidence is rarely organized in the source documents

in the same form required for synthesis. Documents often distribute, repeat, refine, or qualify information across sections and sources, and related evidence may differ in scope, assumptions, time
period, or level of granularity [57, 58]. The raw evidence table preserves these local, source-grounded
records; reconciliation organizes them into coherent evidence units for the current question. We therefore introduce an evidence reconciliation step that groups related records, combines complementary
evidence, preserves meaningful distinctions, solves inconsistencies using extracted provenance and
rationales, and produces an answer-ready view of the evidence table.
Definition 3. The Reconciliation Task takes a question q, evidence variables ∆(q, M), and a raw
evidence table Iq0 produced by chunk-level extraction, and returns a reconciled evidence table:
IqR = Reconcile(q, ∆, Iq0 ).
The system first groups records by question-specific primary-key variables and then reconciles records
within each group. Within a group, the agent merges duplicate records, combines complementary
records, preserves meaningful distinctions, and marks unresolved conflicts. The reconciled evidence
table IqR preserves the provenance spans, excerpts, and extraction rationales of the original records
while organizing them into an answer-ready evidence representation.
Conceptually, reconciliation requires comparing evidence records to determine whether they express
the same evidence, provide complementary information, conflict under the same scope, or should
remain distinct. Let there be n evidence records in the raw evidence table Iq0 . A direct reconciliation
procedure would compare all pairs (zi , zj ) where 1 ≤ i < j ≤ n, requiring O(n2 ) comparisons.
This is infeasible when the evidence table is large.
From database literature [59, 60], keys provide a way to identify which rows refer to the same
underlying object. In our setting, SLIDERS selects a set of question-specific primary-key variables:
columns that define what should be compared for the current question. For example, a financial
question may use company and fiscal year as primary-key variables, while a clinical question may
use intervention, population, outcome, and time point. Formally, the system selects

Facilitating Grouping. Independently extracted rows may refer to the same entity, time period, unit,
or outcome using different surface forms. For example, “J. Smith” and “John Smith” may refer to
the same person. Canonicalization ensures semantically connected records are grouped together for
further reconciliation despite any superficial differences in actual evidence values SLIDERS therefore
canonicalizes the selected primary-key variables before grouping, resolving aliases, normalizing units
and dates, and standardizing values when those distinctions matter for the current question.
Reconciliation Operations. After grouping, a reconciliation agent inspects each group of evidence
records. For each group, the agent decides how the records should be resolved: duplicate records are
merged, complementary records are consolidated, conflicts are inspected against their source spans
and rationales, and meaningful distinctions are preserved rather than forced into a single value. The
agent then uses SQL operations to manipulate the evidence table, producing a reconciled evidence
table IqR . This table preserves the original provenance spans, excerpts, and extraction rationales,
while organizing the evidence into a form that is ready for answer generation.
3.4

Synthesizing Answers

Once the reconciled evidence table IqR has been constructed, the synthesis agent generates the
response from the evidence table rather than from the raw corpus or unorganized chunk outputs.
Importantly, IqR may still contain a large collection of textual excerpts, so the agent uses evidence
variables to retrieve a bounded evidence context E ⊂ Iqc for final synthesis. Each selected evidence
entry contains evidence variable values, free-text excerpts, provenance spans, and extraction rationales,
allowing the agent to verify whether the excerpt supports the intended inference. The final answer
is then generated from a series of inspection of selected evidence context, with citations to the
supporting spans and explicit caveats for missing information, unresolved disagreements, or evidence
that remains ambiguous. More importantly, this evidence table can be used for answering further
questions.

Evaluation on Benchmarks Beyond LLM Context Limits

We evaluate SLIDERS as an evidence-synthesis system for systematic-review-style workflows. In all
tasks, the system is given a research question and a document corpus; the goal is not merely to answer
a single question from long context, but to assemble, reconcile, and use evidence with sufficient
coverage and provenance to support detailed analysis.
We first evaluate on BrowseComp+ [26], a deep-research benchmark which tests whether systems
can identify and synthesize evidence distributed across multiple documents. To test the follow-up
analysis of SLIDERS, we introduce WikiCeleb100 (WC), a new corpus of 100 Wikipedia articles
with a research question and 21 related follow-up questions, designed to test reusable evidence-table
construction and subgroup comparison. WC comprises Wikipedia articles for the 100 most-viewed
celebrity pages from November 2025 to January 2026 (3.9M tokens); the research question is “Who
debuted at the youngest age across the following industries: Music, Film, Content Creation, and
Other?”. It requires extracting each celebrity’s date of birth, debut date, and industry from across all
100 articles. As a larger follow-up analysis benchmark, we present FinQ100 (FQ). FQ comprises
the most recent 10-Q filings of 100 randomly selected SEC-listed companies (36M tokens) with 25
questions. The research question is “Which company has the lowest long-term borrowing?”. This
question is harder because many filings do not state borrowing explicitly, requiring inference from
context. Full descriptions of the benchmarks, construction protocols, and metrics are in Appendix B.
Baselines. We compare against retrieval, base-LM, and agentic baselines; full implementation
details are in Appendix C. BM25 [61], LongRAG [20], GraphRAG [22]: dense / long-chunk / graph
retrieval with Qwen3-4B for indexing and GPT-4.1 as the reader. DocETL [24]: map–reduce with
per-question schemas (GPT-4.1). RLM [54]: recursive task decomposition (GPT-5). GraphRAG and
DocETL become cost-prohibitive (marked $$) for BC+ and FQ, having more than 6M tokens. For
RLM we give all 25 questions for FQ at once and ask it to answer all the questions.

Evaluation on Benchmarks Within LLM Context Limits

To broaden our comparison with techniques that cannot handle documents beyond the size of context
windows in frontier models, we evaluate SLIDERS on three additional benchmarks intended to
test “long-context QA”. FinanceBench (FB) [62] is single-document financial QA over public
filings (150 questions, ≤363K tokens). Loong [63] is multi-document QA where every provided
document is required (∼11 docs per instance, ≤300K tokens, finance/law/papers, English and
Chinese). Oolong [64] (Oolong-Synth, 256K tokens) is an aggregation benchmark where models
must classify individual data points and aggregate them into a global answer.
Baselines. In addition to baselines in Sec. 4, we also evaluate: Base LM: GPT-4.1 and Qwen3.5122B-A10B [65] (1M-token context), CoA [23]: sequential chunk summarization with running
context (GPT-5), and RLM with GPT 4.1 and GPT 4.1-mini as LLM backbone.
Observation 1: Using Evidence Table also improves long context QA accuracy, even when the
corpus already fits in the model’s context window. As shown in Table 3, SLIDERS achieves the best
average performance on the three within-window benchmarks (FB, Loong, Oolong), outperforming
the strongest baseline (GPT-4.1) by 9.54 points on average. The largest within-window gain occurs on
8

Oolong, where every question requires aggregating many local reasonings into a global answer – the
regime in which each excerpt is important or they are widely scattered through the corpus. The gain
shows up even when the input fits in the base model’s window indicates that what bounds accuracy is
whether the retained context is feasible for targeted retrieval, not the raw context length. As observed
in Sec. 4, SLIDERS with Qwen3.5 122B-A10B, stays competitive with SLIDERS using GPT 4.1 and
also outperforms base LMs on Loong, and Oolong.
Observation 2: Each stage of SLIDERS contributes to robust evidence synthesis. Figure 6(a)
shows that removing major components generally reduces performance across FinanceBench, Loong,
and Oolong. The full system achieves the best result on FinanceBench and Oolong, with especially
large gains on Oolong: removing chunk-level extraction drops performance from 64.67 to 40.00,
showing that systematic evidence extraction is necessary when relevant information is distributed
across long inputs. Removing reconciliation also lowers performance on all three benchmarks,
indicating that extracted evidence must still be organized and resolved before answer generation. The
only exception is Loong, where the Direct Table variant slightly exceeds the full system by 0.08
points. The extracted table was already small, additional reconciliation may provide less benefit.
Observation 3: SLIDERS is more stable than baselines as token length increases. Figure 6(b)
compares accuracy across token lengths on Loong and FinanceBench. On Loong, SLIDERS remains
above 70% even at the longest inputs, while RLM drops sharply as the context grows, reaching below
50% at the largest token length. A similar pattern appears on FinanceBench. SLIDERS maintains
high accuracy across longer contexts and remains strongest at the largest input size. These results
suggest that SLIDERS does not depend on directly reasoning over the entire long input.

7

Conclusion

SLIDERS frames systematic-review-style question answering as the construction and reconciliation
of a reusable evidence table, rather than as retrieval over passages or direct long-context synthesis. By
extracting both structured variables and source-grounded excerpts, then using a reconciliation agent
to consolidate overlapping evidence, resolve inconsistencies, and preserve provenance, SLIDERS
produces an evidence table that supports both initial research questions and follow-up analysis. Across
long-context, deep-research, and large follow-up benchmarks, SLIDERS outperforms retrieval-based,
long-context, and agentic baselines, showing that large-corpus synthesis requires not only finding
evidence, but organizing it into a coherent, queryable representation. Future work should further
evaluate the semantic correctness of reconciliation decisions and study human-in-the-loop workflows
for auditing and refining evidence tables. Limitations are discussed in Appendix E.

8

Broad Societal Impact

This work has several potential positive societal impacts. Systematic reviews are central to evidencebased decision making in medicine, social science, policy analysis, finance, law, and other technical
domains, but producing them manually requires substantial expert labor and can take many months.
By automating parts of evidence discovery, extraction, reconciliation, and synthesis, SLIDERS
could reduce the cost and time required to produce systematic-review-style analyses. This may
help researchers, analysts, policymakers, and domain experts examine larger evidence bases, ask
follow-up questions more easily, and produce syntheses with more explicit provenance than standard
retrieval-first systems. In domains such as healthcare and public policy, improved evidence synthesis
could support better-informed decisions, provided that outputs remain subject to expert review.
At the same time, the work also has potential negative societal impacts. Because SLIDERS is designed
to synthesize evidence across large document collections, errors in extraction, reconciliation, or
answer synthesis could be amplified into apparently authoritative conclusions. This risk is especially
important in high-stakes domains such as medicine, law, finance, and policy, where incorrect or
incomplete syntheses could mislead decision makers. The system may also inherit biases from the
underlying document corpus or language models, omit relevant evidence despite its goal of broad
coverage, or overstate certainty when the available evidence is incomplete or conflicting. More
broadly, automation of systematic-review-style workflows may encourage overreliance on machinegenerated syntheses, reduce incentives for careful manual validation, and advantage organizations
with access to large-scale compute and proprietary document collections. For these reasons, SLIDERS
should be used as an assistive tool rather than a replacement for expert judgment, with careful auditing
of extracted evidence, provenance, uncertainty, and failure cases.

Method Details

A.1

Question Decomposition

The original question posed by the user is not directly suitable for all stages of the pipeline. In
particular, extraction and reconciliation are intermediate steps that impose different requirements on
task formulation. Extraction should enumerate all relevant candidate evidence from each document
chunk, while reconciliation should combine these candidates into a globally consistent database.
Conditioning either stage on the original question can cause them to prematurely answer the question
rather than constructing the intermediate representation.
Consider the query: “Return the second poem about the Great Wall of China.” Suppose the first chunk
contains one relevant poem (P1), and the second chunk contains two relevant poems (P2, P3). If the
extractor is conditioned on the original question, it may incorrectly interpret the ordinal constraint
“second” at the chunk level: it extracts nothing from the first chunk (since no second poem exists
locally) and extracts P3 from the second chunk (as the second poem within that chunk). This results
in an incorrect global outcome, since the true second poem across the entire document collection is
P2. Similar issues arise for other global constraints such as superlatives, rankings, and aggregations.
To prevent such premature answer-oriented behavior, SLIDERS decomposes the input question q
into component-specific queries. We generate an extraction query qe that specifies what information
should be identified from each document chunk, and a reconciliation query qr that defines how partial
extractions should be combined and cleaned to form a coherent database. Schema induction and final
answer synthesis, in contrast, operate directly on the original question q, as these stages are explicitly
responsible for capturing the full semantic intent of the user’s request and producing the final answer.
A.2

Context-Aware Chunking

Each chunk retains the raw text as well as its structural metadata, including the current heading
path (e.g., Header 1 → Header 1.1 → Header 1.1.3), the chunk index, the document title, and the
document description. This ensures that every chunk is self-contained, carrying both global and local
context needed for faithful extraction.

B.1

Evaluation Metrics

For BrowseComp+ we use the official evaluation method, reporting binary accuracy for answering
questions. For WikiCeleb100 and FinQ100 we use llm as a judge and partial scoring simlar to
Oolong’s numeric metric. For financebech, we use llm-as-a-judge evaluation and use the official
metrics for Loong and Oolong.
B.2

Benchmark Statistics

As shown in Table 4, the benchmark suite covers a range of document scales, data sources, and
reasoning requirements, from single-document extraction to large-corpus multi-hop retrieval. FinanceBench contains one document per question and focuses on realistic financial extraction and
arithmetic over 150 questions. Loong and Oolong evaluate mid-scale settings: Loong uses roughly 11
real documents per question and emphasizes retrieval and aggregation, while Oolong uses synthetic
single-document inputs for classification and aggregation. The remaining benchmarks stress larger
evidence sets. WikiCeleb100 and FinQ100 each contain 100 documents, requiring aggregation,
comparison, and arithmetic over real-world corpora. BrowseComp+ is the largest setting, with 1,000
documents and 150 questions designed to test multi-hop retrieval and reasoning across a substantially
broader context.
B.3

Benchmark Construction

Question construction begins from a seed query: for WikiCeleb100, “Which artist made their debut
at the youngest age across Film, Music, Content Creation, and Other industries?”; and for FinQ100,
“Which company has the lowest long-term borrowing?” We derive follow-up questions by reusing
and recombining the extracted information, including temporal cohorts (e.g., artists debuting in the
1950s or born in the 1970s) and aggregate financial properties (e.g., companies with no long-term
borrowing or borrowing exceeding $80M).
To annotate gold answers, we use evidence extraction task to extract an initial structured representation,
then manually verify all extracted values against the original Wikipedia articles and financial filings.
Automated extracted is used to serve only as a starting point to accelerate the annotation process;
the final ground-truth was determined entirely through manual verification and correction. We
then manually reconcile the resulting tables to obtain a consolidated database representation of
each document set, and manually author SQL queries for each question. For each benchmark, we
additionally retain five questions that cannot be answered using SQL alone.

WikiCeleb100 and FinQ100 are primarily follow-up analysis benchmarks: they test whether an
evidence table built for an initial research question can support related downstream questions without
reprocessing the full corpus. They also function as large-corpus QA benchmarks, but their main
purpose is to evaluate reusable evidence-table construction.
We acknowledge that these benchmarks favor systems capable of evidence extraction and reuse.
That is intentional: this is the capability being evaluated. However, the evaluation does not give
SLIDERS privileged access to the gold table, and competing systems are free to extract, aggregate,
or build intermediate representations. The fact that removing reconciliation substantially degrades
performance on both WikiCeleb100 and FinQ100 shows that the task is not solved by extraction
alone; large-scale grouping, deduplication, conflict handling, and answer-time use of the table are
also required.
Evaluation. SLIDERS currently assumes that follow-up questions are answerable from the evidence
variables induced for the original research question. If a follow-up requires a missing variable, the
current implementation may fail. A natural extension is to detect missing variables, regenerate or
extend the schema, and run incremental extraction only for the newly required fields.

Baselines

C.1

Baseline Implementations

We compare against three families of baselines: retrieval-augmented methods, base LMs operating
directly on the full corpus, and agentic scaffolding methods. All baselines use the same evaluation
protocol (LLM-as-a-judge with partial scoring) as SLIDERS. Models referred to below are listed in
Table 1 and Table 3 of the main paper.
RAG (dense retrieval). Standard chunk-level dense retrieval. Documents are split into fixed-size
chunks of 4k tokens and indexed with Qwen3-4B as the embedding model. At inference, the question
is embedded and the top-k chunks are retrieved by cosine similarity and concatenated as context for
the GPT-4.1 reader, which generates the answer.
LongRAG. Follows the original LongRAG recipe [20]: longer chunks (16k tokens) are used so that
fewer retrievals are needed and each retrieved unit carries more local context. Embedder Qwen3-4B;
reader GPT-4.1.
GraphRAG. Microsoft’s graph-structured RAG [22]. The corpus is processed through entityrelation extraction to build a knowledge graph, then community summaries are produced bottom-up.
At query time, the system selects communities relevant to the question and uses their summaries as
context. Embedder Qwen3-4B; reader GPT-4.1.
Base LMs. We pass the full corpus directly to the model in a single prompt. We test GPT-4.1
(1M-token context) and Qwen3.5-122B-A10B (1M-token context). No retrieval, summarization, or
chunking is performed.
DocETL. Map-reduce extraction with per-question schemas [24]. The system generates a plan
that maps over chunks (16k tokens) to extract structured fields, then reduces by aggregating across
chunks. Worker and reasoning model: GPT-4.1.
Chain of Agents (CoA). Sequential chunk-by-chunk (16 tokens) summarization with a runningcontext buffer [23]. Each agent ingests one chunk plus the running summary from the previous agent
and produces an updated summary; a final manager agent answers the question from the last summary.
Worker model: GPT-4.1-mini; reasoning model: GPT-4.1 (manager).
RLM (Recursive Language Models). Recursive task decomposition [54]. The reasoning LM
(GPT-5) decides at each step whether to read a sub-document directly or to delegate to a sub-call (its
own recursive instance) on a smaller portion of the corpus; sub-calls return summarized findings to
the parent. Worker model: GPT-5-mini. We use the authors’ default recursion-depth and step-budget
settings. We run RLM for maximum 30 iterations.

D.4

Relevance Gate Analysis

We evaluate the relevance gate on 20 sampled incorrect predictions across benchmarks. Across 516
chunks, 282 were rejected by the relevance gate, with only 1 false negative, yielding a false-negative
rate of 0.4%. We note that this estimate is derived from a limited sample of pipeline-level failures
and may not be fully representative of the gate’s overall accuracy across the corpora and leave it for
future analysis. This indicates that the relevance gate is not a major source of error; most failures
arise downstream (e.g., schema mismatch or reconciliation).
D.5

Multi-Document Summarization Case Study

To demonstrate generality beyond QA, we ran SLIDERS with the prompt “Summarise the given
research papers” on three ML papers from the Loong benchmark. The system induced a 5-table
schema: PaperSummary (title, authors, abstract), PaperContributions, PaperMethods, PaperFindings,
and PaperConclusions, each with paper_title as linking key and free-text fields. It extracted 117
records across 3 papers, then aggregated them into coherent per-paper summaries. Because extraction
runs per-chunk rather than over the full corpus, it avoids information loss from context-window
pressure seen in direct LLM baselines. This case study suggests that SLIDERS may extend beyond
factoid or tabular QA to more open-ended synthesis tasks.
D.6

Issues Discovered by SLIDERS

During error analysis, we identified cases where the ground truth annotations in FinanceBench and
Loong are incorrect. Because SLIDERS traces provenance, we can verify whether a predicted answer
is correct or the ground truth itself is erroneous. We identified two types of errors: (1) answers that are
spread across multiple pages, and (2) incorrect or incomplete annotations. For FinanceBench, which
assumes answers are localized to a single page, we found more complete answers spanning multiple
pages for question IDs financebench_id_02416, financebench_id_00283. For Loong in the
financial domain, we found ground truth entries with missing units and incorrect values; for example,
for a question the annotation says the accounts payable is 1,740 instead of the correct value of
1,663 thousand. This is the same example we provide in Figure 2. These findings demonstrate the
auditability and interpretability of SLIDERS.

Limitations

evidence variable dependence. The induced evidence variable determines what evidence is recoverable at answer time; questions whose evidence does not factor cleanly into typed fields fall back on
free-text columns.
Extraction completeness. The relevance gate trades a small recall loss for a large precision gain, but
rare or implicit evidence the gate underweights could be silently dropped.
Reconciliation errors. Aggressive canonicalization can collapse genuinely distinct entities, particularly in domains with high-cardinality keys or near-duplicate naming conventions.
Latency. The pipeline requires multiple sequential LLM calls, resulting in higher latency (2-3 min
end-to-end) than single-call baselines; this is acceptable for accuracy-critical workflows but not for
real-time applications.

Data Reconciliation

Primary Key Selection Selects primary key fields which are used to group data rows for further
reconciliation.
# instruction
You are selecting **primary keys** for database tables that have been automatically extracted from documents.
## Purpose
Your selected primary key will be used for **downstream data consolidation**:
1. **Deduplication**: Rows with the same primary key will be identified as duplicates
2. **Conflict Resolution**: When duplicates exist, we’ll merge them by selecting the best value for each field
3. **Table Consolidation**: The final cleaned table will have one row per unique primary key
## Context
- The data was automatically extracted from unstructured documents (PDFs, text, etc.)
- Extraction errors create duplicate rows for the same real-world observation
- Multiple extractions of the same data point may have conflicting values
- Your primary key defines which rows represent the "same thing" and should be merged
## Your Task
Select the **conceptual primary key** that identifies unique observations in the ideal cleaned table. When two rows
have the same primary key, they should represent the same real-world observation and be consolidated into one row
.
## Key Principles
### The Key Defines What Gets Consolidated
Your primary key determines which rows will be merged together:
- Rows with **identical primary key values** $\rightarrow$ consolidated into ONE row
- Rows with **different primary key values** $\rightarrow$ kept as separate rows
- During consolidation, we merge conflicting field values by selecting the most reliable extraction
### Separate Identifiers from Values
**Identifiers** define WHAT is being observed - these form the primary key:
- Entity identifiers: company, user, product, document
- Temporal identifiers: date, period, timestamp
- Categorical identifiers: metric_type, event_type, category
- Relationship identifiers: source_entity, target_entity
**Values** are the observed data - these get consolidated during conflict resolution:
- Measurements: amounts, counts, percentages, scores
- Content: text, descriptions, labels, classifications
- Attributes: status, flags, properties
**Rule**: Primary keys should contain ONLY identifiers, never measured values.
Examples:
- Metrics over time: Key = entity + time_period (metric values get consolidated)
- Events with content: Key = entity + timestamp (content fields get consolidated)
- Entity relationships: Key = source + target (the relationship itself IS the identifier)
### Field Preferences
**STRONGLY PREFER business/domain identifier fields** as primary keys.
**Use ‘row_id‘ or ‘document_name‘ as the primary key in the scenarios where other fields are allowed to have duplicates
as in text that represents dataset.**
## Analysis Guidelines
### 1. Understand the Data Domain
Ask yourself: What is this table measuring or recording?
- Financial data: Observations about companies at points in time
- Event logs: Actions by users at timestamps
- Relationships: Connections between entities
- Classifications: Labels applied to items
### 2. Identify the Identifier Fields
Which fields define WHAT is being measured (not the measurement itself)?
- Entity identifiers: company_name, user_id, paper_title, product_id
- Temporal identifiers: date, period_end, timestamp, fiscal_year
- Categorical identifiers: metric_name, event_type, category
- Relationship identifiers: source_entity, target_entity
Exclude value fields from the key: amounts, counts, text content, computed metrics, labels
### 3. Build Composite Keys
Most tables need multiple dimensions to identify observations:
- Financial: entity + time (+ metric_name if multiple metrics per row)
- Events: entity + time (+ event_type if multiple events per entity-time)
- Relationships: source + target

35

- Time series: entity + time + measure_type
### 4. Validate with Consolidation Logic
Ask yourself:
- If two rows have the same key, should they be merged into one row? If yes, good key.
- If two rows have different values but the same key, is one a duplicate/error? If yes, good key.
- Are you including measured values in the key? If yes, reconsider - those should be consolidated, not part of the key.
- Does the key define the "grain" of the consolidated table? If yes, good key.
### 5. Use Statistics as Supporting Evidence
The table statistics (null counts, distinct values) can help validate your choice:
- Key fields should have low null rates
- Key combinations should have reasonable cardinality
- But don’t let statistics override semantic meaning
### 6. Expect Duplicates - That’s Why We Need the Key
The current extracted data will have duplicates with the correct key - this is by design:
- Multiple extractions of the same observation create duplicate rows
- These duplicates will be consolidated using your primary key
- Text variations (spacing, punctuation) will exist in both keys and values
- Your job: identify the conceptual key that groups rows for consolidation, not ensure perfect uniqueness now
