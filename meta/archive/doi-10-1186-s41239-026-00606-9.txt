Abstract
Despite widespread adoption of large language models (LLMs), most students
cannot effectively prompt them. The core challenge is teaching students how
to ask: transforming prompting from trial-and-error guessing into a systematic,
transferable skill. Existing solutions, such as static templates, rule-based hints,
and automated rewriting, either ignore individual learning needs or optimize
outputs without building competence, leaving students dependent and unable
to generalize. ARPG+ is a real-time coaching system grounded in cognitive load
theory and zone of proximal development that senses when learners struggle,
delivers calibrated just-in-time interventions, and fades support as skills develop. The
system tracks learner capability with uncertainty quantification, estimates cognitive
overload from behavioral signals, diagnoses prompt quality across six dimensions,
and adapts scaffolding intensity through a dynamic schedule with periodic skill
probes. A lightweight-deep dual architecture ensures fast responsiveness for routine
interactions while reserving richer analysis for critical moments. Evaluation with
simulated learners shows ARPG+ produces improvements: prompt quality increases
143% beyond unguided practice, learners achieve independence in 91% of final
interactions versus 59% under fixed support, and the approach generalizes to other
domains without retraining. Our work establishes that principled real-time coaching
can improve prompt quality, accelerate learning, prevent cognitive overload, and
foster durable autonomy. All reported evaluations are conducted on LLM-based
simulated learners; the present work does not yet constitute empirical validation with
real students, and classroom validation in authentic educational settings is identified
as a necessary subsequent step.

learn how to ask valuable questions (Zamfirescu-Pereira et al., 2023) across domains.
Teaching the capability to ask is difficult because effective prompting is a metacognitive (Didolkar et al., 2024) practice rather than a fixed recipe. Students must monitor
their understanding, regulate cognitive effort, and choose strategies (Gao et al., 2024)
appropriate to the task and audience. Tasks differ in the number of interdependent elements they introduce, the degree of prior knowledge they presume, and the kinds of
intermediate products they require. If support is too sparse, learners face overload and
give up; if support is too heavy-handed, they become dependent on templates and fail
to generalize. The sweet spot is a moving target that depends on moment-to-moment
cognition (Lundqvist et al., 2024), not just static profiles or course-level objectives. Prior

rely on learners to select and adapt techniques in real time. Automatic prompt optimization (Wan et al., 2024) methods search the space of phrasings to improve task results.
However, they optimize outputs rather than learning and rarely expose why a change
helps or how to reuse the strategy. What is missing is a coach that senses the learner’s
state while they are prompting, intervenes only when it matters, and aligns its guidance
(Ma et al., 2025) with immediate task success and long-term skill growth.
We address the problem by treating prompting as a learnable skill and by building
ARPG+, a real-time, theory-guided coach that senses, decides, and intervenes within
the flow of conversation. The core idea is to operationalize learning-science constructs
into online signals: intrinsic, extraneous, and germane effort are estimated from prompt
structure and interaction telemetry; an overload-risk signal and a struggle index track
when support is needed; and a dynamic estimate of ability maintains a moving boundary
of the learner’s current zone of proximal development (Chaiklin et al., 2003).
To keep guidance both principled and fast, ARPG+ adopts a dual-model design: a
lightweight path delivers sub-turn checks and formative nudges, while quality shifts
or periodic checkpoints trigger a selective deep path. Assistance is governed by a
scaffolding-density schedule that fades as competence emerges but includes periodic
reinforcement to probe for robust understanding. Prompt quality is represented as a sixdimensional vector—structural completeness, semantic clarity, contextual grounding,
task specificity, constraint explicitness, and output conventions—enabling fine-grained
diagnostics and a geometric progress measure. Feedback content and granularity are
chosen to reduce uncertainty about goals and evaluation criteria, and coaching is framed
as sequential decision making: a policy over micro-interventions balances progress with
overload risk and efficiency under safety constraints. Our contributions can be concluded as follows:

Problem formulation and theoretical foundations
Problem formulation

We view a learner–LLM session (Scarlatos et al., 2025) as a sequence of turns
t = 1, . . . , T . At each turn the learner drafts a prompt xt given the dialogue history Ht = {(x1 , y1 ), . . . , (xt−1 , yt−1 )} and receives the model reply yt . The interface
exposes lightweight telemetry τt (e.g., dwell time, edits, revokes, hint clicks) that the
coach can observe. The coach’s task is to decide, in real time and with minimal disruption, whether and how to intervene so that the learner’s prompting skill improves.

Scaffolding theory

St ∈ [0, 1] and couple fading to safety and diagnostic probes. A simple, implementable

The ARPG+ algorithm
Algorithm overview

ARPG+ is a sense–interpret–decide–act–learn loop that runs at each turn of a conversation (Fig. 2). The loop is engineered to be theory-grounded, low-latency, and personalized. Sensing collects only what is needed to act in real time: the current draft xt , recent
context Ht , and lightweight telemetry τt . Interpreting transforms these raw inputs into
compact signals introduced in "Problem formulation and theoretical foundations" section: the six-dimensional prompt-quality vector pt , an overload-risk estimate rt derived
from intrinsic/extraneous/germane proxies, a struggle indicator ζt summarizing interactional friction, an uncertainty-aware capability belief bt , and an estimated task demand
Dt .
Decision-making is organized around two latency tiers. A lightweight path performs
hygiene checks and formative nudges within a strict budget. A deep path is available for
richer analysis and personalization, but it is invoked only when needed. A single trigger variable ξt concentrates computation where it matters by combining abrupt quality
shifts, sustained struggle, safety concerns, and scheduled probes into one decision:

Experiments and evaluation
Experimental settings

The methodology below is a simulation-based stress-test of the coaching logic. It speaks
to algorithmic feasibility, the internal coherence of the regulation behaviour, the contribution of each component, and within-simulation cross-task robustness. It does not,
and is not meant to, speak to educational effectiveness, learner experience, classroom
integration, equity across student populations, or transfer to authentic practice. "Discussion and conclusion" section returns to this boundary in detail.
The decision to evaluate ARPG+ on simulated learners at this stage rests on three converging considerations. Practical. The design space of a real-time coaching system, with
its joint dependencies among cognitive-load signals, ZPD tracking, scaffolding intensity, and feedback granularity, is too large to be explored efficiently in a single classroom
cohort. Simulation provides controlled access to a thousand-learner sample stratified
across cognitive profiles, and supports the ablation and sensitivity analyses that a classroom timeline could not accommodate. Ethical. Deploying an adaptive coach whose
regulation behaviour has not yet been internally validated risks exposing real students to
interventions whose timing and intensity have not been stress-tested; simulation-based
pre-validation is the responsible methodological order. Institutional. The classroom validation described in "An empirical agenda for classroom validation" section, and in particular the cross-level, cross-discipline, cross-language design that the equity question

requires, depends on institutional review timelines, multi-course instructor partnerships, and academic-cycle scheduling that exceed any single revision horizon. We treat
the simulation as a methodological precursor, in the spirit in which pre-clinical work
precedes clinical trials, and not as a substitute for it.
Validating real-time coaching systems requires large-scale evaluation across diverse
learner profiles and task complexities. Traditional user studies face inherent limitations: small samples, uncontrolled confounds, and high costs. We adopt an LLMbased simulation framework that generates data at scale. Each simulated learner is an
autonomous agent characterized by three cognitive traits: initial capability µ0 (starting
proficiency), learning rate α (adaptation speed), and cognitive capacity Ccap (overload
threshold). During interaction, agents maintain dynamic state tracking capability, load,
and performance trends. Interaction patterns—pause durations, editing frequency,
hint-seeking—follow stochastic processes calibrated to human data. We employ a dualmodel architecture separating generation from evaluation: the behavior generator (Doubao_lite) produces prompt drafts based on agent profile, state, task, and feedback, while
the independent evaluator (Deepseek_v3) scores prompts along six quality dimensions
without condition knowledge. We instantiate 1000 virtual learners stratified across three
cognitive profiles with a 1:2:1 ratio, detailed in Table 1.
Each learner completes T = 20 turns across five domains of increasing difficulty:
information retrieval, content generation, data analysis, code writing, and complex reasoning. We compare ARPG+ against three baselines representing current practice. Baseline provides no guidance, establishing a performance floor. Template offers ten static
prompt skeletons accessible on demand, representing non-adaptive tools. Rule implements 50 hand-crafted if-then rules reacting to surface features without load awareness
or capability tracking. All conditions use identical learners, tasks, and protocols; only the
coaching mechanism varies.

Discussion and conclusion
Positioning ARPG+ within the prompt engineering education literature

Existing work on teaching students to prompt occupies three rough positions. Pattern
catalogues and authoring aids treat prompting as a craft to be transmitted (Schmidt
et al., 2024); workshop-based interventions treat it as an AI-literacy competency to be
cultivated through structured instruction (Knoth et al., 2024; Woo et al., 2026); and
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al., (2023) frame it as end-user programming that novices fail at,
exploring prompt designs opportunistically and overgeneralising from single successes.
ARPG+ accepts the third diagnosis but adds an in-the-flow coaching layer that the first
two paradigms cannot provide.
The record for interventions tempers easy optimism. Woo et al.’s 100-minute history-course intervention (n = 27) improved AI knowledge and produced richer postworkshop prompt libraries, but gains in AI self-efficacy were modest and students’
post-workshop prompts closely mirrored the strategies the instructor had walked
through, raising the familiar question of whether what was acquired generalised beyond
the workshop (Woo et al., 2026). Knoth et al. document a similar pattern at the population level: AI-literacy components predict prompt quality, but the gradient varies across
individuals and is not uniformly responsive to instruction (Knoth et al., 2024). Read
together with the Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. diagnosis, the picture is consistent: static, offline interventions reach students at the moment of learning but not at the moment of
failure.
ARPG+ targets exactly that gap. Whether moving guidance into the conversational
stream and fading it on competence actually solves the close-mirroring problem is the
question our simulation cannot adjudicate. We would also note that ARPG+ operationalises only one slice of the broader AI-literacy agenda mapped by Long and Magerko (2020) and Kasneci et al., (2023), namely the production of effective requests. A
system that drove up prompt-quality scores while leaving critical engagement with AI
untouched would have succeeded at the narrow target while failing at the more important one.
Adaptive scaffolding and the question of whether adaptivity actually helps

The premise that adaptive scaffolding outperforms simpler alternatives reads as settled
wisdom, but the empirical record is more uneven than the design rhetoric suggests.
Kulik and Fletcher reported a median ITS effect of d = 0.66 over conventional instruction, with substantial variability traced to test-instruction alignment (Kulik & Fletcher,
2016). Ma et al. found ITS more effective than teacher-led instruction (g = 0.42) but not
significantly better than individualised human tutoring (Ma et al., 2014), and VanLehn
revised the long-standing two-sigma estimate sharply downward (VanLehn, 2011). The
headline message is solid but heterogeneous benefit, not uniform superiority.
Two scaffolding-specific findings deserve direct attention. Belland et al.’s meta-analysis
of 144 STEM studies returned an average effect of ḡ = 0.46 and, more pointedly, found
no advantage for scaffolds with fading over scaffolds without fading (Belland et al., 2017),
a result that gradual-release designers would not predict. Munshi et al. report something
even sharper at the classroom level: adaptive scaffolds produced mixed results, with
strategic hints helping while encouragement prompts did not, and the response patterns
differing between high- and low-performing students (Munshi et al., 2023). Aleven et al.

Page 26 of 33

Ye et al. International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education

(2026) 23:30

summarise a decade of ITS help-seeking research under the title Help helps, but only so
much (Aleven et al., 2016), capturing the same lesson from a different angle.
ARPG+’s scaffolding policy is shaped by these specific constraints: contingency over a
ZPD band rather than time, fading gated on safety, and probes designed to test for robust
competence rather than lock in apparent mastery. Our ablation, in which removing scaffolding drops independence from 0.915 to 0.662, is the strongest internal evidence that
the design is doing useful work. We would not, however, present this as resolving the
broader debate. The Belland et al. result on fading is one that real-world deployment will
need to confront directly.
AI feedback, self-regulated learning, and the autonomy paradox

Feedback has a long-standing reputation as one of the strongest influences on learning, but Wisniewski et al.’s replication of the Visible Learning meta-analyses returned a
medium effect (d = 0.48) and warned that feedback “cannot be understood as a single
consistent form of treatment” (Wisniewski et al., 2020). The heterogeneity was substantially driven by the information content conveyed rather than feedback’s mere presence.
ARPG+’s gap-weighted selection of micro-interventions and its why-this-hint rationales
are designed to make feedback informational rather than merely directive, though the
simulation cannot tell us whether that matters in practice.
The more disquieting evidence concerns what generative-AI feedback does to self-regulation. Fan et al.’s randomised study found that the ChatGPT group’s essays improved
by the largest margin but their knowledge gain and transfer were no better than other
groups, and trace analysis revealed reduced metacognitive activity (Fan et al., 2025).
The authors named this metacognitive laziness, and the label has stuck because it captures a system that moves the proximal outcome while hollowing out the processes that
make learning durable. dos Santos and Birdwell give this its theoretical name: a Zone of
No Development that emerges when AI assistance never withdraws (Santos & Birdwell,
2025).
ARPG+ is best read as a direct architectural response to this concern. The fading
schedule, the periodic probes, the independence-rate metric, and the deliberate withholding of help inside the ZPD band all serve the goal of making sure the system can
stop helping. The 91% independence rate in late-session turns is encouraging on its face,
but it describes what simulated learners do. The mechanisms producing metacognitive
laziness in Fan et al.’s study include affective and motivational components our agents
do not model, and we take seriously the possibility that those could erode the autonomy
gains we observe. The honest reading is that ARPG+’s design treats the autonomy paradox as a first-class concern and that the system can in principle deliver fading behaviour;
whether learners’ self-regulation is genuinely preserved is a question only classroom validation can answer.
Cognitive load in AI-assisted learning

The claim that AI tools should reduce extraneous load is widely repeated but unevenly
supported. Sweller, Van Merriënboer and Paas’s 20-year retrospective on cognitive load
theory reminds us that the value of an instructional move depends on its interaction
with prior knowledge (Sweller et al., 2019), and generative AI complicates the picture
further because evaluating model output is itself cognitive work that did not exist before

Page 27 of 33

Ye et al. International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education

(2026) 23:30

the tool. Martin et al.’s Load Reduction Instruction framework is the most direct attempt
to put this concern on a principled footing, arguing that current generative-AI adoption
fails to manage cognitive burden for novices and offering five corrective principles (Martin et al., 2025). The structural overlap with ARPG+ is considerable: our overload-risk
estimator, granularity dial, ZPD-aware demand estimate, and fading schedule are operational counterparts to LRI’s pedagogical prescriptions.
The cognitive-offloading literature gives a separate reason for caution. Risko and Gilbert document how the propensity to offload is shaped by metacognitive evaluations
that can be systematically miscalibrated (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). A system that reduces
felt effort may also reduce the cues learners rely on to judge their own competence, making the metacognitive calibration problem worse rather than better. ARPG+’s rationale
strings try to preserve the diagnostic signal while offloading operational effort, but we
have no direct evidence this mitigation works. Our simulated learners do not exhibit the
miscalibration patterns Risko and Gilbert describe, and a system that looks well-behaved
on metacognitively well-behaved agents may behave quite differently on the population
it is actually meant to serve. Our simulation reports the load profile a cognitive-loadtheory-informed coach should produce: extraneous load down 64%, germane load up,
total load reduced 29%. This is internal consistency, not evidence that ARPG+ has solved
the AI-and-cognitive-load problem in practice. Whether human learners, with the affective and motivational dynamics our agents lack, would show the same profile is the real
question, and it is not one we can answer from this study.
Scope of evidence and what remains unproven

The four subsections above each end with a localised statement of what our simulation cannot adjudicate. We bring those statements together here. LLM-based simulated learners are a legitimate methodological instrument, with a tradition that predates
the current generation of generative agents (Käser & Alexandron, 2024) and has been
extended in recent agent-based behavioural simulation work (Park et al., 2023; Yuan et
al., 2026). They support a particular kind of inference, and the present work is best positioned by being explicit about that division rather than letting the simulation numbers
suggest more than they can support.
What the simulation does establish is bounded. ARPG+ is algorithmically feasible:
the dual-path architecture operates within the latency budgets of 50 ms (fast path) and
300 ms (deep path) under continuous conversation, and the trigger and policy logic
remain stable across a 20-turn session. When deployed against agents whose cognitive
parameters respond to the relevant signals, the system produces the regulation behaviour a cognitive-load- and ZPD-informed coach should produce, with extraneous load
shifting toward germane load and a scaffolding-intensity trajectory that matches the
schedule by design. The five core components contribute dissociably under ablation,
each leaving its own pattern of degradation when removed. Within-simulation crosstask application from text generation to four other domain families preserves most of
the quality and independence gains. These are claims about system feasibility and simulation-based performance, in the narrow sense those terms invite, and not about educational effectiveness.
What remains unproven is more extensive. Educational effectiveness in any pedagogically meaningful sense has not been established: the agents do not experience the

Page 28 of 33

Ye et al. International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education

(2026) 23:30

affective, motivational, or interpersonal pressures that shape how real students engage
with coaching, and our quality and independence metrics are not classroom assessment.
Learner experience is entirely outside our evidence base. Simulated agents cannot report
whether the interface feels supportive or intrusive, whether why-this-hint rationales feel
illuminating or patronising, or whether periodic probes feel like a meaningful test of
competence or an interruption. Classroom integration is similarly unaddressed: we have
not tested instructor adoption, fit with existing curricula, time-on-task constraints, or
coexistence with other educational tools. Equity is the boundary we want to flag most
explicitly. The three Gaussian profiles in Table 1 are useful as a controlled stress-test of
the regulation logic, but they cannot stand in for the diversity of real student populations
along dimensions that matter for educational equity, including first-generation status,
second-language learning, neurodivergence, socioeconomic background, prior exposure
to AI tools, and cultural variation in help-seeking and tutoring norms. A system that
performs well across three Gaussian clusters of simulated agents is not, by that fact, a
system that performs well across these populations, and we view it as a research priority
to find out whether it does. Transfer to real practice encompasses the rest: the rougher
conditions of unreliable devices, distracted classrooms, low-motivation sessions, and the
edge cases that simulation environments by their nature smooth out.
Our position, then, is that this is the appropriate form of evidence at this stage of the
research programme rather than the end of it. The simulation tells us whether the design
is internally coherent, whether the components do what they are meant to do, and where
the system is brittle under perturbation. It does not, and cannot, tell us whether the system would help real students learn. Classroom validation is the necessary next step, and
its design, including the populations it must include for the equity claim to be answered,
is taken up in the next subsection.
Theoretical contributions and broader implications

Read against the literature canvassed in "Positioning ARPG+ within the prompt engineering education literature–Cognitive load in AI-assisted learning" sections, ARPG+’s
contribution sits at the level of framework and design rather than educational effectiveness. Three points are worth distinguishing. The framework-level contribution is the
operationalisation of cognitive load, the Zone of Proximal Development, and contingent
scaffolding into a single real-time decision loop with calibratable signals. Prior work has
operationalised each construct in isolation (Belland et al., 2017; Kulik & Fletcher, 2016;
Martin et al., 2025); what ARPG+ shows is that they can be combined into a sub-turnlatency control law that decides when to intervene, at what granularity, and when to
withdraw. Representationally, prompt quality is reframed as a six-dimensional diagnostic space within which gap-weighted feedback selection becomes a computable problem,
a more specific commitment than the holistic prompt scoring used in workshop-based
evaluations (Knoth et al., 2024; Woo et al., 2026). Architecturally, the fading schedule,
periodic probes, and independence-rate metric are not generic engineering choices but
a direct response to the autonomy paradox documented by Fan et al., (2025) and named
by Santos and Birdwell (2025); together they instantiate the design principle that an AI
coach must be able to stop helping.
Two broader implications follow for educational technology research. One concerns design defaults: the cumulative weight of the autonomy literature suggests that

Page 29 of 33

Ye et al. International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education

(2026) 23:30

generative-AI support is, by default, sticky, and that disengagement must be designed
in rather than added as an afterthought. ARPG+ offers one instantiation; the larger
point is that fading should be treated as a system-level mandatory feature for this class
of tool. The other concerns evaluation. The field’s habit of judging AI-supported learning systems on immediate output quality, with autonomy and transfer demoted to secondary outcomes, is hard to defend in light of Fan et al.’s metacognitive-laziness finding.
Autonomy-related outcomes deserve promotion to first-class status, and independence
and transfer metrics deserve the methodological attention that quality metrics currently
receive.
The framework also defines its own research priorities. The load proxies and the
struggle index currently rely on heuristic feature extraction, leaving room for learned
representations of these signals. The six-dimensional prompt-quality model is by design
domain-agnostic, and which dimensions should be added, reweighted, or replaced for
multimodal or code-centric prompting remains an open question. Moving from controlled Gaussian profiles to cohorts that include the equity-relevant dimensions of
"Scope of evidence and what remains unproven" section organises the empirical agenda
we take up next.

An empirical agenda for classroom validation

Classroom validation is not a single additional experiment but a multi-phase research
programme. The agenda below is organised by three study types, namely controlled
classroom studies, longitudinal designs, and qualitative analyses, and by four sources
of variation that any credible answer to the equity question must traverse: educational
level, discipline, language, and learner profile.
The natural first phase is a cluster-randomised controlled trial at the classroom level,
comparing ARPG+ against workshop-based instruction (Knoth et al., 2024; Woo et al.,
2026), static prompt templates and pattern catalogues (Schmidt et al., 2024), and an
unassisted control. To meet the equity demand the design needs to span at least three
educational levels, two language cohorts, and a mixture of STEM and humanities disciplines, recognising that the within-discipline variation Knoth et al. document is itself
a methodological signal that pooled effects can obscure. Outcomes should extend
beyond immediate prompt quality to a cold-start transfer task in an unseen domain family and an explicit independence-from-system measure administered after coaching is
withdrawn.
The second phase addresses retention and transfer through longitudinal designs across
at least three time points: immediate, four-to-six weeks, and a full-semester followup. The relevant outcomes are which dimensions of the six-dimensional quality vector
decay first under disuse, whether near-transfer and far-transfer follow distinct trajectories, and whether the strategies acquired with ARPG+ transfer to interaction with other
generative-AI tools. The last point is essential in light of Fan et al., (2025): longitudinal
trace data of the kind their study collected can in principle distinguish a learner who
has internalised the strategy from one whose performance was scaffold-dependent and
is reverting toward metacognitively lazy patterns.
The third phase, no less important than the first two, addresses questions that quantitative outcomes cannot answer. Semi-structured interviews and think-aloud protocols

would document how learners interpret the why-this-hint rationales, whether they perceive periodic probes as meaningful tests or as interruptions, and at what moments they
accept, ignore, or actively resist real-time coaching. Classroom observation and instructor focus groups would surface integration questions, including how ARPG+ coexists
with existing curricula and how teachers calibrate their own role in the presence of an
in-the-flow coach. We anticipate that learner profile, language background, and prior AI
exposure will produce different acceptance patterns, and we would treat such differentiation as a primary finding rather than as noise.
Conclusion

ARPG+ began with a specific theoretical question about whether the constructs that
learning science treats as central to skill acquisition, namely cognitive load, the Zone
of Proximal Development, and the gradual transfer of responsibility through scaffolding, can be operationalised into a single real-time decision loop that runs inside a
learner’s conversation with a generative model. The article has presented a framework
that answers that question affirmatively at the level of system design, evaluated under
simulation-based conditions that allow the regulation behaviour to be stress-tested at
controlled scale. The contribution is best read as a framework and a methodological precursor rather than as an empirical claim about classroom learning, and the next step,
to which we are committed, is the classroom validation whose scope and population
requirements have been laid out in "Scope of evidence and what remains unproven–An
empirical agenda for classroom validation" sections .
