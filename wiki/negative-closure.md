# Negative closure

> The answerer's repertoire for ending the exchange before the question has
> been understood — and the asker's repertoire for resisting it. The stopping
> rule is throughput, not resolution.

## Definition / Mechanism

Ross & Dewdney (1998) name **negative closure**: the moves by which a library[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s4]
staff member ends a reference transaction before the user's need is met. Their
stated mechanism is an incentive claim — bypassing the interview, failing to
ask a follow-up question and making unmonitored referrals occur "when the
primary goal of the library staff member is to satisfy system demands by moving
on to the next question." The paper analyses ten such strategies on the
answerer's side and eleven counter-strategies users deploy to keep the
transaction open.

The **unmonitored referral** is the diagnostic case: the asker is sent
somewhere else — another desk, another library, a catalogue — with no check
that the destination can answer, so the transaction closes with the answerer's
throughput satisfied and the need untouched.

Measured frequencies, on the same accounts that measure whether an interview
happened at all — Nilsen (2004), *Information Research* 9(2) paper 171, the
Library Visit Study write-up, Table 3:

| Behaviour | Physical desks (261 visits) | Virtual desk (40 accounts, 2003) |
|---|---|---|
| Unmonitored referral | 37% (96) | 28% (11) |
| Follow-up question **asked** | ~36% | 30% (12) |
| Reference interview conducted | 49% (129) | 20% (8) |

The source reports the follow-up as an incidence, not an absence; the rate of
*no* follow-up is its complement (~64% physical, ~70% virtual) and is not a
figure the study states. In live chat, referral is rare rather than reflexive:
only 4% (17 of 436) of librarian-initiated clarifications ended in a referral
(Radford et al. 2011).

## Why it matters

1. **The answerer has a stopping rule and it is not about $|W|$.** Every
   stopping criterion the wiki carries ([[stopping-rules]], [[sprt]]) is a
   function of [[conditional-entropy|remaining uncertainty]]. Negative closure is a stopping rule
   whose argument is the queue: it fires while the answerer's uncertainty
   about the question is still maximal, because that uncertainty is what the
   interview would have cost.
2. **It converts a comprehension failure into a delivered answer.** Unlike
   [[abstention]], which withholds and is scored as a withholding, negative
   closure returns something — a referral, an unchecked answer — so the
   transaction is recorded as complete and the failure is invisible to any
   measure taken at the answerer's side.
3. **The asker is a participant in the repair.** Eleven counter-strategies
   means the transaction's outcome is jointly determined; an asker who cannot
   or will not resist (a novice — [[novice-expert-problem]]) absorbs the
   closure silently.
4. **The cheapest counter-move is measured and rare.** Gers & Seward (1985),
   as reported by Radford et al. (2011), found correct answers at 76% when
   "Does this completely answer your question?" was asked and 52% when it was
   not, with the follow-up occurring in 12% of encounters — one of the three
   behaviours Ross & Dewdney name as negative closure when it is missing
   ([[query-clarification-rate]]).

## Transfer

| | |
|---|---|
| **Home field** | a staffed reference desk with a queue behind the asker, a supervisor, and statistics kept per question answered |
| **Here** | any answerer able to end an exchange by emitting something rather than nothing — a referral, a link, a partial answer, a handoff to another agent |
| **What survives** | the incentive account (credit per closed transaction, not per resolved need); the unmonitored referral as the diagnostic move; the joint determination of the outcome by the asker's counter-moves |
| **What does not** | the ten named strategies, which are desk-specific and not fully recovered here; and the frequencies, which are measurements of human library visits and are not a base rate for any other kind of answerer |

## Limits

- The 1998 article was not read in this pass. The ten strategies and eleven
  counter-strategies are known here from its abstract, which names only three
  of them (bypassing the interview, no follow-up, unmonitored referral); the
  remainder are not claimed.
- The incentive claim — system demands as the driver — is Ross & Dewdney's
  interpretation of accounts, not a measured contrast between answerers under
  different throughput pressure. No study read here varies the queue and
  observes the closure rate.
- An earlier draft of this note misstated the 2004 figures twice, as the
  verifier found: it attributed the paper to "Ross & Nilsen" (paper 171 is
  single-authored by Kirsti Nilsen; Ross led the earlier phases of the Library
  Visit Study) and it reported the follow-up rate as a *lack* of follow-up.
  Both are corrected above. The package key for this work is `ross-2004`,
  retained for the study series, not the byline.
- The physical-desk referral figure is 37% in Table 3; the paper's prose gives
  it as "more than one-third". The 4% chat referral rate is therefore not
  evidence that negative closure is rarer online — the desk studies put
  unmonitored referral alone near a third of visits (37% physical, 28%
  virtual), and referral is one of ten strategies.
- The frequencies come from a different instrument than the taxonomy: MLIS
  students reporting on their own visits, self-selected questions, and coding
  by the investigators. They establish that the behaviours are common, not
  that they cause the unmet need.
- Point 1 is this wiki's placement of negative closure among stopping rules;
  neither source frames it as a stopping criterion. Reading the follow-up rate
  as a proxy for the whole behaviour is likewise this wiki's inference: Gers &
  Seward measure answer accuracy against the follow-up, not negative closure,
  and Ross & Dewdney count the missing follow-up as one strategy of ten.
