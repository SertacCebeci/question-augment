# Output-equivalence interleaving

A model that proposes several answers to one request and shows the asker only K of them has to choose which K to show, and the order of its own scores is not obviously the right choice. The paper this note rests on studies the case where the answers are programs and each program can be run: the model writes N candidate programs for one natural-language request, and the first few are what a user will read.[^c14] Because the candidates can be run on the user's data, each one yields an output.[^c11] The mechanism the authors call semantic interleaving works on those outputs. Candidates are partitioned into equivalence classes by the output they produce; within each class they are ordered by score; and the list is then built by taking the top member of each class in turn, then the second member of each, and so on.[^c1] A candidate that gives the same output as a higher-ranked one is moved lower in the ordering.[^c2] The first K slots therefore hold distinct outcomes for as long as the pool contains that many.

The step runs after a filter. Candidates that fail to execute are removed first, so that every surviving candidate has an output to group by.[^c3]

The reason offered is a choice of objective rather than a heuristic. Taking the K highest-scoring programs maximises the sum of their individual probabilities. What matters to the asker is the probability that the intended program is somewhere in the shown set. For two shown programs, that is the first program's probability plus the second's probability multiplied by the chance that the asker rejects the first given that they accept the second. A second program with the same output as the first gets a large discount, because an asker who accepts one will very likely accept the other. The authors say that ranking by plain score "usually does not provide the user a good experience because" the quantity being maximised is a different one.[^c4] Under their assumption that same-output programs are almost always accepted together and different-output programs almost never, the procedure of taking the most probable program in each class not yet shown gives the K-list that maximises the chance of acceptance. The theorem is stated as: "If Assumption 4 holds, then Objective 1 can be met by identifying" a program outside the class of those already chosen.[^c5] The authors' broader point is that the default order of a sampler is "generated by LLMs are not the best candidates for showing to users".[^c6]

The effect is measured on the paper's benchmarks, and it is narrow in one respect. Interleaving does not change the top candidate, so top-1 accuracy is untouched: "semantic interleaving does not change the top candidate".[^c7] For Pandas and SQL it adds "around 10% absolute gain in semantic match accuracy" at the cut-offs reported.[^c8] The gain is "generally smaller for M", the Power Query language.[^c9] The authors read the split as a reversal of the filter's: for well-represented targets, "LLMs do not have difficulty with generating syntactically" valid code, but they rank it less well; for M the difficulty is in producing executable code at all.[^c10] The motivation for showing more than one answer is the ambiguity of the request, which the paper states as a reason K must exceed one: the question can be "inherently ambiguous and thus surfacing only one result" would not cover the user's intent.[^c12]

## Limits

- The theorem is conditional. Assumption 4 is an idealisation with a small constant ε: same-class pairs get a conditional acceptance probability of ε, different-class pairs 1 − ε. The paper proves the result under it. The text does not measure how often a user accepts one of two same-output programs; its metrics are execution and semantic-match accuracy against reference outputs, not acceptance.
- The grouping key is the output on a sample. Execution runs "on the input dataset 𝐷 (or a small sample if" the data is large.[^c13] Two programs that agree on the sample can differ on the full data, so the classes can be coarser than true equivalence (my inference from the procedure, not a result the paper tests).
- Equal output stands in for equal answer. How outputs are compared (order, types, tolerance) decides which candidates merge, and the paper does not study that choice on its own. The grouping also inherits the blindness described in [[output-equivalence-blindness]]: a grader that reads only outputs cannot separate programs that differ in how they reach them.
- With one output class the procedure reduces to the score order, so the step can change a list only when the candidates produce more than one output (my inference from the procedure). How often real candidate pools do so is not reported here.
- The method needs executable outputs, and all three domains have them. Transfer to a question-answering setting without execution is my inference: the output key that drives the grouping would have to be replaced by some other equivalence, and the paper does not supply one.
