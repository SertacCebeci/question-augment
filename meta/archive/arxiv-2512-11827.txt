Assessing Greenspace Attractiveness with ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini: Do AI Models
Reflect Human Perceptions?
Milad Malekzadeh1*, Magdalena Biernacka2, Elias Willberg1, Jussi Torkko1, Edyta Łaszkiewicz2,
Tuuli Toivonen1
Abstract
Understanding greenspace attractiveness is essential for designing livable and inclusive urban
environments, yet existing assessment approaches often overlook informal or transient spaces
and remain too resource-intensive to capture subjective perceptions at scale. This study examines
the ability of multimodal large language models (MLLMs), ChatGPT GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Haiku,
and Gemini 2.0 Flash, to assess greenspace attractiveness similarly to humans using Google
Street View imagery. We compared model outputs with responses from a geo-questionnaire of
residents in Łódź, Poland, across both formal (e.g., parks, managed greenspaces) and informal
(e.g., meadows, wastelands) greenspaces. Survey respondents and models indicated whether
each greenspace was attractive or unattractive and provided up to three free text explanations.
Analyses examined how often their attractiveness judgments aligned and compared their
explanations after classifying them into shared reasoning categories. Results show high AI–
human agreement for attractive formal greenspaces and unattractive informal spaces, but low
alignment for attractive informal and unattractive formal greenspaces. Models consistently
emphasized aesthetic and design-oriented features, underrepresenting safety, functional
infrastructure, and locally embedded qualities valued by survey respondents. While these
findings highlight the models’ potential for scalable pre-assessment, they also underscore the

1. Introduction
Greenspaces provide multiple ecosystem services and benefits to human health and wellbeing,
especially in urban areas where the majority of the global population lives (Frumkin et al., 2017;
Markevych et al., 2017). Recent research has moved beyond questions of greenspace availability
to examine their quality and attractiveness (van den Berg et al., 2015), understood here as the
extent to which individuals willingly choose to visit and spend time in a greenspace that meets
their needs, expectations, and preferences (Biernacka and Kronenberg, 2018). Scholars have
assessed greenspace attractiveness through a range of approaches, including surveys,
participatory and mental mapping and interviews (Biernacka and Kronenberg, 2018; Otto et al.,
2024), as well as data-driven techniques that integrate expert evaluations with remote sensing
and street-level imagery (Zhang et al., 2023).
However, evaluating attractiveness across different forms of urban greenery remains a challenge,
as these spaces vary greatly in their management, appearance, and social meaning (Biernacka
and Kronenberg, 2018). Formal greenspaces, such as parks, managed forests, cemeteries, and
street greenery, are typically maintained by municipal authorities and designed to provide
structured recreation and aesthetic value. In contrast, informal greenspaces, including vacant lots,
abandoned brownfields, and self-regenerating areas, are often unmanaged, less accessible, and

2

more diverse in their ecological and visual qualities. Although both contribute to urban resilience
and ecological functioning, they tend to evoke distinct perceptions and expectations among city
residents (Biernacka and Kronenberg, 2018). Understanding which features make these varied
greenspaces attractive to diverse demographic groups is therefore essential for planners seeking
to design livable, inclusive, and ecologically balanced cities.
Recently, generative AI tools, especially multimodal large language models (MLLM), have
emerged as a promising technique to assess to assess the attractiveness and quality of greenery
and more broadly of urban environments. MLLMs have shown strong capabilities in vision–
language tasks, including generating evaluations and descriptions of images in response to
simple textual prompts (Liang et al., 2024). To assess attractiveness, a quickly growing number
of studies have compared AI evaluations to human perception and shed light on the internal
reasoning processes of the MLLMs models, which largely remain obscure. For example,
Malekzadeh et al., (2025) compared evaluations of urban attractiveness generated by ChatGPT-4
from street view imagery against the perceptions of residents and tourists and found a promising
agreement between the assessments, but also spatial mismatches. In a similar fashion, Wedyan et
al., (2025) assessed MLLMs capabilities in estimating walkability finding both alignment and
notable difference between the human and computer-based assessments, while (Ki et al., 2025)
proposed a framework for integrating MLLMs to support walkability assessments in planning.
Building on these methodological advances in urban design and environmental quality research,
it is timely to ask whether MLLMs can be used to complement people’s assessments of
greenspace attractiveness.
It appears clear that MLLMs have potential to address several limitations of the more established
greenspace evaluation methods. First, research on greenspace attractiveness has predominantly
3

concentrated on formal greenspaces, while informal or transient ones remain underexplored,
despite their importance for everyday urban experiences. This imbalance means that public
preferences for informal spaces are often overlooked in planning and policy contexts (Biernacka
& Kronenberg, 2018). Second, existing assessment methods, whether based on audits (Biernacka
et al., 2020) or participatory surveys and their geo-enabled extensions such as Public
Participation GIS (PPGIS) (Łaszkiewicz et al., 2025), tend to be resource-intensive and difficult
to scale (Knobel et al., 2019). Given these challenges, MLLMs offer a promising opportunity for
scalable and relatively low-effort evaluations of greenspace attractiveness, capable of
encompassing both formal and informal environments and supporting more inclusive greenspace
management and planning.
Yet to date, exploring the applicability of MLLMs for evaluating human perceptions of urban
environments, particularly in the context of greenspaces, remains a emerging field, largely due to
their novelty. In particular, there is gap in understanding the reasoning processes of these models,
specifically their ability to explain why certain environments are perceived more positively than
others, rather than merely classifying them as such. In the context of greenspace attractiveness, it
remains unexplored how well and reliably MLLMs can capture the multisensory nature
experiences in different spatial contexts and the diverse features that affect attractiveness.
Similarly, it remains open whether they can adequately represent differences in perceptions and
values between demographic groups as hidden gender and other biases remain a common
problem for AI models (Hall and Ellis, 2023). We also do not know whether MLLMs are more
suitable for evaluating the attractiveness of large, formal greenspaces compared with more
fragmented and smaller informal greenspaces.

4

We employed three state-of-the-art MLLMs, including ChatGPT GPT4o, Claude 3.5 Haiku, and
Gemini 2.0 Flash together with a stratified geo-questionnaire collected from Lodz, Poland to
compare AI-generated evaluations of urban greenspace attractiveness to human perception. More
specifically, we compared respondents’ binary and descriptive assessments of the attractiveness
of different urban greenspace categories with the classifications and open-ended reasoning
generated by three MLLMs. As the input for the AI models, we used local street view imagery
collected from the locations mapped by the geo-questionnaire respondents. In our analysis, we
distinguished between formal and informal greenspaces and used a role-playing approach in
which the MLLMs were prompted to first employ the perspective of a generic citizen and then
the socio-demographic profile of the individual participant who had evaluated the greenspace.
Finally, we decoded the AI evaluations using semantic segmentation.
The main contribution of our study lies in demonstrating how multimodal large language models
can approximate human reasoning about greenspace attractiveness through visual–text
evaluation.
2. Methodology
2.1. Overview
While human experiences of greenspaces are inherently multisensory and shaped by direct
interaction with the environment (Thibaud, 2011), AI models require a visual proxy to process
and interpret such environments. To bridge this gap, we employ Street View Imagery (SVI) as a
standardized visual input.
Human participants were asked to identify greenspaces in the immediate vicinity of their place of
residence that they perceived as attractive or unattractive and to provide open-ended explanations

5

for their assessments. In parallel, we designed prompts for three MLLMs—OpenAI’s ChatGPT
GPT-4o, Anthropic’s Claude 3.5 Haiku, and Google’s Gemini 2.0 Flash—using the same
introductory context and framing as provided to participants. Each model was prompted in two
ways: (1) from the perspective of a generic citizen of Łódź, Poland, and (2) from the perspective
of a hypothetical citizen whose socio-demographic profile matched that of the participant
evaluating the same greenspace. In both cases, the models generated free-text explanations for
their judgments of greenspace attractiveness.
We systematically compared the explanatory reasoning of the AI models and human participants
using both semantic clustering and manual categorization approaches. In addition, recognizing
the proprietary and opaque nature of these commercial AI models, we conducted supplementary
image analysis to explore the visual features potentially influencing the models' assessments. The
following sections detail the methodological steps of this process.
2.2. Study Area
The study was conducted within a 500 m buffer around the Jasień and Karolewka rivers in Łódź,
Poland, the country’s fourth largest city, with 645,693 inhabitants in 2024 and a total area of 293
km² (Central Statistical Office of Poland, 2025). Historically, the city’s rivers and surrounding
forests supported the rapid development of the textile industry in the 19th century, but
industrialisation also led to severe environmental degradation, with many rivers now channelled
underground (Kronenberg et al., 2017). Today, green spaces in Łódź occur in a variety of forms,
ranging from highly maintained parks to unmanaged wastelands (Biernacka et al., 2023). The
selected river valleys capture the full spectrum of green space types and urban structures found
across the city. Their western sections feature suburban housing, transport infrastructure, and
extensive informal green spaces; the central sections include dense residential areas, formal
6

parks, and industrial zones; and the eastern sections contain agricultural fields interspersed with
informal greenery. Although the rivers themselves are relatively short (12.6 km and 3 km), the
surrounding 30.77 km² study area, representing about 11% of the city’s total area, covers an
urban–peri-urban gradient characteristic of Łódź (Supplementary Materials I - Figure S1).
Greenspaces occupy approximately 59% of this area, inhabited by around 17% of the city’s
population (about 112,000 residents) (Biernacka et al., 2023). This makes the study area well
suited for analysing residents’ perceptions of both formal and informal green spaces.
2.3. Human Assessment of Greenspace Attractiveness
To explore how residents perceive the attractiveness of both formal and informal UGS, we
conducted a household survey among individuals living within 500 m of the Jasień and
Karolewka river corridors in Łódź. The survey targeted a representative sample of residents (N =
407) and was carried out by trained interviewers from an external research company between
November 2019 and January 2020.
A stratified random sampling procedure was used. The Jasień Valley study area was divided into
eight strata based on territorial divisions defined by the research team, and the number of
participants selected from each stratum was proportional to its estimated population size. In total,
2,000 addresses were randomly selected. Interviews were conducted face-to-face at participants’
homes. If a selected resident was not available or declined participation, interviewers proceeded
to the next randomly selected address within the same stratum. The survey achieved a 32.2%
response rate, with an average interview duration of approximately 19 minutes.
The questionnaire included questions on the use and perception of UGS, as well as respondents’
socio-economic characteristics, including education level (primary, lower secondary, vocational,
secondary, higher), job status (student, employed, pensioner, unemployed, other), number of
7

children (none, one, two or more), gender (men, women, other), and birth year. A central
component was a geo-questionnaire, in which respondents identified and evaluated green spaces
in the vicinity of their home, whether formal (e.g., parks, forests, allotment gardens) or informal
(e.g., neighborhood green spaces, waterside green spaces, brownfields), as attractive or
unattractive, and provided up to three reasons for their assessment. Each selected green space
was geolocated by placing a point on an interactive map. In total, respondents identified 210
formal and 36 informal sites as attractive, and 44 formal and 72 informal sites as unattractive.
The responses were originally in Polish and were translated into English using one of the Polishspeaking members of the research team.
2.4. AI Evaluation of Urban Greenspaces
2.3.1 Data
We obtained panoramic Google Street View (GSV) imagery for the greenspaces identified by
survey respondents and the areas in their immediate vicinity, using the Google Maps API
authorized through an API key. For formal greenspaces, image collection was confined to the
official park boundaries. In contrast, for informal greenspaces lacking clearly defined perimeters,
we applied a 100-meter buffer around each point mapped by the participants to delineate the area
of interest. To ensure comprehensive coverage of the surrounding environment in both cases, we
added an additional 10-meter buffer to the resulting polygons. To systematically sample the
spatial extent of each greenspace, we generated a regular grid of points within the buffered
polygons, using a spacing of 50 m between points. This was operationalized by iteratively
creating points at fixed intervals along the x and y axes within the bounding box of each
polygon, and retaining only those points falling within the polygon’s boundaries. Each of these
interior points served as a query location for GSV image acquisition. Each GSV image was
8

downloaded at a resolution of 640 × 640 pixels, with a horizontal field of view of 60° and a pitch
angle of 0°. For each location, we assembled a panoramic 360° image by capturing six
directional images oriented at 0°, 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, and 300°, with 0° facing true north.
Given the input limitations of the AI models, a maximum of 10 images was assigned to each
participant-evaluated greenspace, regardless of whether it was formal or informal. For cases,
particularly in large formal parks, where more than 10 images were available, we manually
selected a representative subset. This selection aimed to encompass a diverse range of park
features and functions, including but not limited to social infrastructure, playgrounds, and seating
areas. This approach was necessary because participants typically evaluated entire parks rather
than specific locations within them, necessitating a holistic and representative visual input for the
AI models.
2.3.2 Models and Prompts
We employed three state-of-the-art MLLMs to assess SVI: OpenAI’s ChatGPT GPT-4o,
Anthropic’s Claude 3.5 Haiku, and Google’s Gemini 2.0 Flash. These models were selected
based on their widespread use, established multimodal capabilities, and the availability of both
user-friendly interfaces and scalable application programming interfaces (APIs), which allowed
for scalable and automated querying at the required volume.
Our prompt design aimed to replicate the human evaluation process as closely as possible.
Specifically, we formulated prompts that mirrored the instructions and framing provided to
human participants. However, to elicit the most contextually relevant responses from the models,
we implemented a role-playing approach in the prompt design. In the first approach, each model
was instructed to assume the role of a generic citizen of Łódź, which in practice meant that the
prompt simply referred to a “citizen of Łódź” without providing any additional demographic
9

details. In the second approach, the models were prompted to adopt the socio-demographic
profile of the individual participant who had evaluated the same greenspace. This allowed us to
investigate whether the adoption of a personalized demographic context influenced the model’s
evaluation and reasoning.
In both approaches, we explicitly defined the concept of “greenspace attractiveness” and
instructed the models to provide (1) a binary judgment indicating whether the greenspace was
attractive or unattractive, and (2) three free-text reasons explaining their evaluation. The
responses were unconstrained by predefined categories to align with the open-ended nature of
the human participants' answers. The exact wording of the prompts for both conditions is
provided in Supplementary Material II.
All models were queried using a maximum of 10 images per location, corresponding to the
models’ input limitations. The temperature parameter, which influences response creativity and
variability, was set to 0.5 to balance coherence and variability in the outputs. Each prompt was
submitted as an independent, single-turn query; we did not employ multi-turn conversational
prompting or context chaining (commonly referred to as "single-shot" prompting).
Following the retrieval of model outputs, we performed a standardized cleaning process to
remove extraneous text and ensure that the responses adhered to the requested format. Some
models occasionally added superfluous commentary or explanations beyond the specified
structure. The final dataset consisted of six distinct responses for each location, reflecting three
models and two prompt conditions per model (generic and personalized). Examples of model
outputs and the extraneous explanatory text removed during the cleaning process are provided in
Supplementary Materials III.

2.5. Comparison of Human and AI Assessments
The comparison between human and AI evaluations was conducted in two stages. First, we
assessed the level of agreement between human participants and the AI models regarding
whether each greenspace was perceived as attractive or unattractive on a one-to-one basis.
Second, we examined the underlying reasons provided for these evaluations, comparing the
explanatory statements generated by the models with those expressed by human participants.
Given that both human and AI responses were open-ended free-text, we applied two
complementary analytical approaches to assess the similarity of these explanations. The first
approach involved semantic clustering of the textual responses using unsupervised machine
learning techniques. While the semantic clustering approach provided an initial overview of the
reasoning patterns, it did not capture the full contextual meaning of the responses. This was
likely due to the complexity of natural language explanations, the diversity of vocabulary used
by participants and models, and the challenges inherent in clustering short, context-dependent
sentences. To address this limitation and enable a more interpretable comparison, we performed a
manual categorization of all explanatory reasons. The detailed methodology and results of the
semantic clustering analysis are provided in Supplementary Materials IV.
2.4.1 Manual Categorization of Explanatory Reasons
Each reason provided by participants and AI models was reviewed and assigned to one of twelve
predefined categories, which are listed in Table 1. These categories were developed inductively
based on recurring themes observed in both the human and AI-generated responses, and refined
through iterative discussion among the research team to ensure conceptual clarity and
distinctiveness.

2.6. Decoding AI Models’ Outputs

As the AI models used in this study are proprietary systems, their internal architectures and
multimodal reasoning processes, particularly in the context of visual perception and scene
evaluation, remain opaque. While these models demonstrate considerable capabilities in object
recognition and spatial reasoning (Chen et al., 2024), the specific mechanisms through which
they derive judgments of greenspace attractiveness are not transparent. To investigate this blackbox behaviour, we conducted an exploratory image analysis focusing on the visual features
present in the Street View imagery used for AI evaluation.

We first applied semantic segmentation to quantify the relative proportions of key environmental
features within each image. Specifically, we extracted the pixel coverage of five primary object
classes: greenery, sky, road, sidewalk, and buildings. For each class, we calculated its proportion
relative to the total pixel count of the image, yielding a normalized measure of its visual
prominence within the [0, 1] range. While semantic segmentation performs well in identifying
larger environmental elements, it is less appropriate for smaller, countable objects, such as
benches or individual people, where frequency, rather than area, is a more meaningful metric. To
13

capture these smaller-scale but perceptually relevant elements, we additionally employed
instance segmentation targeting five object categories: vehicles, decorative elements, urban
furniture, people, and lighting/signage. This allowed us to count the number of distinct objects
detected within each image. To ensure comparability between the count-based and area-based
measures, we normalized all values using a min–max scaler, rescaling object counts to the [0, 1]
range consistent with semantic segmentation proportions. Because min–max scaling is sensitive
to outliers, values exceeding the mean + 2 standard deviations were excluded prior to
normalization. A full mapping of ADE20K classes to the aggregated categories used in both
semantic and instance segmentation and examples of segmented scenes is provided in
Supplementary Material V.

The semantic segmentation task was completed using the NVIDIA SegFormer-B5 model,
specifically the “nvidia/segformer-b5-finetuned-ade-640-640” variant (Xie et al., 2021). For the
instance segmentation, we employed the “oneformer_ade20k_dinat_large” variant of the
OneFormer model (Jain et al., 2023). Both models are pre-trained on the ADE20K dataset (Zhou
et al., 2017) and are publicly available.
The segmentation analysis was conducted on the Finnish CSC’s Puhti supercomputer, using
Nvidia V100 GPUs. The resulting pixel-level classifications for each image were aggregated and
used as explanatory features to explore associations between visual content and the reasoning
categories generated by the AI models.

reflects two factors: first, informal greenspaces often lack the conventional design elements that
models are trained to associate with attractiveness; second, SVI often captures these spaces from

unattractive informal greenspaces (up to 87.5%), suggesting that models effectively detect

greenspaces, agreement remains low (13.6%–25.0%), indicating that models tend to default to a

Taken together, these findings indicate that MLLMs perform well when evaluating visually clear,
formal environments and when detecting unattractiveness in informal greenspaces. However,
they struggle with the subjective and often less visually explicit attractiveness of informal
greenspaces, partly due to the limitations of street-level imagery as the input source.
3.2. Manual Categorization of free-text responses
When examining the distribution of categories across the models, several patterns emerge
(Figure 1). Comparing AI-generated and participant responses reveals several key discrepancies.
The most notable is safety, which participants frequently mention, particularly for unattractive
greenspaces, but which AI models almost entirely overlook. For attractive formal greenspaces,
AI models emphasize infrastructure for walking and nature, whereas participants place
comparatively less emphasis on these features. In attractive informal greenspaces, AI models
focus on aesthetics, while participants more often mention infrastructure for physical activity and
play, highlighting the importance of functional use over visual appeal.
In unattractive greenspaces, aesthetics emerges as the dominant reason across both formal and
informal areas for all groups. However, while safety remains a concern for participants,
particularly in formal greenspaces, it is largely absent from the models’ explanations. In contrast,
AI models frequently cite other factors, such as infrastructure for walking, nature, and social
infrastructure, that participants mention far less in unattractive contexts.

4. Discussion
Our study compared greenspace attractiveness evaluations generated by three state-of-the-art
multimodal large language models with human perceptions collected through a local geoquestionnaire in Łódź, Poland. Based on SVI and open-ended evaluation tasks, we assessed both
21

agreement on binary attractiveness judgments and similarity in the explanatory reasoning across
formal and informal greenspaces, revealing systematic biases in how AI interprets formal and
informal urban nature.
We found that AI–human agreement followed a clear pattern across greenspace types. In formal
greenspaces, models consistently tended to classify areas as attractive, as they reliably identified
conventional park features such as greenery, walking infrastructure, and scenic layout. This
tendency resulted in high agreement for attractive formal greenspaces but low agreement for
formal greenspaces that participants rated as unattractive. In informal greenspaces, where
attractiveness often depended on functional qualities or experiential values less visible in
imagery, models typically judged these areas as unattractive. Consequently, agreement was low
for attractive informal sites and high for unattractive ones. Across contexts, models
overemphasized aesthetic cues and underrepresented functional infrastructure, especially in
informal spaces, reflecting a potential bias towards curated, design-oriented environments.
Furthermore, the negligible differences between generic and personalized prompts suggest that
model outputs remain primarily image-driven, with limited sensitivity to socio-demographic
framing. Beyond these socio-demographic factors, human perceptions of green spaces are also
shaped by individual experiences, abilities, and emotional associations, which influence what
people notice and value (Wolff et al., 2022). This inherent subjectivity remains difficult for AI
models to capture.
The strong agreement we observed for attractive formal greenspaces aligns with Malekzadeh et
al., (2025), who found that MLLMs achieve their highest performance when visual inputs
conform to conventional design features. This pattern suggests that such models may be
implicitly tuned, through their training data, to recognize and value curated, well-maintained
22

environments that dominate online image repositories, where informal spaces are rarely depicted
or explicitly labelled as greenspaces (Pietrzyk-Kaszyńska et al., 2017). The overemphasis on
aesthetics in our results reflects patterns reported in recent computer vision–based urban form
studies (Vanky and Le, 2023), where visual appeal frequently outweighs functional or social
qualities in model outputs. The low agreement observed for attractive informal greenspaces
supports the critiques of Biernacka and Kronenberg, (2018) and van den Berg et al., (2015), who
argue that the willingness to spend time in a greenspace, and its perceived attractiveness, often
stems from experiential, functional, and locally embedded qualities, as well as prior knowledge
or perceptions of the place, that are not readily discernible in visual imagery alone.
Unlike findings from text-only LLM research where user profiling can influence responses
(Marvin et al., 2024), our study found minimal effect of socio-demographic framing in visual–
text evaluations, suggesting that current MLLMs rely predominantly on visual pattern
recognition with limited integration of social context. The concentration of AI reasoning within a
narrow set of categories such as nature, aesthetics, infrastructure for walking, and social
infrastructure, supports critiques by (Belaroussi, 2025; Malekzadeh et al., 2025) that MLLMs
employ a constrained conceptual vocabulary when interpreting urban spaces. While such
consistency may facilitate standardization, it risks overlooking place-specific cultural or
historical factors shaping perception. Without targeted training and prompt design, these models
may perpetuate a “design-centric” bias rooted in their reliance on visual cues alone. Lacking
awareness of social or historical context, they equate what is visually orderly or well-maintained
with positive quality, while failing to recognize less visible dimensions of urban equity, such as
safety, accessibility, inclusion, or cultural significance, that shape how places are actually
experienced.

23

Despite their current limitations, our findings point to the growing potential of multimodal AI
models as supportive tools in urban and landscape planning. While they cannot capture the lived,
multisensory, or social dimensions of place, such models can already assist planners in
identifying visual patterns of neglect or appeal, mapping perceived attractiveness at scale, and
simulating how design modifications might influence public perceptions. Off-the-shelf AI
systems can lower the technical threshold for integrating data-driven insights into planning,
supporting more iterative, participatory, and visually grounded decision-making (Fu et al., 2025;
Malekzadeh, 2026). Working alongside local expertise and citizen input, these models can
support planners by assessing practical design options and creating clear visual or textual
previews, helping planners align spatial design with the diverse ways people experience and
value urban greenery.
5. Limitations and Future Research
Our use of SVI restricts evaluations to street- or route-visible perspectives, which may obscure
or misrepresent key qualities of informal or enclosed greenspaces. A more comprehensive
assessment would benefit from incorporating more inclusive visual coverage. Another limitation
relates to the image cap imposed by the AI models, which allowed us to input only ten images
per greenspace regardless of its size. For larger greenspaces, different respondents may have
evaluated the same site differently because they experienced distinct sections of it; however, as
these were officially classified under a single park name, participants referred to them
collectively. Although for these sites, we manually selected images to capture the most salient
and diverse features, many areas inevitably remained undocumented, potentially affecting the
results. Future MLLMs with higher input capacity could accommodate more extensive imagery,
providing a fuller representation of large and spatially heterogeneous greenspaces. Finally, our

24

analysis was limited to a single city, which constrains the generalizability of the findings.
Replicating the study across cities and countries with different types of green infrastructure,
cultural expectations, and perceptual norms would help assess whether the observed AI–human
agreement patterns hold in other contexts.
6. Conclusion
Our comparison of AI-generated and human evaluations of greenspace attractiveness shows both
the promise and the present constraints of multimodal large language models in capturing the
qualities that make urban nature inviting. The models proved capable of aligning with human
perceptions when visual cues were clear and conventional, yet struggled when attractiveness
depended on more subtle, functional, or experiential aspects. This gap is expected; human
judgments are shaped by multisensory, cultural, and contextual knowledge that remains largely
inaccessible to current AI systems relying solely on imagery.
The key takeaway is that MLLMs can already serve as a practical, scalable pre-assessment tool,
particularly for identifying strengths and weaknesses in visually explicit environments. Yet, for
decisions that demand a fuller understanding of how people experience urban nature, they should
be embedded within participatory and context-rich approaches. As these technologies improve,
their greatest value for planning and landscape management will come from complementing,
rather than replacing, the diverse human perspectives that ultimately define what makes a
greenspace truly attractive.

Supplementary Materials IV - Semantic Clustering of Explanatory Reasons
To compare the explanatory reasons provided by participants and AI models, we employed a
semantic clustering approach. This involved transforming the free-text responses into vector
representations, applying dimensionality reduction for visualization and clustering, and
subsequently grouping similar explanations into thematic clusters.
We first embedded the textual responses using the Sentence-BERT model, specifically the "allmpnet-base-v2" variant (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019). This transformer model was selected
after preliminary tests comparing multilingual and English-only models, including LaBSE (Feng
et al., 2020) and LASER (Artetxe and Schwenk, 2019), which showed limited performance on
our Polish-English mixed corpus due to reduced accuracy on smaller or domain-specific datasets.
The all-mpnet-base-v2 model provided a better semantic representation for our predominantly
English AI outputs and manually translated participant responses.
Following vectorization, we explored several dimensionality reduction techniques for preparing
the data for clustering and visualization, including UMAP (McInnes et al., 2018) and t-SNE
(Maaten and Hinton, 2008). Based on our preliminary analysis, we selected t-SNE due to its
superior separation of semantically distinct clusters. The final t-SNE parameters were set to
perplexity = 20, learning_rate = 100, and initialization = PCA, optimized for the structure of
our dataset.
For clustering, we experimented with various algorithms and determined that K-Means
clustering with a pre-specified number of 10 clusters yielded the most interpretable and nonoverlapping groupings of reasons. The choice of 10 clusters was based on visual inspection of
the t-SNE plots and preliminary runs that revealed this number captured the key thematic
patterns present in both human and AI-generated explanations. Each cluster was then
39

qualitatively examined and labelled based on the predominant reasoning patterns observed in the
grouped responses (Table S2). These categories encompass key themes such as natural features,
social infrastructure, aesthetics, and others. For clarity, abbreviated labels are used in the
visualizations. Some clusters combine multiple themes, reflecting the limitations of sentencelevel embeddings that capture word-level similarity but may overlook contextual nuance. For
example, the cluster “Sa, Ae” merges safety- and aesthetics-related explanations. Similarly,
broader categories like “Nature” include more specific subgroups, such as references to shade or
biodiversity.
