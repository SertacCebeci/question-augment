Abstract. Annotators routinely disagree on laughter boundaries and
subtle chuckles, yet temporal laughter localization typically evaluates
against a single reference annotation. We show that this disagreement is
structured rather than random noise. Re-annotating the SMILE-Temporal
benchmark (672 videos, 1,683 events) with 3-5 annotators per video (alpha = 0.757), we find systematic patterns: disagreement is 1.73x larger at
offsets than onsets, far more common for chuckles than full laughs (77%
vs. 20%), and predictable from event attributes (AUC 0.831). Evaluating against a single annotator breaks down under this structure: system
scores shift by 0.246 F1 depending on the chosen ground truth, correctly
ranking systems only 69.7% of the time (vs. 80% against all annotators).
We propose a disagreement-calibrated evaluation that scores predictions
against the full annotator distribution using conformally calibrated tolerance bands (wider at offsets, +/-0.727 s, than onsets, +/-0.5 s). The
per-annotator annotations and analysis code are available at https:
//github.com/WSCSports/MTLLFM-temporal-laughter-localization.
Keywords: Laughter localization · Annotator disagreement · Multiannotator datasets · Evaluation protocols · Affective behavior analysis

Introduction

When does a laugh begin - and, harder, when does it end? Ask several trained annotators to mark the same clip and they return different answers (Fig. 1): aligned
on the sharp attack of a clear laugh, scattered across its gradual decay, and split
on whether a faint chuckle is a laughter event at all. This is not an exotic corner
case. Laughter is among the most frequent and most studied affective signals in
real-world video, and localizing it in time, not merely detecting its presence, underpins applications from affective indexing to highlight generation. Yet laughter
localization benchmarks, like temporal affect benchmarks generally [11, 16, 31],
evaluate systems against a single reference annotation (henceforth single-GT ),
treating every difference between annotators as noise around one true boundary.

This paper argues, with data, that these differences are not noise. Our thesis: single-ground-truth evaluation misrepresents temporal laughter localization.
Laughter boundaries are not point events; annotators disagree about them in
structured, measurable ways; and that disagreement should be modeled rather
than averaged away. To test it, we re-annotate the SMILE-Temporal benchmark [9] with multiple independent annotators per video and release SMILEDisagreement, to our knowledge the first publicly available, per-event, multiannotator temporal-boundary corpus for laughter in affect video. We state this
claim narrowly and position it against LEAD [10], Vargas-Quiros [30], and
GEBD [26] in Sec. 2.
The disagreement we find is not random. Annotators agree well overall (Krippendorff’s α = 0.757 [13] at 100 ms, stable across bin sizes and annotator counts),
so the signal is trustworthy; but where they disagree is systematic. Two regularities stand out. First, offsets are 1.3–1.7× noisier than onsets: a laugh has a
sharp attack but a gradual decay, so its end is genuinely harder to pin down than
its start. Second, low-intensity “chuckles” are contested far more often than full
laughs (77% vs. 20% of events), an intensity effect that survives every control
we apply: modality, duration, annotator count, choice of event matcher, and the
(imperfect) reliability of the intensity label itself. This is not only about whether
a laugh is marked at all: even when all annotators agree a chuckle occurred, its
boundaries are about twice as spread out as a full laugh’s at the same duration.
Disagreement is, moreover, predictable from event attributes alone (Sec. 4.3).
In short, the ambiguity is a property of the events, not annotator caprice, and
averaging it away is not a harmless convenience but a modeling error.
The practical casualty is evaluation. Because annotators genuinely differ,
any single one is an arbitrary reference: the same system’s frame-F1 moves by
0.246 on average depending on which annotator is chosen as ground truth, and
- in controlled experiments with synthetic systems of known quality - single-GT

concentrate exactly where human disagreement does (Sec. 5).

Related Work

Human label variation and perspectivism. A decade of work has challenged
the assumption that annotation converges on one correct label: disagreement reflects genuine properties of the data rather than annotator error [2], and is reproducible and inherent rather than resolvable by more careful annotation [19]. The
resulting perspectivist, learning-with-disagreement program [23, 29] keeps the
variation and evaluates against it - via the soft label distributions of the LeWiDi
benchmark [15], label-distribution learning [6], and, in vision, full human label
distributions over CIFAR-10 [20]. All of this concerns categorical labels, where
disagreement is a rate over classes. We port the perspectivist question to temporal
boundaries in affect video, where a label is an interval and disagreement acquires
geometry: it can concentrate at onsets or offsets, scale with event intensity, and
widen or narrow per event.
Temporal boundary ambiguity in video. Vision has long had evidence
that temporal boundaries are soft: recognition accuracy depends on which temporal extent of an action one assumes [25], conscientious workers disagree substantially on temporal extents [27] and interaction bounds [17], and diagnostic
analyses identify imprecise boundary localization as a principal error source in
temporal action detectors [1]. Detection [26] responds by collecting five annotators per video and evaluating against each separately. These efforts, however,
treat the variation as noise to be managed - averaged away, regularized against,
or (in GEBD [26]) attached to instantaneous cut points. Affective events extend
over time, with an onset and an offset whose uncertainties differ - the systematic

Multi-annotator affect, laughter, and sound-event corpora. Closest
to our resource, the LEAD dataset [10] releases strong labels from 20 annotators for general sound events and analyzes how onset/offset labels vary, but
stops short of a disagreement-aware evaluation. For laughter specifically, VargasQuiros et al. [30] compare annotation modalities with two annotators per segment and aggregate their labels rather than releasing the per-event boundaries
of ≥3 distinct annotators. Affect corpora (RECOLA [24], SEWA [12]) ship multiple raters as dimensional traces rather than discrete event boundaries, and
the ABAW competition series [11] evaluates affect recognition against a single
reference annotation per video. The classic laughter resources (MAHNOB [21],
AMI [3]) and the detection and segmentation line built on them [4,7,18,22,28] all
treat a laugh’s extent as given - as do the SMILE benchmark [9] we re-annotate,
the multimodal localizer trained against it [8], and its successor SMILE-Next [14],
which scales laughter understanding to language models but neither localizes
boundaries in time nor models annotator disagreement. Our corpus occupies the
intersection these leave open (Table 2): per-event onset/offset distributions from
≥3 distinct annotators, on laughter/affect video, publicly released.

3

The SMILE-Disagreement Dataset

We re-annotate the videos of SMILE-Temporal [9], a temporal laughter-localization
benchmark, with multiple independent annotators, each marking laughter onset
and offset boundaries. To our knowledge it is the first publicly released, perevent, multi-annotator temporal-boundary corpus for laughter in affect video;
Table 2 positions it against the closest prior resources.
3.1

Annotation protocol

Six trained annotators labeled the videos independently, without seeing one another’s labels. For each perceived laughter event an annotator marked its onset
and offset and assigned three attributes: intensity (full laughter vs. chuckle);
source, the dominant cue the laughter was perceived through (acoustic, visual,
or both); and speaker (audience, 4,298 of the 4,554 benchmark marks, vs. individual speaker, 256). Source records the dominant channel, not an exclusive
one: a visual cue may still contribute where a laugh is labelled acoustic, possibly without the annotator consciously registering it, so the acoustic-dominant
split (Sec. 3.3) reflects the primary perceived channel, not the absence of visual
information.
The benchmark is every video with ≥3 distinct annotators (672 videos; pervideo counts in Table 1), the accepted minimum for reliability analysis; the count
is uneven, and Sec. 4 confirms every finding holds when videos are subsampled
to three. It carries 4,554 annotator marks (one region drawn by one annotator;
the full campaign collected 7,950, the rest repeats or below-threshold videos),
which cross-annotator matching merges into 1,683 distinct events.

3.2

Annotator consistency

A per-annotator profile (anonymized A1-A6, matching the released data) is given
in the supplementary. No annotator is an outlier: frame-level agreement with the
majority of the others spans 0.6-0.78 F1, productivity and existence rates are
comparable, and onset/offset style biases stay within a few tenths of a second.
The disagreement structure analyzed in Sec. 4 is therefore a property of the
videos themselves, not of one annotator’s labeling style.

3.3

Composition

Of the 672 benchmark videos, 578 contain at least one annotated laughter event;
these 578 total 4.49 hours of video (median 26.0 s, IQR 15.9–35.6 s), and after
matching holds 1,683 distinct events - 2.5 per video and 6.24 per annotated
minute - with laughter marked by at least one annotator covering 21.1% of
recorded time (14.7% by majority vote). Median event duration is 1.83 s for full
laughter vs. 1.16 s for chuckles, one reason low-intensity events are easier to miss
outright (Sec. 4.3). Across all 7,950 campaign marks, 75.4% are full laughter and
24.6% chuckles. By cue, annotators judged 94.3% acoustic-dominant, 4.8% audiovisual, and 0.9% visual-only; laughter’s most salient cue is usually its sound,
so an acoustic-leaning split is expected, and this is the dominant channel rather
than the only one, on what remains full audiovisual video (Sec. 3.1). The audiovisual minority is where the difficulty concentrates: visually-involved events are
disagreed about more than acoustic-only ones (69.0% vs. 37.0%; 95% Wilson CI
[62.0, 75.3], n=184 matched events), so the corpus still exercises the subtle, crossmodal cases an audiovisual system must resolve. Finally, 94.4% of marks are
audience laughter: the source videos are edited, broadcast-style content, where
laughter comes predominantly from an audience rather than a single on-screen
speaker. This is a domain property, not a confound behind our findings: the offset asymmetry replicates within both the audience and the individual-speaker
subsets (Sec. 4.2).
We publicly release SMILE-Disagreement - the per-annotator onset/offset
annotations and metadata as a JSON file, for non-commercial research purposes, together with the full analysis code, at https://github.com/WSCSports/
MTLLFM-temporal-laughter-localization. The underlying video content is
part of the original SMILE dataset [9] and is not redistributed; our release adds
only the multi-annotator annotation layer.

3.4

Relation to prior multi-annotator resources

Multi-annotator boundary annotation exists in adjacent settings, and we claim
no priority over it; Table 2 positions our corpus against the closest work. Each
prior resource lacks one of our four defining properties: LEAD [10] annotates
general sound events, not affect video; Vargas-Quiros et al. [30] use two annotators per segment (48 total), aggregated and unreleased; GEBD [26] marks
instantaneous cut points rather than onset/offset intervals; and RECOLA [24]
/ SEWA [12] provide continuous dimensional traces rather than discrete perevent boundaries. Our contribution is the intersection they leave open: publicly released, per-event onset/offset distributions from ≥3 distinct annotators
on laughter/affect video.

4

Analysis and Findings

We treat the multi-annotator labels not as noise to be averaged into a single
ground truth but as a distribution to be characterized; all statistics are computed on the 672-video benchmark of Sec. 3 (a summary table is in the supplementary). We first establish that the agreement signal is stable - the claim that
disagreement is structured is only meaningful if it is - and then show that where
annotators disagree is governed by two factors: where a boundary lies (onset vs.
offset) and how intense the laughter is.

4.2

Offsets are fuzzier than onsets

For events that multiple annotators localized in common, we measure the acrossannotator standard deviation of each boundary. Offsets are markedly noisier
(Fig. 2a): the mean offset spread (0.253 s) is about 1.73× the onset spread
(0.146 s; one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank p = 4.3 × 10−19 , video-level bootstrap 95% CI on the greedy-matcher ratio [1.54, 1.97]). It is not an artifact of
the corpus’s dominant communal audience laughter, whose decay many people
could stop marking at different times: it holds within audience-labeled events
(1.75×, bootstrap 95% CI [1.56, 1.97], n=1, 328) and, indicatively, within the
small individual-speaker group (1.54×, CI [1.06, 2.27], n=60) - single-person
laughs show the same offset fuzziness. The cause is acoustic. Measuring the
RMS energy envelope around each co-marked event (half-max crossing times in
a padded window, so they do not depend on the human boundaries), the central
peak rises faster than it falls: the decay is 1.48× the attack (median; video-level
bootstrap 95% CI [1.4, 1.5]; decay exceeds attack in 68% of 1, 278 events). A
sharp attack but gradual decay makes the endpoint acoustically the less determined boundary - the direction of the annotation asymmetry. The effect is
aggregate, not an event-wise dose–response (within events, decay length does
not predict offset spread, Spearman ρ= − 0.07), so we read the offset fuzziness
as a general perceptual consequence of the gradual decay. Any evaluation should
therefore grant more tolerance at offsets than at onsets, a prescription we make
precise in Sec. 5.
4.3

Intensity drives disagreement

The second and larger factor is intensity, and it acts on both whether an event
is marked and where its boundaries fall.
Existence. Calling an event disagreed when not all of a video’s annotators
marked it (greedy-overlap matcher; an event’s intensity is the majority vote of
its co-markers’ labels), a chuckle is disagreed about 77% of the time versus only
20% for full laughter (Fig. 2b; video-level clustered bootstrap 95% CI on the gap
[52, 61] points) - equivalently, 68% of all disagreed events are chuckles, against
just 14% of unanimous ones. Low-intensity laughter is where humans disagree;
unambiguous full laughter is where they agree.

5

Disagreement-Calibrated Evaluation

The findings of Sec. 4 imply that a single annotator is a poor stand-in for “ground
truth.” This section makes the damage concrete, then proposes and validates an
evaluation that scores a system against the distribution of human labels. The
protocol is built from the human annotations alone - no trained model is required
to construct or validate it (Sec. 5.4 then applies it to one).
5.1

A single ground truth is arbitrary

We first quantify the arbitrariness. Treat each annotator as a “system” and score
it, at the frame level (100 ms), against each of the other annotators, one at a
time. Two facts emerge. First, human–human agreement averages a frame-F1 of
about 0.681: this, not 1.0, is the realistic ceiling for any system, yet a singleground-truth (single-GT) benchmark implicitly treats perfection as attainable.
Second, and more damaging, a system’s measured score depends heavily on which
annotator is chosen as reference: for each (video, system) pair we take the max–
min range of its frame-F1 across the available reference choices; the mean range
is 0.246 F1 (Fig. 3a). Under single-GT, the reported number is partly an artifact
of an arbitrary choice.

(ii) A conformally calibrated tolerance band. For each boundary type b ∈

lenient where humans themselves are least certain, inheriting by construction
the boundary asymmetry of Sec. 4.2 rather than ignoring it.
These global widths are driven by a heavy tail of ambiguous events; an adaptive variant, which rescales each r by the local spread of the co-markers’ boundaries, attains the same coverage with narrower per-event widths, widening only
on the events where the annotators themselves disagree. Its specification, and
two further calibration details, are in the supplementary.
A prediction’s band score is the fraction of its matched boundaries falling
within ±qb of the corresponding human median. Predicted events that overlap
no human event contribute no boundaries to it: hallucinations and misses are
punished by the soft-IoU term instead - a hallucinated event’s frames enter the
denominator of Eq. (1) at full weight, a missed event’s frames enter it at their
human weight si , and neither adds to the numerator. Sec. 5.3 probes this division
of labor for exploitability.
Two caveats delimit what the calibration promises. First, coverage is marginal
(it holds on average over the pooled residuals, not per video or annotator), and
pooling treats residuals as exchangeable although they cluster within videos,
within annotators, and within events (each event yields several leave-one-out
residuals). The annotator axis we verify empirically: calibrating on five annotators and testing on the sixth yields 0.86–0.92 (mean 0.895, target 0.90) across all
six annotators and both boundary types - approximately calibrated, with mild
under-coverage at the extremes. That check pools events, so the other clustering
axes remain assumptions, not verified properties. For a system’s predictions the
band is a human-calibrated tolerance, not a coverage guarantee: it asks whether
a prediction deviates from the consensus by more than one human deviates from
the rest. A model’s residuals need not be exchangeable with human ones, and
Sec. 5.4 shows they are not.

5.3

Validation: does it rank systems better?

A good evaluation should rank a better system above a worse one, so we test
exactly that. We inject synthetic “systems” of known quality, the consensus localization perturbed with increasing boundary jitter (four tiers, so tier order is
ground-truth quality order), and ask, for each adjacent pair and over five random seeds, whether a scoring rule ranks the better system higher (Fig. 3). Three
rules are compared, differing in how they use the annotators: frame-F1 against a
single arbitrary annotator; frame-F1 averaged over all annotators; and soft-IoU
against the annotator distribution.
Single-GT gets the ranking right only 69.7% of the time; both distributionaware rules reach ∼80% (multi-annotator average 80.4%, soft-IoU 80.0%). The
soft-IoU–vs–single-GT gap carries a paired-bootstrap 95% CI of 9–11 points;
that bootstrap resamples pooled ranking trials independently, ignoring withinvideo correlation, so the true interval can only be wider. The gain decomposes
cleanly: essentially all of it comes from evaluating against all annotators rather
than an arbitrary one (the choice single-GT benchmarks silently make), while
the soft-IoU rule matches, but does not exceed, the averaged rule’s reliability
on these synthetic systems. What the distributional form adds is therefore not
ranking power: the reference-annotator degree of freedom is already removed
by any rule that uses all annotators, so the form’s own contribution is graded
credit for partially-agreed events and the calibrated tolerance band of Sec. 5.2,
whose role the next paragraph tests. The decomposition is also robust to how the
synthetic systems are generated: perturbing a random annotator’s labels instead
of the consensus gives 67.9% / 77.6% / 77.4%.
The tolerance band plays a deliberately different role. Scored alone it declares
67% of adjacent-tier pairs tied : both systems sit within the human-calibrated tolerance, and an evaluation aligned with human ambiguity should indeed refuse
to rank differences smaller than human disagreement. When it does decide, it
decides correctly 85% of the time; and the full protocol (soft-IoU + band) preserves ranking reliability (79.7%; flat at 79.5–80.0% across mixing weights of
0.25–0.75, so the unweighted mean is a convention, not a tuned parameter). Nor
is the composition gamed by the obvious boundary-only play: a degenerate “system” predicting one well-placed 0.2 s sliver per video scores 0.159 under the full
protocol versus 0.657 for even the worst jittered tier - its soft-IoU collapses, and
even its band score is only 0.248.

5.4

On a real localizer

Synthetic tiers validate a metric; a real system is what the protocol is for.
No laughter-specific audiovisual localizer is publicly released: UnAV-100 localizers [5] carry a laughter class among their hundred categories but ship no
laughter-specialized checkpoint, and this corpus is precisely the resource such
a model would need. We therefore run the strongest public trained laughter localizer available, the audio-only segmentation model of Omine et al. [18], with
its released checkpoint and inference settings, over the 578 benchmark videos
with at least one annotated laugh, yielding 1,075 predicted events. (On the 94
additional videos where no annotator marked laughter it predicts only 5 events,
so it almost never hallucinates.) An audio-only system is a reasonable first entry
for this corpus, whose reported cue is 94.3% acoustic (Sec. 3); its errors can be
read against the annotators’ own cue labels.
The effects the protocol was built for all appear on the real system (Table 3).
The swing is real: the reference-annotator choice moves the model’s per-video
frame-F1 by even more than the swing measured with annotators-as-systems,
though a system operating farther below the ceiling has more room to swing,
so we read the direction, not the magnitude. The band does metric work,
most clearly at offsets. Under the global band the model falls short of the
0.90 human construction target at both boundary types; under the adaptive band
its onset coverage sits within the human annotator-out range (itself measured
under the global band, so we read the adaptive comparison as indicative only),

Scope. The tier systems of Sec. 5.3 are synthetic (the standard device for validating a metric under a controlled quality ordering), and Sec. 5.4 confirms the
headline effects on a trained system, including the prediction that real localizers
fail on the events where human disagreement concentrates. The recommendation
stands regardless: report temporal-localization performance against all annotators, ideally as a distribution with conformally calibrated tolerance, and never
against a single one. Although we instantiate it on laughter, the protocol acts
only on temporal boundaries and their inter-annotator spread, so it transfers
to any affective behavior annotated as multi-rater event segments. The nearest fit is temporal-phase segmentation of facial expressions and action units:
onset–apex–offset annotation is event-bounded in the same shape this corpus
releases, and porting needs only multi-rater phase boundaries, with the tolerances re-calibrated per task (the offset asymmetry we measure is a property of
laughter, not of the method). Continuous dimensional traces (valence–arousal in
RECOLA [24], SEWA [12], and the ABAW series [11]) have no event bound-

Limitations

Our analysis is based on SMILE-Temporal, whose laughter is predominantly
acoustically cued, preventing a meaningful study of audio–visual disagreement.
Furthermore, the conformal tolerance bands are calibrated to a single annotation
pool and protocol, so their widths should not be interpreted as universal. Finally,
evaluation on real systems is limited to one public audio-only laughter localizer.
Extending the analysis to additional datasets, annotation pools, and multimodal
localizers remains future work.

7

Conclusion

Single-ground-truth evaluation misrepresents temporal laughter localization. Laughter boundaries are not point events; annotators disagree about them in structured, measurable ways; and that disagreement should be modeled rather than
averaged away. The dataset is the enabler; the findings and the evaluation protocol are the contribution. For practitioners the prescription fits in one sentence:
evaluate temporal affect against all annotators - ideally the distribution, with tolerance conformally calibrated to human spread - never against a single reference;
expect about 1.45× the tolerance at offsets as at onsets; and treat low-intensity
events as intrinsically ambiguous rather than mislabeled. We release SMILEDisagreement as per-annotator distributions with all analysis code - including
the evaluation harness a public laughter localizer already runs under (Sec. 5.4),
where its misses concentrate on the events humans contest - and see two natural
next steps: broadening the real-system panel across modalities, and collecting
visually-driven laughter to extend the analysis beyond the acoustic channel.

Conformal band: specification details
Three details supplement the band’s definition in Sec. 5.2 of the main paper.
Calibration/test k-mismatch. Calibration holds out one annotator (median
over k−1) while a prediction is tested against the median of all k - the more
stable of the two, so the applied band errs conservative.
Per-co-marker-count strata. The pooled quantile is not an artifact of the
thinnest events. Splitting the leave-one-annotator-out calibration pool by comarker count: with k=2 co-markers (n=2, 162 residuals per boundary type) the
90%-coverage half-widths are ±0.535 s (onset) and ±0.712 s (offset); with k≥3
(n=1, 653) they are ±0.467 s and ±0.734 s - close to the pooled ±0.5/±0.727 s,
so the pooled quantile is not driven by the thin-calibration events.
