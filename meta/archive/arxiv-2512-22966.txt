Abstract
In this study, we evaluated the eJects of prompt engineering on the clinical reasoning
performance of ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and LIama 3.3 70B across ﬁve core clinical
decision-making tasks using 36 clinical cases. Prompt engineering using two variants of
MedPrompt improved performance on relevant diagnostic testing, the most challenging
task at baseline, but produced mixed and sometimes adverse eJects elsewhere, indicating
that prompt engineering is not a one-size-ﬁts-all solution.
Keywords: Clinical decision-making; Large Language Models; Prompt engineering; AI in
healthcare

Introduction
Clinical decision-making is recognized as a cognitively demanding process in safe and
eJective patient care. Yet diagnostic and therapeutic errors remain alarmingly prevalent 1.
Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as a potentially transformative paradigm for
clinical decision support. Recent studies have highlighted this potential by documenting
their strong performance on standardized medical knowledge benchmarks such as USMLE
and MedQA, achieving expert-level accuracy or even surpassing the performance of
trained physicians 2,3. Prompt engineering strategies—such as chain-of-thought (CoT)
reasoning and few-shot prompting—have been shown to enhance LLM reasoning in both
medical and non-medical tasks 4,5, with the MedPrompt framework as a prominent
example 6.
However, success on knowledge-based examinations does not translate to eJective
performance in real-world clinical reasoning, which requires the integration of patientspeciﬁc data, prioritization under uncertainty, and the timing and arrangement of
diagnostic test and therapeutic interventions 7-10. Moreover, approaches such as
MedPrompt have been tested almost exclusively on OpenAI models like ChatGPT 6, and
while these methods are model agnostic, their generalizability to other LLMs has not been
explored. This underscores the pressing need for systematic evaluations that assess how
diJerent prompting strategies inﬂuence LLM performance across the full spectrum of
clinical workﬂow. In this study, we aimed to address this gap by systematically evaluating
knowledge recall and the clinical reasoning performance of three state-of-the-art LLMs—
ChatGPT-4o (OpenAI), and Gemini 2.5 Flash (Google), and LIama 3.3 70B (Meta)—across
ﬁve key stages of sequential clinical decision-making using both baseline prompting and
prompt engineering approaches.

Results
A total of 36 publicly available clinical cases were extracted from the Merck Sharpe &
Dohme (MSD) Clinical Manual 11. These clinical vignettes are designed to reﬂect real-world
clinical scenarios that healthcare professionals commonly encounter and involve a
structured clinical reasoning workﬂow that begins with patient’s history of present and past
illness (HPI), review of systems (ROS), and physical examination (PE); it then proceeds
through ﬁve sequential decision-making tasks: diJerential diagnosis, essential immediate
steps, relevant diagnostic testing, ﬁnal diagnosis, and treatment recommendations (Figure
1). For each decision-making task, LLMs were provided with progressively richer clinical
input: initially, HPI, ROS, and PE for diJerential diagnosis; and for the remaining tasks, HPI,
ROS, and PE along with the ground truth for all prior tasks in the sequence. This ensured

the model had access to all pertinent context as it moved through the clinical reasoning
sequence.

We ﬁrst comprehensively evaluated each LLM’s out-of-the-box performance proﬁle across
the ﬁve clinical reasoning tasks using basic prompting in order to better understand their
inherent strengths and weaknesses in a more realistic clinical context. To this end,
relevant patient information for each reasoning task was presented to the models without
additional prompt engineering strategies. We further investigated whether the performance
may diJer between the default temperature for each model and zero temperature settings.
The ﬁndings of this analysis are summarized in Figure 2. LLMs showed marked task
dependence across the clinical reasoning workﬂow. All three models achieved nearperfect performance for ﬁnal diagnosis, the lowest performance for relevant diagnostic
testing, and moderate to moderately high accuracy for diJerential diagnosis, essential
immediate steps, and treatment recommendation. Across models, Gemini 2.5 Flash
consistently matched or outperformed its peers in all tasks. Although modest modelspeciﬁc diJerences were observed between default and zero temperature settings, none
were statistically signiﬁcant (Wilcoxon Signed Rank test, p < 0.05); directionally, Gemini 2.5
Flash tended to perform slightly better at zero temperature, Llama 3.3 70B was largely
insensitive to temperature setting, and ChatGPT-4o showed mixed temperature-dependent
patterns across tasks.

Following our baseline evaluation, we sought to determine whether prompt engineering
could further enhance the clinical reasoning capabilities of the LLMs. To this end, we
leveraged prompt engineering strategies from MedPrompt 6, a structured prompt
engineering framework that integrates self-generated CoT reasoning with dynamic few-shot
learning and answer choice shuJling ensemble. In this study, we used the same selfgenerated CoT and answer choice shuJling approaches as in MedPrompt 6. For dynamic
few-shot learning, however, we explored two diJerent approaches. The ﬁrst utilized a
targeted dynamic few-shot selection scheme based on the K nearest neighbors (KNN),
where K examples are chosen based on their semantic similarity to the test question, as
originally proposed in MedPrompt 6. The second approach substituted this with random
dynamic few-shot selection, where K exemplars were chosen at random from the training
set (Figure 3). We excluded ﬁnal diagnosis from our analysis as all three LLMs showed nearcomplete accuracy for this task using basic prompting (Figure 2), which left little room for
further improvement. The results of this analysis is shown in Figure 4. Neither MedPrompt
variant produced uniform performance gains across models or tasks. Prompt engineering
substantially improved performance for relevant diagnostic testing, the most challenging
task in out-of-the-box evaluation across all three models, except for Gemini 2.5 Flash with
random few-shot prompting. For example, the accuracy of ChatGPT 4o for relevant
diagnostic testing surged from 30.8% to 50.7% with MedPrompt using KNN few-shots (a
19.9% improvement), whereas Llama 3.3 70B and Gemini 2.5 Flash showed 17.9% and
13.5% improvement, respectively.

However, prompt engineering using MedPrompt had mixed or even counterproductive
eJects elsewhere, including consistent performance declines in diJerential diagnosis and
treatment recommendation for all or most models. For instance, MedPrompt with KNN
dynamic few-shot prompting led to a sharp decline in ChatGPT 4o’s diJerential diagnosis
performance from 71.1% to 56.0%. Furthermore, KNN dynamic few-shot prompting did not
consistently outperform random few-shot prompting, further indicating that the eJect of
prompt engineering was strongly task- and model-dependent.

Discussion
Our ﬁndings indicate that LLM performance is shaped by the clinical task, the model, and
the prompting strategy applied. The highly inconsistent and sometimes counterproductive
eJects of prompt engineering revealed that added reasoning structure by MedPrompt is
beneﬁcial only in selected settings. This motivates closer consideration of how task
complexity, model behavior, prompting strategy, and deployment context shape LLM
clinical reasoning performance.
Task-dependent out-of-the-box LLM performance suggests diAering reasoning
demands across tasks: Our analysis of out-of-the-box LLM performance revealed
signiﬁcant variability across clinical tasks (Figure 2). This performance spectrum could
reﬂect the inherent cognitive demand and complexity of each task. The near-perfect
accuracy in ﬁnal diagnosis, for instance, can be likely attributed to the availability of a
complete set of information to the model, including all prior ground truth and deﬁnitive
diagnostic test results, which signiﬁcantly constrains the diagnosis space and minimizes
uncertainty. Conversely, relevant diagnostic testing likely proved the most diJicult because
it requires reasoning under uncertainty, weigh the potential diagnostic yield of multiple test
options, and strategize the most eJicient path to a diagnosis. This suggests that diJerent
stages of the clinical workﬂow place distinct reasoning demands on LLMs.
Model accuracy was robust to temperature changes, though the choice remains
critical for clinical applications: Our initial rationale for including the zero temperature
setting in our analysis was that reduced variability in model outputs might help ensure
more consistent and reproducible clinical reasoning, which is particularly valuable in highstakes healthcare environments. On the other hand, zero temperature may also
compromise the performance as it could potentially favor more conservative or
prototypical responses, impacting the breadth or creativity of the model's clinical
reasoning. Examining both settings allowed us to explore whether certain clinical reasoning
tasks beneﬁt from more predictable, consistent responses (high determinism) versus
allowing for a more ﬂexible and broader range of potential solutions (greater breadth). Our
analysis identiﬁed subtle model-speciﬁc trends between temperature settings, although

none of the observed diJerences were statistically signiﬁcant. This suggests that model
accuracy is mostly robust to this hyperparameter within the scope of the MSD dataset.
However, the choice of temperature setting remains clinically signiﬁcant. While the
observed diJerences in accuracy were marginal in this benchmark, the degree of output
determinism vs. variability has implications beyond performance alone. In particular, more
stable responses can facilitate the auditability of model outputs across identical inputs,
medico-legal accountability, and integration into clinical workﬂows. The decision to
prioritize deterministic, reproducible outputs over more variable responses depends on the
speciﬁc clinical task and intended use case and is a critical consideration for the safe and
reliable deployment of these models in clinical workﬂows. This warrants further exploration
across larger and more diverse datasets.
Structured prompting can degrade performance by over-constraining a model’s
reasoning process: A key ﬁnding of our study is that while MedPrompt’s structured prompt
engineering aims to guide models toward more systematic reasoning, it does not
universally improve model performance, and in several cases, it can paradoxically be
counterproductive, particularly when the prompts impose elaborate reasoning scaJolds or
demand tight alignment between example semantics. Performance drops when subjected
to prompt engineering were most evident for ChatGPT-4o; however, this eJect is not unique
to one model or one task; Llama 3.3 70B and Gemini 2.5 Flash also experienced marked
performance degradation in treatment recommendation and diJerential diagnosis,
respectively, with both prompting strategies (Figure 4). This indicates that even models that
beneﬁt from advanced prompting in some scenarios, can be impaired by it in others.
These observations suggest that, in some settings, imposing strict step-by-step reasoning
and multi-example few-shot prompting may be counterproductive. Although the
mechanism underlying this performance degradation under structured prompting cannot
be resolved directly from our benchmark, a plausible explanation is that prompt length and
context-window competition may lead to distraction from core task-relevant signals.
Speciﬁcally, longer and more complex prompts—with added instructions, multiple
examples, and mandated reasoning structure—consume limited context and can shift
model attention toward generic or example-consistent patterns, thereby reducing its ability
to focus on the clinically relevant details of the current vignette when selecting an answer.
Emerging ﬁndings in the broader LLM similarly caution that forcing models into rigid,
structured reasoning paths can undermine their natural reasoning process 12-14. For
example, both Kim et. al 12 and Shao et al 14 argue that CoT functions as a structural
constraint that drives models to mimic reasoning patterns rather than engage in genuine,
ﬂexible, and novel inference pathways.

Prompt engineering was most eAective for tasks with greatest baseline diAiculty:
Prompt engineering provided substantial beneﬁts for the single most challenging task in
out-of-the-box emulation: relevant diagnostic testing (Figure 4). Across all three models,
both or at least one MedPrompt variant appeared to provide an eJective structure aid for
guiding the models’ reasoning in a setting where their baseline capabilities were weakest.
This suggests that when a model lacks a robust internal strategy for a high-uncertainty task,
the explicit structure of a prompt could be highly beneﬁcial.
Semantic similarity alone may not optimize few-shot prompting: Another notable
observation was that MedPrompt with the more targeted 𝐾NN dynamic few-shots did not
always outperform that with random dynamic few-shots (Figure 4). This ﬁnding indicates
that relying solely on semantic similarity to select few-shot examples may not always yield
the best results for model performance. The reason for this unexpected observation could
be that the presumed advantage of closely matched examples can be oJset in some cases
by the loss of broader contextual diversity or alternative reasoning strategies. These mixed
outcomes are in contrast to the consistent improvements reported in the original
MedPrompt paper for medical knowledge exams 6 and highlight the intricate interplay
between model architecture, the speciﬁc clinical reasoning task, and the chosen
prompting strategy in real-world clinical scenarios.
The study's ﬁndings should be interpreted in the context of several key limitations: It is
important to consider several limitations when interpreting our ﬁndings. First, this work is
an exploratory benchmark conducted on a modest dataset of 36 clinical vignettes with
uneven task-speciﬁc sample sizes, including a small sample size for essential immediate
steps (n=10). The modest and uneven sample sizes across tasks reduce statistical power
for task-speciﬁc comparisons and limit our ability to distinguish stable performance
diJerences from sampling variability. These considerations reinforce the need for largerscale evaluations. Second, the clinical cases were sourced from the MSD Manual, an
evidence-based educational resource with curated, well-structured narratives and answer
sets. Although this ensures high-quality data for our analysis, it does not fully capture the
diversity and complexity of real-world clinical practice, which often involves fragmented,
incomplete, or ambiguous information from electronic health records. Consequently, the
observed performance—particularly on tasks that beneﬁt from well-structured context—
may reﬂect “textbook-style” reasoning and be inﬂated relative to real-world clinical
environments. Third, model performance was primarily assessed using a metric deﬁned as
the degree of overlap between predicted multiple-choice answers and ground truth, which
eJectively corresponds to recall. This quantitative approach, while objective and allowing
partial credit, does not capture several clinically important dimensions, including the
relative severity of omissions (e.g., missing a critical diagnostic test vs. omitting a lower-

yield option), the safety implications of incorrect or redundant selections, or the clinical
appropriateness of the model’s underlying reasoning. To better characterize omissiondriven errors vs. over-selection of incorrect options, we additionally reported precision and
F1 score in Supplementary File 1; however, more clinically grounded evaluations will
require clinician-weighted or safety-oriented metrics that distinguish critical omissions
from lower-impact errors. Fourth, because our evaluation is conducted in a closed-form
multiple-choice setting in which models select from predeﬁned options, it does not assess
hallucinations or other failure modes that arise in open-ended content generation. Finally,
our prompt engineering framework relied on static LLM-generated CoT examples and did
not include real-time clinician feedback or reinforcement mechanisms, which may limit
safety and adaptability in dynamic clinical environments.
Implications for safe clinical integration of LLMs: Together, this study demonstrates that
strong LLM performance on medical knowledge exams does not necessarily translate to
eJective performance in sequential clinical decision-making tasks, as assessed within this
set of structured clinical vignettes. This underscores a crucial gap between the theoretical
knowledge of LLMs and their performance in more realistic clinical workﬂows. Our results
further indicate that there is no “one-size-ﬁts-all” solution for optimizing LLM performance
in clinical decision-making; the eJectiveness of prompting strategies to guide the language
models is highly dependent on the speciﬁc clinical task at hand. This implies that the path
toward safe and eJective deployment of LLMs in healthcare involves the development of
tailored approaches that pair the right model with a relevant prompting strategy to the right
task, rather than a search for a single, universally optimal model or prompting technique.
Accordingly, the safe clinical integration of LLMs will likely require hybrid,
clinician-in-the-loop workﬂows with uncertainty-aware outputs, rather than fully
autonomous use. In this setting, LLM recommendations merely serve as decision support
and remain subject to human review and conﬁrmation, particularly in ambiguous or
high-risk cases. Future research can expand on this work by incorporating larger-scale and
more diverse datasets that better reﬂect real-world clinical complexity, and evaluate
whether newer reasoning models show similar task- and prompt-dependent behavior
across sequential clinical decision-making workﬂows.

Methods
Data sources: The dataset used in this study was sourced from the Merck Sharpe &
Dohme (MSD) Clinical Manual Professional Version 11. The MSD Manual is a widely
recognized, evidence-based medical resource authored by subject matter experts and
peer-reviewed prior to publication. It provides comprehensive clinical information across a
wide range of specialties and is commonly used by healthcare professionals for education

and decision support. A total of 36 clinical cases were available in this database at the time
the study was conducted, each representing an individual patient case. The cases were
extracted using web-scrapping in Python. These cases are structured to reﬂect realistic
clinical scenarios and include key components of a standard patient encounter, such as
HPI, ROS, PE results, and, where applicable, laboratory data.
LLMs examined: We evaluated the performance of three leading LLMs: two commercial
models—ChatGPT-4o (OpenAI) and Gemini 2.5 Flash (Google)—and one open-source
model—LIama 3.3 70B (Meta). Each model was accessed through its respective API. For
ChatGPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Flash, we used oJicial API endpoints provided by OpenAI and
Google, respectively. Requests were made using Python scripts through the openai and
google.generativeai libraries. For Gemini 2.5 Flash, we disabled thinking by setting the
thinking budget to zero (thinkingBudget = 0) to maintain comparability with the other
evaluated models. For the open-source LIama 3.3 70B model, we employed the
together.ai Python SDK, accessing the model via the Together AI platform. API requests
were structured as JSON objects specifying the model’s name, prompt messages, and
function-calling parameters.
Prompting LLMs: Each clinical vignette was input into the model as a single continuous
session to preserve contextual continuity. A new session was initialized for each case to
avoid cross-contamination of responses between vignettes. Questions involving visual
data, such as medical images, were excluded from analysis. Clinical decision-making
tasks were ordered as follows: diJerential diagnosis, essential immediate steps, relevant
diagnostic testing, ﬁnal diagnosis, and treatment recommendation. These tasks were
presented to the models as a series of prompts, where the input to the model for each
clinical decision-making task included HPI, ROS, PE results, along with the ground truth for
all preceding tasks. For example, model inputs for diJerential diagnosis were HPI, ROS,
and PE results. The inputs to the next clinical task, essential immediate steps, included
HPI, ROS, and PE results as well as the ground truth for diJerential diagnosis. Each
question was tested three times (each in a new session) to account for potential variability
in responses. The model’s output for each trial was compared against a predeﬁned set of
correct answers extracted from the MSD database. Notably, not all case studies included
all ﬁve question types; in such instances, the model was directed to proceed to the next
available question type. The number of clinical vignettes for each category of questions is:
36 in essential diJerential diagnosis, 10 in essential immediate steps, 21 in relevant
diagnostic testing, 33 in ﬁnal diagnosis, and 31 in treatment ordering.
Prompt engineering: Prior to applying prompt engineering, we partitioned the cases for
each task into training and test sets; approximately 30% of cases were allocated to the

training set for the generation of few-shot examples with self-generated CoT reasoning,
while the remaining cases formed the test set for model evaluation. We constructed the
training dataset by identifying question instances for which the model’s self-generated CoT
reasoning produced an answer that matched the ground truth answer. Speciﬁcally, we
prompted each LLM with the instruction “Let’s think step by step.” to elicit CoT reasoning.
From the resulting output, we extracted the model’s ﬁnal answer and compared it to the
correct answer for each question type. If the model’s answer matched the ground truth, the
case was included in the training set. Otherwise, it was assigned to the test set. In
situations where the self-generated CoT did not yield a suJicient number of high-quality
training examples, we supplied the correct answer to the model and prompted it to
generate a corresponding CoT explanation. These synthetically augmented CoT examples
were included in the training set until the desired sample size was achieved. Once the
training set was ﬁnalized, we applied the structured MedPrompt prompting techniques to
evaluate performance on the test set.
For each task, 𝐾 = 3 cases from the training set were selected as examples using 𝐾NN or
random dynamic few-shot prompting. For 𝐾NN dynamic few-shot learning introduced and
utilized in MedPrompt 6, we ﬁrst computed vector representations of both training and test
set questions using the OpenAI’s client.embeddings.create() function. Three few-shot
examples were then selected for each test question based on cosine similarity, prioritizing
examples that were semantically similar to the target test question in terms of clinical
context. Answer to these questions already involved CoT explanation generated by the
respective LLM under evaluation.
In addition to the original MedPrompt framework, which involved 𝐾NN dynamic few-short
prompting 6, we also examined a variant of MedPrompt, where we replaced the 𝐾NN
dynamic few-shot prompting with random dynamic few-shot prompting, where the
examples from the training dataset for each test question were selected randomly for fewshot learning. For answer choice shuJling ensemble, another key component of
MedPrompt, answer choices were shuJled to minimize answer-order bias. Three prompt
variants with shuJled answer choices were generated and aggregated in an ensemble
framework for robust evaluation.
Performance evaluation: Model performance for questions with multiple answer choices
(diJerential diagnosis, essential immediate steps, relevant diagnostic testing, treatment
recommendation) was calculated based on the percent overlap between the modelgenerated responses and the ground truth answers in the MSD database. This allowed us to
award partial credit to LLMs when the model’s answer intersected with the correct set but
was not fully correct. For example, in diJerential diagnosis questions, if the ground truth

listed ﬁve possible conditions (e.g., pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, asthma, heart
failure, and bronchitis) and the model correctly identiﬁed three of them (e.g., pneumonia,
asthma, and heart failure), then the performance score for that instance would be
calculated as the proportion of correct predictions: 3 out of 5, or 60%. Each clinical
question was evaluated over three independent runs to account for potential variability in
model responses. In addition to the overlap-based accuracy score, which eJectively
corresponds to recall), we computed precision (proportion of selected options that were
correct) and their harmonic mean (F1 score) for tasks with multiple correct answers. These
metrics are deﬁned as follows: Recall =

For each case and run, accuracy (recall), precision, and F1-score were ﬁrst calculated and
the three run-level values were then averaged to obtain one case-level value for each
metric. Overall performance for each question type was calculated as the mean of these
case-level values across all relevant questions within that category.
For ﬁnal diagnosis, where there was a single correct answer choice per case, the model’s
answer for each clinical case was directly compared with MSD correct answers, labeling 0
(incorrect) or 1 (correct). The average score was calculated for the three runs within each
case, and overall accuracy was calculated across cases; for example, if the model
answered correctly in two of three runs, the case-level accuracy was 2/3. Overall accuracy
was then calculated as the mean of these case-level accuracies across all cases. Precision
and F1 score were not reported because, in this single-answer setting, both are identical to
accuracy at the case level and therefore also yield the same overall mean across cases.
For all tasks we have additionally reported 95% conﬁdence intervals estimated by
nonparametric bootstrapping over clinical vignettes (resampling vignettes with
replacement and recomputing the mean performance). All baseline prompting, prompt
engineering, and performance evaluation implementations were conducted within Google
Colaboratory and can be accessed in Supplementary File 2.
Statistical methods: To evaluate the statistical signiﬁcance of performance diJerences
between LLMs under diJerent prompting strategies or temperature settings, we conducted
statistical hypothesis testing using the paired Wilcoxon Signed Rank test using case-level
performance values. All tests were two-sided, and statistical signiﬁcance was based on a

p-value of < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using the tidyverse, ggsignif
packages in R.
