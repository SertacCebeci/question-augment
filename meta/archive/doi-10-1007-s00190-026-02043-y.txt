A sequential post-selection testing procedure for univariate
congruence models with nested and non-nested hypotheses

Abstract
Least-squares-based testing procedures for unstable-point identification in geodetic monitoring networks are vulnerable to
the smear effect, whereby the influence of a true displacement spreads over several coordinate differences. This leads to a
displaced point classified as stable (masking) and a stable point classified as unstable (swamping), a problem that becomes
more severe when several points move simultaneously. Recent sequential and combinatorial procedures reduce these effects,
but they often lack explicit control of the stepwise false-alarm rate and do not treat post-selection in a formal way. This paper
presents SEQCUP, a sequential combinatorial post-selection testing procedure for univariate congruence models when the
number and location of displaced points are unknown. The method uses two-epoch observation differences, remains invariant
with respect to datum definition, and retains a strictly linear congruence model. At each stage, SEQCUP compares the current
null model with higher-dimensional alternatives by means of a quadratic-form statistic built from the difference between their
orthogonal projection matrices. The critical value is calibrated with Monte Carlo simulations under the parameterized null
displacement model, conditional on the data-driven model selected at the previous stage, so that the resulting test remains
valid for both nested and non-nested hypotheses within a unified framework. A stopping rule also limits the maximum
number of points inspected in the sequential procedure. It relies on the network topology, excludes models that share the
same projector, and uses a normalized distance between projectors to avoid stages with potentially weak separability and
pronounced smear effects. Numerical experiments with trilateration, GNSS baseline, and levelling networks, together with
literature-based scenarios, show that SEQCUP controls false alarms effectively and attains high mean success rates for model
identification over a wide range of signal-to-noise ratios. The method performs at least as well as classical procedures and
remains comparable to contemporary combinatorial and information-criterion-based methods, with clear advantages in several
scenarios involving multiple displaced points and low-to-moderate signal-to-noise ratios.

1 Introduction
One of the main challenges in geodetic data analysis is
detecting geometric changes in objects or regions subject to
displacements or deformations, such as dams, bridges, wind
turbines, volcanoes, or tectonic plates. Given its broad application, this study focuses on the congruence model, which
simply assesses whether an object, or part of it, is deformed
or not (Welsch and Heunecke 2001).
In this context, the monitored structure is typically measured at least twice over time by means of a geodetic network,
and a testing procedure is applied to decide whether any
displacement has occurred after the first epoch. Typically,
the input data consist of coordinate differences between two
epochs, which are often estimated by least squares (Pelzer
1971; Mierlo 1977, 1978; Niemeier 1979; Caspary 2000;
Heunecke et al. 2013; Nowel 2020). Robust methods are
also popular and have advanced significantly in this field
(Chen 1983; Duchnowski 2010, 2013; Nowel and Kamiński
2014; Zienkiewicz 2015; Zienkiewicz and Baryla 2015;
Wiśniewski and Zienkiewicz 2016; Batilović et al. 2022),
but they are beyond the scope of this work.
The statistical procedure starts with the definition of the
null model H0 , which assumes that the observed coordinate
differences are solely due to probabilistic fluctuations. Under
this assumption, all network points are therefore treated as
stable. Since the unstable points are not known a priori, the
alternative hypotheses are formulated for the case where
at least one point is unstable. Here, one faces the problem
of dealing with multiple alternative hypotheses (Lehmann
2012; Yang et al. 2013). To decide among the available competing models, consecutive hypothesis tests are often applied
to identify one by one (Lehmann and Lösler 2017), in a
similar way to iterative data snooping (Baarda 1968; Kok
1984; Teunissen 2006). However, these iterative tests are statistically non-rigorous because they restrict the alternative
hypothesis to a single unstable point, and early misidentifications propagate through subsequent iterations (Nowel
2020). This limitation, especially in cases involving multiple displacements, has been highlighted by several authors
(Hekimoglu et al. 2010, 2014; Durdag et al. 2018; Nowel
2020).
To address the limitations of iterative procedures, a noniterative combinatorial approach has been proposed, in which
all possible combinations of displaced points are considered
simultaneously. This strategy enables the comparison of all
candidate deformation models in a single stage and removes

the need for point-by-point specification of the congruence

and discussed in detail by Velsink (2015) and Velsink (2018).
Another combinatorial method is discussed by Lehmann
and Lösler (2017), who apply various information criteria to
select the model with the smallest value among all candidates.
To account for models with different levels of complexity,
penalty terms are introduced to penalize overfitting when
more points are treated as unstable. As noted by Nowel
(2020), the combination of model fit and penalty defines the
identification criterion, although there is still no consensus on
which information criterion should be preferred. Moreover,
fully combinatorial methods can pose significant computational challenges, especially for networks of practical size.
Despite notable progress in this field, several challenges
remain and call for further investigation (Baselga 2011;
Duchnowski 2010, 2013; Zienkiewicz and Baryla 2015;
Zienkiewicz 2015; Wiśniewski and Zienkiewicz 2016; Biagi
and Caldera 2013; Wujanz et al. 2018).
Among recent contributions, Nowel (2020) introduced the
Combinatorial Iterative Detection, Identification, and Adaptation (CIDIA) procedure, which combines combinatorial
search with likelihood ratio tests within the Detection–
Identification–Adaptation (DIA) framework to overcome the
limitations of both point-by-point and purely combinatorial
approaches. A similar strategy was previously investigated
by Klein et al. (2017), although their focus was on identifying multiple simultaneous outliers.
CIDIA operates as follows. After the global test rejects the
null hypothesis, all candidates of equal dimension, typically
single-point shifts and, optionally, block-shift scenarios, are
examined, and the model that minimizes the sum of squared
residuals is selected. Because all candidates involve the same
number of parameters, no penalty term is required. The
selected model then replaces the current null model, and
a new global test is carried out with a significance level
coupled to the initial one to preserve comparable statistical
power. However, the identification step remains inherently
data-driven. Any choice of a model or hypothesis component
based on the inspected data introduces an additional layer of
randomness, because the model specification becomes subject to sampling variability (Kuchibhotla et al. 2022).
Any sequential procedure that updates the null model
based on the selected alternative and ignores post-selection
inference faces a structural limitation. First, the alternative
hypotheses form a highly dependent family, since many candidate models share substantial parts of both the functional
and stochastic models. This dependence complicates the
interpretation of the corresponding test statistics and moves
the procedure away from the simple framework of independent tests. Second, because the alternative model is selected
from a set of mutually non-nested hypotheses on the basis of
the observed data, either by choosing the best-fitting model

or the one with the largest test statistic, the procedure departs
from the classical likelihood-ratio framework. The null distribution that would hold for a pre-specified hypothesis no
longer applies to the post-selection test statistic. If subsequent
inference ignores this post-selection effect and still relies on
nominal critical values, the stepwise false-alarm probability
may be inflated, and the actual post-selection Type I error
can exceed the user-defined significance level.
In this contribution, we introduce a sequential combinatorial post-selection testing procedure. The method is
motivated by the fact that any data-driven choice among
competing models alters the distribution of the test statistic and invalidates classical likelihood-ratio theory. This
consequence is well documented in the statistical literature on post-selection inference, including resampling-based
multiple-testing frameworks (Westfall and Young 1993),
exact post-selection inference (Lee et al. 2016), and Monte
Carlo-based procedures designed to restore correct test levels (Dufour 2006). Following the general principle of Monte
Carlo calibration in post-selection contexts, a critical value
is computed under the current null hypothesis by explicitly
replicating the same selection rule applied in the sequential
procedure. Within this framework, the user-defined significance level is retained as a nominal upper bound throughout
the procedure. Once the correct set of displaced points is
identified at a given stage, any subsequent model expansion is
evaluated under conditional hypotheses that reflect this identical selection mechanism. As a result, we show empirically
that the false-alarm rate at later stages does not inflate and
remains less than or equal to the user-defined significance
level, which preserves statistical coherence throughout the
sequential procedure.
Another important aspect that arises here is the stopping
criterion associated with the sequential nature of the proposed
method. The sequential procedure ends once the current null
model can no longer be rejected. However, as the displacement of a single point may be distributed over several network
points, the procedure may be pushed to stages with increasingly complex alternative models, so that higher-dimensional
alternatives may fit the data spuriously well. As a result,
the method may identify more unstable points than actually exist. This effect becomes more pronounced in scenarios
with multiple simultaneous displacements. In the latter case,
the probability of statistical overlap also increases, as two
or more models may become practically indistinguishable
from one another (Yang et al. 2013). Therefore, a stopping
constraint is proposed here to define the maximum number
of points to be inspected. Although practical recommendations suggest that this number should remain well below the
total model redundancy (Lehmann and Lösler 2016, 2017),
it is still necessary to adopt additional strategies to ensure an
adequate level of hypothesis separability (Yang et al. 2013).
It is worth noting that the issue of non-separable hypotheses

has been discussed within the DIA framework by Zaminpardaz and Teunissen (2019), where specific adaptations may

Here, we adopt a strategy that first analyses the network
topology and then examines the orthogonal projection matrices associated with all admissible alternative models. The
topological analysis provides an upper bound on the number
of admissible displacement models, whereas the evaluation
of the projection matrices for point groups of the same dimension reveals when two models share the same projector. In
such cases, the corresponding hypotheses are not geometrically separable and that dimension is therefore not accepted
in the process. The analysis is then repeated for models of
lower dimension until the projection matrices associated with
all admissible combinations are distinct. However, we show
that geometric separability does not always prevent statistical overlap, although such cases become very rare once this
filter is applied. In this context, we also introduced a normalized distance between the projection matrices of competing
hypotheses to quantify their geometric separation. We also
briefly investigate how this metric relates to the chosen significance levels and to the signal-to-noise ratio, defined by
the displacement magnitude and the uncertainty of the epoch
difference vector. Nevertheless, we show that the geometric
criterion eliminates a major source of ambiguity that would
otherwise persist regardless of how large the true displacement is or how it manifests in the observation vector.
Another feature of the proposed method is that it builds
upon the univariate congruence model under the mean-shift
framework, which operates on inter-epoch differences of
(pseudo-)observations rather than on adjusted coordinate differences. The Fredericton approach has been used to identify
unstable points within a univariate framework by examining
changes in distances and/or angles between epochs (Chen
1983). These quantities, however, are derived from leastsquares adjusted coordinates. Although this procedure avoids
dependence on a specific datum, it introduces an additional
adjustment step and tends to become less conclusive when
several points produce similar test responses (Gökalp and
Taşcı 2009).
Nevertheless, the approach proposed here applies to both
linear (e.g. levelling and GNSS baseline) and nonlinear (e.g.
trilateration) geodetic networks without requiring model linearization, a process that may otherwise reduce detection
power (Lehmann and Lösler 2018). It also removes the need
to define a common datum or to apply the S-transformation
(Baarda 1973; Neitzel 2004; Aydin 2017; Nowel 2019;
Erdogan et al. 2021), thereby simplifying the displacement
analysis. This simplification is particularly advantageous for
networks with weak geometry and limited redundancy, as
emphasized by Chrzanowski et al. (1982). Hereafter, we refer
to the proposed approach as SEQCUP (Sequential and Combinatorial framework for Unstable Point identification).

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. First,
we describe SEQCUP and its underlying principles. Next,
we present the mathematical formulation and explain how
to construct the displacement design matrix for trilateration,
levelling, and GNSS baseline networks. We then introduce
a combined topological and projection-matrix separability
analysis to determine the upper limit of the identification process. Subsequently, we assess the performance of SEQCUP
through simulations on trilateration and GNSS baseline
networks, as well as on a real trilateration network with
controlled field displacements. The method is then externally compared with classical and contemporary approaches
reported in the specialized literature. Finally, we discuss the
conditions under which the proposed procedure may face
practical challenges and the strategies that can be adopted
to address such situations. We then summarize the main
contributions of this study and outline directions for future
research.

2 SEQCUP method and principles
Before presenting the proposed method, it is important to
recall that in conventional deformation analysis (CDA),
two sets of epoch-wise estimated coordinates of identical geodetic network points are typically obtained through
least-squares estimation (LSE) and then compared to identify unstable points. In that context, the coordinate vectors
and their covariance matrices at both epochs must refer
to a common datum, which can be established through
the theory of connecting two-epoch adjustment results
(Teunissen 1985).
In the proposed approach, the differences between two
epochs of geodetic observations of the same quantities
are used to determine whether a specified set of points
remains stable (congruent) between epochs. Here, we consider cases in which the observations are directly measured at
both epochs rather than derived from minimally constrained
network adjustments. This assumption does not affect the
formulation of the method, which remains valid whether the
compared observations are directly measured or computed
from adjusted coordinates. For simplicity, we consider only
the measurements that have passed quality control in each
epoch (for example, through an iterative data-snooping procedure) and are therefore assumed to be free of outliers.
Without loss of generality, the analysis is restricted to two
epochs.
The proposed framework is developed under the following
working assumptions:
• A two-epoch observation framework, in which all test
statistics and calibration procedures are formulated exclusively from inter-epoch differences.

stochastic components of the observation errors are independent between epochs.
• The variance factors of the observations are known or

In addition, we compute the empirical stepwise falsealarm rate, denoted by α̂(q j ), which in this case is evaluated
only up to (q j = q1 ). It is important to keep in mind that
overidentification rate (Pover ) occurs when the final specified
model contains more points than those truly displaced. This
represents a decision error in the final outcome of SEQCUP.
In contrast, the stepwise false-alarm rate α̂(q j ) refers to
a distinct phenomenon. It denotes the probability that the
stage-wise test statistic in (21) rejects the current null model
incorrectly when this null model corresponds to the true displaced point. Thus, α̂(q j ) quantifies Type I error rates of the
sequential expansion step (e.g. from q1 to q2 ), whereas Pover
quantifies that decision error rate in the final set of identified
unstable points.
Table 4 shows, for each nominal significance level, the
mean calibrated critical values cα (q1 ) and the corresponding empirical stepwise false-alarm rates α̂(q1 ) under the null
hypothesis at q1 . The mean critical values reported in Table 4
were obtained for each point individually, using all Monte
Carlo experiments associated with that point. The corresponding standard deviations ranged approximately from 1.0
to 0.3, with smaller values occurring for the largest cα (q1 ).
Note that α̂(q1 ) ≤ α in all cases. This behaviour is
expected, because a stepwise false alarm at the transition
from q1 to q2 can only occur in those Monte Carlo replications where the procedure actually reaches the expansion step
and the current null model at q1 coincides with the true displaced point. In many replications, especially those in which
no displacement is detected at the first stage, the algorithm
stops earlier and the transition to q2 is never tested, so a false
alarm cannot occur, as can be seen in Fig. 12. As a consequence, the empirical estimates α̂(q1 ) are conservative with
respect to the user-defined level α.

4 Discussion
The numerical experiments and literature-based evaluations
presented in this work show that SEQCUP achieves effective control of the probability of the false alarms while
preserving a competitive, and often superior, capability for
displacement identification. The method keeps the classical
intuition of a sequential expansion of the null model, but corrects for post-selection effects via Monte Carlo calibration at
each stage. In addition, the procedure combines topological
screening, projector-based separability, and the normalized
distance between displacement subspaces to define a technically consistent stopping level qmax for each network.
The simulation under the true-null hypothesis in the trilateration network confirms that the calibrated critical values
cα reproduce the nominal false-alarm levels. The empirical non-alarm probabilities PNF remain compatible with
the target levels 1 − α for all tested significance levels
(Table 3). When the procedure operates under a true single displacement, the stepwise false-alarm rates α̂(q1 ) at
the transition from q1 to q2 stay below the prescribed α
(Table 4, Fig. 12). On the other hand, when the displacement
magnitude becomes more pronounced, that is, under high
signal-to-noise conditions, the sequential procedure reaches
the expansion stage more frequently and the empirical stepwise false-alarm rate moves closer to the nominal level α
prescribed in the initial stage of SEQCUP (see Fig. 12).
Increasing the significance level enlarges the rejection region
of the test, which improves the detection and identification
rates for displacements close to the noise level. However,
this choice also amplifies overidentification for high signalto-noise conditions, as clearly reflected in the behaviour
of Pover in Fig. 10. This increase in Pover can be interpreted as a swamping effect, in which truly stable points
are absorbed into higher-dimensional displacement models
and are incorrectly classified as unstable. A similar pattern
appears in the GNSS baseline-vector experiment (Table 7),

A sequential post-selection testing procedure for univariate congruence models with nested…

where the empirical rates remain inside Wilson 95% confidence intervals that include the nominal significance levels.
These results indicate that the post-selection calibration strategy succeeds in keeping the stepwise Type I errors under
control, while still allowing flexibility in the choice of α.
The Monte Carlo calibration employed in SEQCUP is
conditional on the assumed stochastic model under the null
hypothesis. Moderate cross-epoch correlations, if neglected,
may alter the effective covariance structure of the inter-epoch
difference vector and lead to biased critical values, which
can in turn cause conservative or liberal stepwise false-alarm
rates depending on the sign and magnitude of the correlation.
Similarly, errors in the variance factors affect the scaling of
the test statistics and therefore shift the calibrated thresholds, potentially impacting the nominal Type I error control.
While the post-selection Monte Carlo strategy mitigates part
of this sensitivity by reproducing the same selection mechanism under the assumed model, substantial misspecification
would require extending the calibration to explicitly account
for uncertainty in the covariance structure.
The experiments also highlight the fundamental distinction between detection and identification and clarify how this
distinction relates to signal-to-noise conditions and network
geometry. In the trilateration network, the probability maps
of detection, correct identification, wrong identification, and
overidentification (Figs. 7, 8, 9 and 10) reveal a strong dependence on the line-of-sight geometry. Displacements aligned
with the dominant sight directions tend to be detected and
identified more easily, whereas displacements in directions
nearly orthogonal to these lines become much harder to identify, especially when the magnitude is close to the two-epoch
uncertainty σe ≈ 2.82 mm. The geometrical analysis in
Fig. 11 explains these patterns in terms of intersections of
trilateration circles.
In the GNSS baseline-vector experiment, the mean failure
and success rates (Figs. 15 and 16) show that reliable identification of three simultaneously displaced points emerges
only when the displacement magnitude reaches a level of
about twice the average standard deviation of the twoepoch observation differences. This behaviour is consistent
with the intuitive transition from noise-dominated to signaldominated regimes.
The role of network geometry appears more explicitly in
the analyses based on the normalized distance d H A between
projection matrices. In the GNSS network, the average value
d̄ H A ≈ 0.82 for the transition between q3 and q4 indicates
a high degree of geometrical separability between consecutive models, which favours stable identification once the
signal-to-noise ratio reaches moderate levels. In contrast,
for the levelling networks adopted from Nowel (2020) and
Erdogan et al. (2021), the initial choices of qmax based only
on topological criteria and on checking for equality of the

candidates lead to ranges of d H A that are clearly too low
for a reliable sequential expansion. For example, in the first
levelling network in Fig. 17, the transition between q9 and
q10 yields mean distances near 0.5 and minima close to 0.3,
which point to only modest geometrical separation and a
high risk of displacement smear among competing models.
The diagnostic based on d H A then supports a more cautious
choice of qmax = q8 , for which the distribution of distances
shifts to the high-separation regime with d̄ H A ≈ 0.8. Under
this setting, SEQCUP reaches an MSR of about 85% for the
challenging q8 scenario, which is more consistent with the
geometrical characteristics of the network than the original
configuration with qmax = q10 .
A similar conclusion arises in the second levelling network, where the initial upper bound qmax = q6 yields
only moderate geometrical separability between q5 and q6
(d̄ H A ≈ 0.67) and reveals a non-negligible fraction of
model pairs with distances near 0.4. The revised bound
qmax = q5 moves the average distance to d̄ H A ≈ 0.84,
with most pairs concentrated in the high-separation range.
Under this configuration, SEQCUP preserves superior identification performance relative to both the classical CDA and
the univariate approach, even under low-to-moderate signalto-noise ratios. Additional experiments with five displaced
points under qmax = q6 confirm that when qmax exceeds
the range suggested by the geometrical analysis, sensitivity deteriorates unless the displacement magnitudes increase
substantially. These observations support the idea that the
choice of qmax should not rely solely on redundancy counts
or on simple rules-of-thumb. A joint assessment of network
topology, projector inequality, normalized distances, and the
expected displacement range is necessary to obtain a consistent and interpretable sequential procedure.
The case studies suggest that the normalized distance d H A
can also be used to derive a practical guideline for selecting
qmax . For the trilateration and GNSS networks, as well as
for the two levelling networks, all configurations that produced stable identification behaviour shared the property
that the average distance between consecutive dimensions
satisfied d̄ H A (q j , q j+1 )  0.8 at the chosen upper bound
qmax . In these cases, most admissible pairs of models fell
into a high-separation region, and SEQCUP maintained high
mean success rates even under moderate signal-to-noise conditions.
Conversely, when d̄ H A dropped to approximately 0.5–
0.7 for the transition from qmax −1 to qmax , the sequential
expansion became more vulnerable to displacement smearing and swamping, and acceptable performance could only
be achieved for substantially larger displacement magnitudes. We therefore propose the following empirical rule:
as a default choice, qmax should be selected so that the transition from qmax −1 to qmax satisfies d̄ H A ≈ 0.8 (with the
majority of pairs above d H A ≈ 0.7). Values of d̄ H A between

123

23

Page 32 of 38

about 0.65 and 0.8 may still be acceptable in applications
targeting large displacements, but should be accompanied
by an explicit Monte Carlo assessment of failure rates. Configurations with d̄ H A  0.65 at the upper transition are not
recommended for routine use, as they tend to amplify masking and swamping effects in the sequential expansion.
The comparisons with existing methods further clarify the
strengths and trade-offs of SEQCUP. In the first levelling network, SEQCUP, CIDIA, GCT, and V maintain high efficacy
when only one point is displaced. As the number of displaced
points increases, V rapidly loses effectiveness and essentially fails for q8 , whereas AICc, although weak for small
q, becomes very effective when more than half of the points
move, which confirms its suitability for heavily distorted configurations. SEQCUP preserves stable performance over the
entire range and remains comparable to CIDIA and AICc
for the most demanding scenarios. In the second levelling
network, SEQCUP consistently outperforms both the univariate approach and CDA for all tested configurations and
significance levels, especially when more than one point is
displaced. This behaviour is consistent with the fact that the
univariate approach further reduces redundancy by introducing an additional mean parameter and does not explicitly
control the false-alarm rate in the sequential specification of
the displacement model.
Taken together, the experiments illustrate that SEQCUP
combines three key elements: (i) explicit control of stepwise
false alarms through post-selection calibration; (ii) a univariate model based on two-epoch observation differences,
which makes the method invariant to the choice of datum
and preserves a strictly linear formulation, thereby preventing the loss of detection power typically associated with
model linearization (Lehmann and Lösler 2018); and (iii)
a geometry-aware stopping rule informed by projector analysis and normalized distances. The results suggest that these
components act in a complementary way. The calibration
strategy keeps the false-positive rates at or below the target
level, the univariate formulation produces a simple functional
model, and the geometrical diagnostic restricts the sequential expansion to ranges in which displacement smear and
statistical overlap are less likely.
From a computational standpoint, the dominant cost of
SEQCUP comes from the post-selection Monte Carlo calibration of the critical values. In the experiments reported in 3,
each calibration stage used M = 1, 000 simulations and the
algorithm was parallelized on a 6-core pool in MATLAB. On
a standard personal computer (13th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM)
i7-13620 H @ 2.40 GHz, 32 GB RAM), a single application
of SEQCUP to the levelling network adapted from Nowel
(2020) in the most demanding configuration (8 out of 12
points displaced, qmax = q10 and α = 10%) required about
4.55 s in total. Of this time, 4.54 s were spent in the Monte
Carlo calibration of the post-selection critical values (7 calls,

123

V. F. Rofatto et al.

with an average of 0.65 s per call), whereas the deterministic
part of the algorithm (adjustment and statistics computation) accounted for only 0.013 s. For the levelling network
adapted from Erdogan et al. (2021) under the configuration
with 5 out of 8 points displaced and qmax = q6 , the total runtime per SEQCUP application was approximately 0.31 s, of
which 0.305 s were spent in critical value calibration (5 calls,
≈ 0.061 s each) and 0.0037 s in the remaining steps. For the
GNSS baseline network (6 stations, 13 baselines), a single
application of SEQCUP, including post-selection calibration
with M = 1,000 simulations per stage, required about 0.2 s.
Thus, the computational effort grows linearly with the number of observations and with the Monte Carlo sample size,
and increases combinatorially with the maximum number of
points qmax to be inspected. In practice, however, the stopping rule on qmax and the early termination when the current
null model is accepted keep the number of evaluated models at a moderate level, so that single SEQCUP applications
remain easily tractable on standard desktop hardware. Computational cost details for each experiment in this study are
provided in Appendix D.
An important practical aspect is the choice of the number of Monte Carlo simulations M used in the calibration of
the critical values at each sequential stage. Larger M stabilize
the estimated quantiles and false-alarm rates, but increase the
computational cost in an almost linear way. For very small
nominal levels, such as α = 0.1%, the Monte Carlo uncertainty can be quantified by Wilson 95% confidence intervals.
For example, for a nominal level of α = 0.1%, n sim =
1, 000 simulations under the parameterized null hypothesis
for displacement yield an empirical false-alarm rate with a
95% confidence interval of approximately [0.02%, 0.56%],
whereas with n sim = 5, 000 this interval shrinks to about
[0.04%, 0.23%]. In this study, using between 1, 000 and
5, 000 simulations per stage provided a reasonable compromise between computational cost and numerical accuracy
of the calibrated critical values for deformation-monitoring
applications.
Future research may explore more efficient Monte Carlo
schemes for critical value calibration, for instance, by
using variance-reduction techniques or surrogate models that
approximate the test quantiles with fewer simulations. The
current diagnostic for qmax is largely empirical and network
dependent, and the thresholds for d H A that characterize “sufficient’ separability have not yet been derived from first
principles. Moreover, geometrical separability does not guarantee full statistical separability, especially under very low
signal-to-noise conditions. These aspects indicate natural
directions for further research, such as the derivation of theoretical bounds that link d H A to decision errors.
In this contribution, we restricted attention to cases where
the epoch-wise covariance matrices are treated as known,
which is a common setting in engineering geodesy once a

A sequential post-selection testing procedure for univariate congruence models with nested…

stable stochastic model has been established from instrument specifications, prior calibration, or separate variancecomponent estimation. This does not represent an intrinsic
limitation of the SEQCUP framework. In principle, estimated
variance components and cross-epoch correlations can be
incorporated as nuisance parameters in the Monte Carlo calibration stage, following the maximized Monte Carlo (MMC)
approach of Dufour (2006). In such an extension, the test
statistic would be simulated under a range of plausible covariance structures and the resulting simulated p-values would
be maximized over the corresponding parameter set, thereby
preserving control of the false-alarm rate even in the presence of stochastic model uncertainty. A detailed treatment of
this extension will be addressed in future research.
Finally, when pseudo-observations are adopted, they
should be employed only when the augmented model preserves full rank and satisfies the topological admissibility
conditions defined through the C-matrix, which guarantee
stable and non-equivalent projection operators. Compared
to direct differencing, pseudo-observations may lead to a
moderate variance inflation due to additional correlation and
constraint effects. The magnitude of this inflation depends
primarily on network geometry and local redundancy. Before
application, it is recommended to verify the presence of
adequate local redundancy at candidate points, acceptable
conditioning of the normal equations without extreme leverage effects, and sufficient geometrical separability between
consecutive model dimensions. In practice, stable behaviour
was observed when the average normalized distance satisfied
d̄ H A  0.8 at the chosen qmax . Detailed numerical analyses
supporting these criteria are presented in Sects. 2.5 and 3.

5 Summary and conclusions
This contribution introduced SEQCUP, a sequential combinatorial procedure for the identification of unstable points
in geodetic monitoring networks when both the number
and location of displaced points are a priori unknown. The
method relies on a univariate model based on two-epoch
observation differences, remains invariant to the choice of
datum, and preserves a strictly linear formulation. A Monte
Carlo calibration step explicitly accounts for post-selection
effects at each stage and keeps the stepwise false-alarm rates
at or below the nominal significance level.
The simulation experiments with trilateration, GNSS
baseline, and levelling networks, as well as the literaturebased evaluations, indicate that SEQCUP maintains effective
control of false alarms and reaches high mean success rates
for model identification under a broad range of signal-tonoise conditions. In particular, SEQCUP performs competitively with, and often surpasses, classical and recent
approaches such as GCT, Velsink’s method, AICc-based

Page 33 of 38

23

selection, CIDIA, the univariate model error approach, and
CDA, especially in scenarios with multiple simultaneously
displaced points. The results also confirm that reliable
identification tends to emerge when the displacement magnitude exceeds twice the average standard deviation of the
two-epoch observation differences in geometrically wellseparated networks.
A second key feature of SEQCUP is the geometrical stopping rule based on a combination of topological analysis,
equality of projection matrices, and the normalized distance
between competing hypothesis subspaces. The case studies show that a purely topological definition of qmax can be
overly optimistic. In contrast, thresholds for the normalized
distance that place the majority of model pairs in a highseparation regime (for example, mean d H A values near 0.8)
lead to more reliable sequential expansions and reduce masking and swamping effects. In practice, the restriction on qmax
and the frequent early acceptance of the null model keep the
computational effort of SEQCUP at a moderate level for the
network sizes considered, even with Monte Carlo calibration
at each stage.
The present study focused on the common situation in
which the epoch-wise covariance matrices are treated as
given, based on instrument specifications, prior calibration,
and separate variance-component analyses. This does not
constitute an intrinsic limitation of the SEQCUP framework. Future work will implement extensions where variance
components and cross-epoch correlations enter as nuisance
parameters in the Monte Carlo calibration, in line with maximized Monte Carlo ideas, and will address multi-epoch
deformation sequences and more general stochastic models. These developments will extend the applicability of
SEQCUP to a wider class of monitoring configurations while
preserving rigorous control of the false-alarm rate.
