# Collaborative policy planning

In a fixed-policy video agent, one pre-defined rule decides which tools are called and in what order. In collaborative policy planning (CPP) several agents work on the same question at once, and each first generates its own tool-invocation policy: "agent generates its unique tool invocation policy tailored" to the query.[^c1] Each agent then executes its policy step by step, and the plans are not frozen. "after each step of execution, each agent receives video clues from peers," and each agent decides whether to refine its original policy.[^c2] The mechanism has three parts that can be varied separately: how the plans are generated, what passes between agents during execution, and how the group's conclusions are merged into one answer.

The paper's comparison with fixed rules is the contrast the mechanism is built on. Existing agents "adhere to pre-defined rules for tool selection and invocation during video understanding" and so do not adapt their plan to the clues they find.[^c3] A group of independently planned agents is a different object: the paper's reading is that the gain comes from diversity among the agents, not from more parameters alone. Group size helps, since "performance consistently improves as the total number of agents increases."[^c4] Architecture matters too. In a 4-agent setup, a group where some agents share an architecture gains less than a fully heterogeneous one, because "structural redundancy among agents reduces discussion diversity, leading to diminished collaborative gains compared to fully heterogeneous groups."[^c5] The paper says that for diverse groups, CPP "enables greater performance improvements than cooperation among homogeneous agents."[^c6]

The merge step is a vote. Of three ways to aggregate the agents' conclusions, majority vote scored best. It "further elevates performance to 60.5/82.3" on the two benchmarks in that table, above the best-score rule (59.9/81.2) and a rule that defers to one chosen model (60.2/81.6).[^c7] The authors read this as showing that "the diversity generated by independent agent planning is best leveraged through lightweight majority consensus, outperforming score-based or authoritybased selection strategies."[^c8]

Training is the second half of the mechanism. Four untrained foundation-model teams under the same protocol stayed below the trained group, and the authors take the gap to show that "collaborative fine-tuning injects task-specific coordination patterns that even stronger proprietary models fail to discover via zero-shot reasoning alone."[^c9] In the ablation, removing the process reward "drops performance by one point on both benchmarks," and disabling agent dropout costs two points, which the authors read as showing that "dynamic topology is the most" critical component.[^c10][^c11] The full configuration, they write, confirms "that dense process feedback and stochastic communication are both necessary for optimal collaborative policy learning."[^c12]

## Limits

- The evidence is the authors' own, on video benchmarks: the headline claim is SOTA "across eight benchmarks,"[^c13] but the ablations I read use only two. The tables are the authors' numbers; no independent replication is in the text.
- The architecture-diversity result rests on one 4-agent setup. Whether diversity from sampling alone, rather than from different models, gives the same gain is not tested in the parts read.
- The vote comparison is among three rules, one of which uses a single chosen model. The claim that voting beats score-based selection is a result for these three rules, not a general law about aggregation.
- A vote presupposes that each agent's conclusion is a discrete answer. Whether it transfers to open-ended video questions is my inference, not tested here.
- The method section (the exact MARL objective and the dropout mechanism) was not read in full; the mechanism above is taken from the ablation labels and the introduction. Costs in calls and tokens of running several agents with tools were not in the parts read.
