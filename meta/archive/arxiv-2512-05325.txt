Abstract
Large reasoning models achieve strong performance on complex tasks by generating extended chains of thought,
but they often “overthink”: continuing to reason long after they internally have enough information to answer
correctly. This wastes inference-time compute and can even hurt accuracy. Existing attempts to stop early either
manipulate decoding with extra sampling and heuristics, rely on auxiliary verifier models, or operate only as
post-hoc analysis pipelines without formal guarantees. We introduce LYNX, an online early-exit mechanism that
turns a model’s own hidden-state awareness into confidence-controlled stopping decisions. LYNX attaches exit
decisions to naturally occurring reasoning cues (e.g., “hmm”, “wait”) during generation, trains a lightweight
probe on hidden states at those cue tokens using supervision from forced exits, and wraps the resulting scores in
split conformal prediction to obtain distribution-free control over the rate of premature exits. Crucially, we train
and calibrate this probe once on a generic mathematical corpus and then reuse it unchanged across benchmarks,
decoding temperatures, and even non-mathematical tasks. Across three model families spanning 1.5B to 32B
parameters (DeepSeek-R1-1.5B, QwQ-32B, and Llama-3.1-Nemotron-8B), a single mathematically trained probe
per base model yields strong accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs. On GSM8K, LYNX matches or improves baseline
accuracy while reducing tokens by 40–65%; on MATH-500 it improves accuracy by up to 12 points with roughly
35–60% fewer tokens; on AIME 2024 it recovers baseline accuracy with more than 50% token savings; and
on CommonsenseQA, a non-math benchmark, it transfers zero-shot with modest accuracy gains and up to
70% fewer tokens. Compared to state-of-the-art early-exit methods, LYNX offers competitive or superior Pareto
frontiers while remaining fully online, requiring no proxy models at inference, and providing explicit, user-tunable
confidence guarantees. Code is available at https://github.com/farukakgul/LYNX.

1. Introduction
Reasoning-oriented large language models (LLMs) have recently achieved breakthrough performance on
mathematical problem solving, logical puzzles, and competitive programming by generating long, explicit
chains of thought (Wei et al., 2022, OpenAI, 2024). Models such as OpenAI’s o1 (OpenAI, 2024), DeepSeekR1 (Guo et al., 2025), Google DeepMind’s Gemini 2.5 (Google DeepMind, 2025), and Anthropic’s Claude
3.7 Sonnet (Anthropic, 2025) routinely emit hundreds or thousands of tokens per problem, exploring and
revising candidate solution paths before committing to a final answer. This test-time “thinking” is a major
source of their strength, but it also raises a central question: how can we allocate this inference-time compute
more intelligently, stopping early when further reasoning is unlikely to help?
Recent empirical studies reveal that large reasoning models frequently exhibit three critical failure modes during extended chain-of-thought generation (Su et al., 2025). First, models often arrive at correct intermediate
solutions but continue reasoning and talk themselves into incorrect final answers, a form of counterproductive
overthinking. Second, even when maintaining correctness, models generate extensive redundant reasoning
that provides no additional value, wasting computational resources without improving accuracy. Third, some
problems trigger unbounded reasoning loops that exhaust the model’s context window before producing any
answer, leading to complete failure. These observations suggest that intelligent early-exit mechanisms are
not merely an efficiency optimization but a fundamental requirement for reliable deployment of reasoning
models.
In this paper we ask: Can we turn a model’s internal confidence signals into an online, cue-triggered early-exit
rule with explicit statistical guarantees? We answer this in the affirmative with LYNX, a framework that enables
reasoning models to leverage their own hidden-state awareness for confidence-controlled stopping decisions.
Unlike existing approaches that manipulate decoding or rely on external verifier models, LYNX operates
along three key axes:
1. Cue-triggered exits. Reasoning models naturally emit structural cues during their thinking process,
tokens like “hmm”, “wait”, “alternatively”, or paragraph breaks that mark transitions between reasoning
segments. Rather than suppressing these cues, we treat them as decision points: whenever a cue appears,
we consider stopping.
2. Self-contained supervision. Instead of relying on a proxy LLM to extract and grade intermediate
answers, we generate training labels by forcing exits at cue positions during data collection: we cut
the chain of thought, prompt the base model to output its final answer, and compare against the
ground-truth label. This yields a large set of cue-level examples where each hidden state is labeled as
“safe to exit now” or “continue thinking”, using only the base model and task answers.
3. Conformal confidence control. A fixed probe threshold is brittle across datasets and decoding settings.
We therefore wrap probe scores in split conformal prediction (Vovk et al., 2005, Shafer and Vovk, 2008,
Angelopoulos and Bates, 2021), turning them into an adaptive stop/continue rule with a user-chosen
miscoverage level. This yields a distribution-free guarantee on the rate of premature exits under mild
exchangeability assumptions.
A central design goal of LYNX is deployability. Training a new probe and retuning thresholds for every benchmark would undermine the goal of efficient inference. Instead, for each base model, we train and calibrate
LYNX once on a generic mathematical corpus and then freeze it. In our experiments, we instantiate LYNX
on modern open-source reasoning models from three distinct families, including Qwen-based architectures
(DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, QwQ-32B) and Llama-based architectures (Llama-3.1-Nemotron-8B), using
a moderate number of problems from a mathematical training corpus to (i) collect cue-triggered forced-exit

labels and (ii) calibrate conformal thresholds. The resulting probe and thresholds are then applied unchanged
across four math benchmarks and a non-math benchmark, demonstrating strong generalization across model
scales (1.5B to 32B parameters), architectural families, task domains, and decoding temperatures.
Our experiments show that this simple, reusable early-exit head yields strong accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs.
On GSM8K, LYNX matches or improves baseline accuracy while reducing tokens by up to 60–65% across
different models; on MATH-500, it improves accuracy by up to 12 points with approximately 35–60% fewer
tokens; on AIME 2024, it recovers baseline accuracy with more than half the tokens removed; and on
CommonsenseQA it transfers zero-shot with modest accuracy gains and substantial token savings. When
compared to state-of-the-art early-exit methods under matched settings, LYNX consistently provides more
favorable Pareto frontiers, while remaining fully online and providing explicit confidence guarantees.
In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:
1. We introduce LYNX , a novel online early-exit framework that combines cue-triggered decision points,
self-contained probe training via forced exits, and conformalized confidence control to enable reasoning
models to make statistically-guaranteed stopping decisions based on their own hidden states, without
modifying decoding, injecting tokens, or requiring external verifiers.
2. We demonstrate cross-architecture generalizability across three model families (Qwen and Llama),
model scales (1.5B to 32B parameters), and task domains (mathematical and commonsense reasoning),
showing that a single mathematically-trained probe per base model transfers effectively without per-task
retraining.
3. We establish superior accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs compared to state-of-the-art methods, with
experiments showing LYNX matches or improves baseline accuracy while achieving 35–65% token
reductions, and provide the first deployment-ready early-exit method with explicit, user-tunable
confidence guarantees via conformal prediction.

2. Related Work
We situate LYNX within three complementary research areas: probing neural representations, inference-time
optimization for reasoning, and conformal prediction for reliable decisions.
Probing internal representations for verification. Probing uses simple classifiers trained on hidden states
to extract information that models encode internally but may not explicitly output (Alain and Bengio, 2016,
Hewitt and Liang, 2019). Zhang et al. demonstrate that reasoning models encode rich signals about solution
correctness in their hidden states: a lightweight probe on activations at intermediate “final answer” tokens
can predict answer correctness with strong calibration on mathematical benchmarks (Zhang et al., 2025).
Related work on latent knowledge shows that hidden states contain information about truth and uncertainty
even when not directly expressed in outputs (Burns et al., 2023). However, these verification pipelines are
fundamentally post-hoc, requiring full trace generation, answer extraction using auxiliary models, offline
labeling, and post-processing. LYNX bridges this gap by using probes online during generation: we train
on hidden states at naturally occurring cues using self-contained supervision from forced exits, enabling
confidence-controlled stopping decisions without external labeling.
Inference-time optimization for efficient reasoning. Large reasoning models frequently overthink,
generating unnecessarily long chains of thought that waste compute and sometimes hurt accuracy (Zhang
et al., 2025, Yang et al., 2025, Jiang et al., 2025, Yong et al., 2025, Tan et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2025, Su
et al., 2025). DEER (Yang et al., 2025) detects action-transition tokens as exit points, elicits trial answers,

Conformal prediction for reliable decisions. Conformal prediction provides finite-sample, distributionfree guarantees on prediction error, requiring only that test examples are exchangeable with a held-out
calibration set (Vovk et al., 2005, Shafer and Vovk, 2008, Angelopoulos and Bates, 2021). Recent work applies
conformal methods to language models: Quach et al. construct prediction sets over token sequences with
coverage guarantees (Quach et al., 2023); and Savcisens et al. uses conformal probes to analyze the trilemma
between accuracy, informativeness, and guaranteed coverage in LLM outputs (Savcisens and Eliassi-Rad,
2025). In the standard setting, given a base predictor f , calibration set {( xi , yi )}in=1 , and nonconformity
score S( x, y), one computes the empirical (1 − δ)-quantile q1−δ of calibration scores to form prediction sets
C( xtest ) = {y : S( xtest , y) ≤ q1−δ } that satisfy Pr[ytest ∈ C( xtest )] ≥ 1 − δ. LYNX applies this framework
to probe scores estimating whether a partial chain of thought is sufficient to answer correctly, yielding a
conformalized threshold that implements a stop/continue rule with user-chosen confidence level c = 1 − δ
and an explicit guarantee on incorrect early exits. To our knowledge, LYNX is the first early-exit method for
reasoning models to provide such distribution-free statistical guarantees.

3. Method
Having established that (i) reasoning models encode internal correctness signals in hidden states, (ii) existing
early-exit methods lack statistical guarantees and require per-task tuning, and (iii) conformal prediction
can provide distribution-free control over prediction errors, we now describe how LYNX combines these
insights into a practical early-exit framework. The key challenge is to transform post-hoc probing pipelines
into an online decision mechanism with explicit confidence guarantees that generalizes across tasks without
retraining.
LYNX operates in two stages (Figure 2). Offline, we generate chains-of-thought on a mathematical corpus,
identify natural cue tokens, construct forced exits at each cue to obtain supervision, and train a probe with
conformal calibration on hidden states. Online, we run the base model unchanged; whenever a cue appears,
the probe scores its hidden state, and a conformalized threshold decides whether to exit or continue. We
formalize each component below, and provide a consolidated pseudo-code description in Algorithm 1 in
Appendix A.

3.4. Conformalized early-exit rule
The probe gψ produces a scalar confidence score pi (e) ∈ [0, 1] for each cue, but how should we convert this
into an exit decision? A fixed threshold (e.g., “exit if p ≥ 0.9”) is brittle across datasets and temperatures.
Instead, we apply split conformal prediction (§2) to obtain a data-driven threshold with explicit statistical
guarantees.

which controls the tradeoff between efficiency and reliability: higher c means more conservative exits (fewer
tokens saved, lower error rate), while lower c means more aggressive exits (more tokens saved, higher error
rate). We set the miscoverage level δ = 1 − c and calibrate a threshold qδ as follows.

Statistical guarantee and practical considerations. Under the standard exchangeability assumption,
that test examples are drawn from the same distribution as calibration data (Vovk et al., 2005, Shafer and
Vovk, 2008), this split-conformal construction controls the fraction of incorrect early exits at approximately
δ. Formally, if ( xtest , etest ) is exchangeable with Dcal and we exit at etest , then Pr[y(etest ) = 1] ≥ 1 − δ
(Angelopoulos and Bates, 2021).
In practice, strict exchangeability would require calibrating on a held-out portion of each test benchmark.
However, standard reasoning benchmarks (AIME, AMC etc.) do not provide separate calibration splits, and
creating such splits would reduce the number of available test examples. Instead, we calibrate LYNX once
on a generic mathematical corpus and reuse the resulting thresholds across all downstream benchmarks.
This design choice prioritizes deployability, enabling zero-shot transfer without per-task tuning, at the
potential cost of some miscalibration under domain shift. Our experiments (§4) show that this single
mathematically-trained probe generalizes effectively across multiple math benchmarks and even to nonmathematical commonsense reasoning tasks, suggesting that the probe learns domain-general signals of
reasoning sufficiency rather than task-specific patterns. For applications requiring stricter guarantees,
practitioners can recalibrate thresholds on a small held-out subset of their target distribution.
For each base model and decoding temperature, we precompute thresholds for a grid of confidence levels
(e.g., c ∈ {0.97, 0.95, 0.90, 0.80, 0.70}) during the offline calibration stage, allowing users to select their
preferred accuracy-efficiency operating point at deployment time without additional computation.

4. Experiments
Having established LYNX’s theoretical framework, we now empirically show how our framework preserves
reasoning accuracy while substantially reducing inference cost, with behavior that generalizes across model
families, architectures, scales, and tasks.
4.1. Experimental setup
Models and LYNX instantiation. We evaluate LYNX on three open-source reasoning models spanning two architectural families and an order of magnitude in scale: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (DeepSeek-AI,
2025), QwQ-32B (Team, 2025) (both Qwen-based), and Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Nano-8B-v1 (Bercovich
et al., 2025) (Llama-based). All three models emit explicit <think> blocks and natural reasoning cues
(“hmm”, “wait”, “alternatively”), making them suitable for cue-triggered early exit.
8

LYNX : Learning Dynamic Exits for Confidence-Controlled Reasoning

For each base model, we instantiate a single LYNX head by training a probe and calibrating conformal
thresholds on Hendrycks MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021) at temperature T = 0.6, following the procedure in
§3. Critically, this same probe is then deployed unchanged at both T = 0.6 and T = 0.0 across all evaluation
benchmarks, testing LYNX’s robustness to decoding temperature shifts without retraining.
Training and calibration data. We use Hendrycks MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021), a challenging mathematical reasoning dataset with seven subject categories (Algebra, Counting & Probability, Geometry, Intermediate
Algebra, Number Theory, Prealgebra, Precalculus) and five difficulty levels. For the DeepSeek-1.5B model,
we draw 400 training and 200 calibration problems per category (2,800 train, 1,400 calibration total);
for QwQ-32B and Nemotron-8B, we use 200 train and 100 calibration per category to balance compute
constraints. On each problem we generate a single long CoT rollout at T = 0.6, extract cue positions,
construct forced-exit labels as in §3.2, and split into probe-training (Dprobe ) and calibration (Dcal ) sets.
Implementation details (probe architecture, optimization, hardware) are in Appendix B.

Evaluation benchmarks.

We evaluate on five benchmarks:

• GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021): Grade-school math word problems (1,319 test examples).
• MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2023): 500-problem subset of competition mathematics.
• AMC 2023 (amc): American Mathematics Competition problems (40 problems).
• AIME 2024 (aim): American Invitational Mathematics Examination (30 problems).

5. Results
We now demonstrate that LYNX achieves superior accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs compared to state-of-the-art
baselines while providing interpretable, statistically-grounded control over early exits. Our analysis addresses
three questions: (i) Does LYNX form better Pareto frontiers than DEER and ToN? (ii) Does the confidence
knob c behave predictably across datasets, models, and temperatures? (iii) How do different model families
and decoding settings affect early-exit performance?
5.1. Main results
Table 2 summarizes accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs at T = 0.0 for DeepSeek-R1-1.5B and QwQ-32B across
all five benchmarks. For each dataset we report vanilla CoT (Baseline), DEER, ToN, and LYNX at multiple
confidence levels c. Boldface configurations indicate recommended operating points that balance accuracy
preservation with token efficiency: specifically, we highlight (i) the highest-performing baseline method per
dataset, and (ii) for LYNX, the configuration that maximizes token savings while maintaining accuracy within
2 percentage points of baseline, a conservative threshold demonstrating that substantial efficiency gains are
achievable without sacrificing correctness. These highlighted points serve as interpretable defaults, though
users can select any c along LYNX’s Pareto frontier depending on their accuracy-vs-efficiency priorities.
Accuracy–efficiency dominance. Across GSM8K, MATH-500, and CommonsenseQA, LYNX consistently
traces better Pareto frontiers than both baselines. On GSM8K with DeepSeek-1.5B, LYNX at c = 0.80 improves
accuracy over baseline (76.80% vs 75.97%, +1.09pp) while reducing tokens by 66.2%. On MATH-500,
c = 0.80 yields +3.24pp accuracy (70.20% vs 68.00%) with 60.1% fewer tokens, simultaneously addressing
overthinking and improving correctness. For CommonsenseQA, c = 0.70 matches baseline accuracy (40.62%
vs 40.54%) with 73.9% token reduction, demonstrating zero-shot transfer from mathematical training to
non-mathematical reasoning.

Similar patterns hold for QwQ-32B: on MATH-500, c = 0.95 maintains accuracy within 0.22pp while saving
22.4% of tokens; on CommonsenseQA, c = 0.70 achieves 61.4% savings with minimal accuracy loss. Figure 3

The confidence knob in practice. Although LYNX exposes a continuum of operating points via c, users need
interpretable defaults. The boldface configurations in Table 2 follow a simple rule: maximize token savings
subject to with minimal accuracy drop. Under this heuristic, optimal c typically lies in {0.80, 0.90, 0.95},
saving 32–66% of tokens across benchmarks. Critically, c behaves monotonically, higher values yield more
conservative exits and better accuracy preservation, exactly as predicted by the conformal framework (§3.4).
This makes c a meaningful user-facing parameter rather than a brittle hyperparameter requiring per-task
grid search.
Comparison to DEER and Think-or-Not. DEER and ToN are strong baselines but exhibit characteristic
weaknesses. On MATH-500 with DeepSeek-1.5B, DEER achieves 59.8% token savings but decreases accuracy
(66.80% vs 68.00%), whereas LYNX simultaneously improves accuracy and saves tokens. The exception
is AIME 2024, where DEER achieves notable accuracy gains; however, LYNX still provides competitive
coverage-controlled operating points (e.g., c = 0.70 matches baseline accuracy with 55.5% savings). ToN
consistently sits inside LYNX’s Pareto frontier once we account for its tree-search overhead, for example, on
GSM8K it achieves only 49.0% savings (vs LYNX’s 66.2% at matched accuracy), despite substantial hidden
sampling cost.
Model family and scale generalization. Results hold across both Qwen-based models (1.5B and 32B
parameters), demonstrating that LYNX scales to larger, more capable reasoning models without retraining.
We further validate generalization to the Llama family (Nemotron-8B) in Appendix D, where LYNX achieves
70–95% token reductions on GSM8K and MATH despite this model’s distinct architectural characteristics
and tendency to use the full 13k token budget (Figure 5).
Temperature robustness and baseline brittleness. A critical advantage of LYNX is deployment at multiple
temperatures using a single probe trained at T = 0.6. Appendix C shows that LYNX maintains strong
performance at T = 0.6 (e.g., 42.9% savings on GSM8K at matched accuracy), whereas entropy-based
methods like DEER exhibit larger accuracy drops at higher temperatures due to increased output uncertainty.
This highlights LYNX’s robustness to decoding configuration shifts, a key practical advantage for deployment
where temperature may vary by use case.
5.2. Qualitative analysis: When and why LYNX exits
Figures 4–8 illustrate LYNX’s decision process across four representative failure modes of reasoning models:
(1) Redundant verification (Fig. 4): The baseline generates 1,105 tokens with extensive re-checking after
reaching the correct answer. LYNX exits at the second cue when the conformal predictive set collapses to
{1}, using only 258 tokens (76.6% reduction) while maintaining correctness.
(2) Repetitive reasoning (Fig. 6, Appendix): After solving a multi-step problem correctly, the baseline
continues with 193 tokens of redundant verification. LYNX detects sufficiency at cue 6 and exits with 22.1%

savings.
(3) Context window exhaustion (Fig. 7, Appendix): On a challenging MATH problem, the baseline enters

6. Conclusion
We introduced LYNX, a cue-triggered, conformal early-exit mechanism that turns reasoning models’ internal
hidden-state awareness into confidence-controlled stopping decisions. For each base model, LYNX trains a
single lightweight probe using self-contained supervision from forced exits and wraps the resulting scores in
split conformal prediction to provide user-tunable guarantees on incorrect early exits. Across three model
families (Qwen and Llama), three scales (1.5B–32B parameters), four mathematical benchmarks, and one
non-mathematical task, a single mathematically-trained probe per model yields strong accuracy–efficiency
tradeoffs: matching or improving baseline accuracy while reducing tokens by 40–95% depending on the
chosen confidence level.
Compared to state-of-the-art methods, LYNX offers competitive or superior Pareto frontiers while requiring
no auxiliary models at inference, no decoding modifications, and providing explicit statistical guarantees.
The method generalizes across temperatures without retraining and transfers zero-shot to non-mathematical
domains, suggesting it captures domain-general signals of reasoning sufficiency rather than task-specific
patterns.
Limitations. Our study focuses on open-source models with explicit thinking segments and self-reflective
cue tokens. Models without such structure may require alternative trigger definitions. Our conformal
guarantees assume exchangeability between calibration and deployment data; under strong domain shift the
effective coverage may deviate from nominal levels. We train separate heads per base model and temperatureindependent probes per family, and do not optimize the cost of forced-exit dataset construction, viewing it
as amortized over downstream usage.
Future work. Immediate directions include applying LYNX to code generation, multi-modal reasoning, and
tool-augmented settings; learning latent decision points beyond hand-specified cues; co-training base models
and probes for improved linear readability; and combining LYNX with external verifiers or self-consistency
methods for additional reliability gains.

7. Appendix.
A. Algorithmic Details of LYNX
In this section we summarize the full LYNX pipeline, including offline cue-level supervision, probe training
and conformal calibration, and the online cue-triggered exit policy, in a single algorithm. Algorithm 1 makes
explicit the roles of the cue set S , probe-training set Dprobe , calibration set Dcal , the probe gψ , the conformal
threshold qδ , and the user-facing confidence level c = 1 − δ, corresponding to the components described in
§3.1–§3.4.

B. Implementation Details
Hardware and compute. All experiments run on 4× NVIDIA RTX A6000 (Blackwell, 96GB) GPUs. Maximum CoT length is set to 13,000 tokens for all models and methods.
Probe architecture. The probe gψ is a two-layer MLP with hidden dimensions [256, 64] and GELU activation,
taking as input concatenated hidden states from three layers (two middle, one final) at each cue token. We
optimize with Adam (lr=1e-3, weight decay=1e-4), class-weighted binary cross-entropy, and early stopping
on a held-out validation split of Dprobe .
Training and calibration data sizes. For DeepSeek-R1-1.5B: 400 training + 200 calibration problems per
Hendrycks MATH category (2,800 train, 1,400 calibration total). For QwQ-32B and Nemotron-8B: 200 train
+ 100 calibration per category to balance compute (1,400 train, 700 calibration total).

C. Results at Temperature T = 0.6
Table 3 reports accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs at T = 0.6 for DeepSeek-1.5B and QwQ-32B. At this higher
temperature, baseline models exhibit increased stochasticity in generation, which affects entropy-based
methods like DEER more severely than LYNX.
Key observations. LYNX maintains strong performance at T = 0.6: on GSM8K with DeepSeek-1.5B,
c = 0.90 achieves near-baseline accuracy (77.86% vs 78.39%, -0.68pp) with 42.9% token savings. On
MATH-500, c = 0.95 preserves accuracy within 2.2pp while reducing tokens by 32.0%. CommonsenseQA
shows 54.6% savings at matched accuracy with c = 0.70.
Critically, DEER and ToN exhibit larger accuracy degradation at T = 0.6 compared to T = 0.0. On GSM8K,
DEER’s accuracy drops by 13.83pp at T = 0.6 vs 8.99pp at T = 0.0. This suggests entropy-based confidence
estimation becomes less reliable under increased sampling stochasticity, whereas LYNX’s probe-based approach
remains robust. The same mathematically-trained probe deployed at both temperatures demonstrates LYNX’s
generalization across decoding configurations without per-temperature retuning.

D. Generalization to Llama-Nemotron
Figure 5 reports results for Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Nano-8B-v1 at T = 0.0. This model presents a distinct
challenge: it consistently uses the full 13k token budget across most problems, suggesting a tendency toward
verbose, potentially circular reasoning patterns. As noted in the DEER paper, Nemotron exhibits lower
confidence in generating intermediate answers, resulting in significantly lower early-stopping rates compared
to Qwen-based models.
LYNX performance. Despite this challenging profile, LYNX achieves dramatic efficiency gains: 91.9–96.6%
token reductions on GSM8K (depending on c), 76.4–92.9% on MATH, and 93.1% on CommonsenseQA. On
GSM8K, c = 0.98 reaches 85.50% accuracy (+31.54pp vs baseline 65.00%) while using only 1,046 tokens, a
91.9% reduction from the 12,965-token baseline. On MATH, c = 0.95 achieves 83.00% accuracy (+0.97pp)
with 85.4% fewer tokens.
A key driver of these accuracy gains, especially on GSM8K and CommonsenseQA, is Nemotron’s difficulty
in producing a final boxed answer within the 13k-token context window. Many baseline rollouts simply
run out of context and are therefore marked incorrect. By contrast, LYNX exits once the calibrated probe
deems a cue safe, cutting off these pathological long trajectories and converting them into short, successful
question–answer runs. This allows LYNX to simultaneously reduce tokens by more than 90% and improve
accuracy by large margins relative to the Nemotron baseline on these benchmarks.
These results suggest that Nemotron’s verbose generation may stem from a form of overthinking or rambling
that LYNX’s early-exit mechanism effectively addresses. By detecting when sufficient reasoning has occurred,
LYNX can curtail unhelpful late-stage verbosity, a potential advantage for models that are prone to such
patterns. This also demonstrates LYNX’s robustness across architectural families: the same probe training
pipeline succeeds on both Qwen and Llama-based models despite their distinct reasoning behaviors.
