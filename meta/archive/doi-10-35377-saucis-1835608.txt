Abstract
This study examines the performance of large language models (LLMs) in Turkish short-answer
assessments within the measurement and evaluation theory framework. The GPT, Gemini, Gemma,
and LLaMA models were evaluated under zero-shot and one-shot conditions with rubric support. The
results show that LLMs have high self-consistency, but decision reliability can vary depending on
prompt format and example sensitivity. Formulating rubrics with clear and concrete performance
indicators increases model-human alignment and assessment fairness. Furthermore, error analyses
revealed that while models often exhibit systematic low-scoring tendencies, they also display an
‘inverted-U’ error pattern, showing higher reliability at score extremes but struggling significantly
with evaluating partial knowledge (intermediate scores). The results indicate that LLMs can support
teachers in formative assessment when properly structured rubrics are used, but ethical oversight and
pedagogical responsibility remain indispensable in final decisions.
Keywords: Artificial intelligence in education, Automated scoring, Evaluation rubric, Large
Language Models (LLM), Validity and fairness

1. Introduction
Assessment and evaluation processes in education play a critical role in determining the effectiveness of learning outcomes and are one of
the fundamental indicators of educational quality [1, 2]. Multiple-choice and matching tests, which are widely used in traditional assessment
methods, offer the advantages of quick, objective scoring. However, they fail to adequately measure students’ higher-order thinking skills
(analysis, synthesis, evaluation) and undermine the assessment’s validity by allowing random choices [3, 4].
To overcome these limitations, assessments structured within the framework of natural language responses (NLR) can provide evidence of
students’ higher-order cognitive skills, such as critical thinking, problem-solving, and creativity [5–7]. Within NLR, short, open-ended responses,
ranging from a few sentences to several paragraphs, allow for in-depth analysis of student thinking [8]. However, the challenges of implementing
such assessments, particularly time-consuming scoring processes [9] and the additional workload they impose on educators [10, 11], limit the
widespread use of short and open-ended questions in educational settings.
In response to this need, the research field of automated short-answer grading (ASAG) has developed, focusing on computational methods for
the automatic evaluation of short and open-ended responses [12]. Pioneering work in the ASAG field has focused on the superficial similarity
between expert reference responses and student responses [13–15], and has been developed through manual feature extraction using machine
learning and large data sets since the 2010s [16].
In recent years, transfer learning-focused ASAG applications, which introduce pre-trained language models and massive datasets and perform
fine-tuning, have stood out for their evaluation performance [10, 12]. High prediction performance has been achieved in ASAG applications
developed by fine-tuning the BERT model, which is based on the transformer architecture [17–20]. Despite the high predictive performance
achieved through transfer learning, the main reason for the limited adoption of ASAG applications in learning environments is the requirement

With the development of various large generative language models (LLMs) such as GPT, LLaMA, Gemini, and Gemma, the evaluation capabilities
of these models across different languages have attracted the attention of researchers. While the fine-tuning processes of transfer learning models
require significant technical expertise [21], generative LLMs offer more accessible alternatives through prompt engineering. These models can
demonstrate effective performance, particularly in zero-shot (without seeing any examples), one-shot (with a single example), or few-shot (with a
small number of examples) learning scenarios, without requiring the large training datasets needed by traditional methods [22, 23]. Although
the use of modern LLMs in ASAG applications appears promising, these models cannot be used directly “out-of-the-box” and require careful
validation [24, 25].
Closed-source models (GPT, Claude, Gemini) with proprietary source code and model weights are limited by data privacy concerns and limited
reproducibility [26, 27]. This increases the importance of open-source alternatives (LLaMA, Gemma, Mistral). The present study also includes
open-source models for comparing ASAG performance.
However, the fact that research in the field is mainly English-focused [21] indicates a significant gap in the literature regarding the ASAG
performance of modern LLMs across different languages. A systematic examination of LLM performance in evaluating short answers across
different languages could make important contributions from both theoretical and practical perspectives. In this context, the research focuses on
evaluating short answers written in Turkish.
The reliable use of automated short-answer assessment systems in educational settings is closely related not only to high accuracy values but
also to the principles of validity, reliability, and fairness, which are fundamental in assessment theory [28–30]. Therefore, examining how
large language models can produce consistent, rubric-aligned, and predictable scoring decisions in different assessment contexts is important.
Furthermore, whether the models’ scores contain systematic errors is critical to determining whether automatic scoring is fair and pedagogically
acceptable.
Within this framework, the study addresses the technical performance of LLMs and the integrity of this performance with respect to assessment
principles, analyzing the relationships among scoring consistency, validity levels, and error tendencies across different models. This study aims
to compare the ASAG performance of modern LLMs (GPT, LLaMA, Gemini, Gemma) in Turkish. The research seeks to answer the following
fundamental questions:
1. How consistent are the results produced by large language models when scoring the same answers?
2. How do large language models perform when scoring short answers using zero-shot and one-shot approaches?
3. How do the error patterns (directionality and stratified dynamics) exhibited by large language models differ across models and score
levels?

2. Literature Review
2.1. LLM-based approaches to automated short answer scoring
Research focusing on the potential of generative modern LLMs (e.g., the GPT series, Claude, LLaMA) in automatic short answer grading
(ASAG) and automatic essay scoring (AES) tasks has gained significant momentum since the 2020s. It has been observed that these models
can achieve remarkable success in zero-shot, one-shot, and few-shot scenarios, particularly with prompt-based evaluation approaches, without
requiring a large number of labeled examples. Work in this area examines various aspects, application domains, and performance of LLMs using
diverse methodologies.
Yoon [31], aiming to overcome the challenges of manual dataset creation, used OpenAI’s GPT-3.5 model with one-shot prompting to extract
justification keys from student responses and combined these keys with Sentence-BERT (SBERT) to develop a system that produces both
analytical and holistic scores. Achieving 67% accuracy and 0.71 QWK on a subset of the ASAP-SAS dataset, this approach demonstrates that an
effective ASAG system can be built with a small amount of manual data. It highlights the potential of LLM prompting to enhance interpretability
and offers a cost-effective solution, particularly when used in a hybrid architecture with traditional text similarity models. Exploring the potential
of more advanced LLMs, Henkel et al. [12] empirically evaluated the ability of GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 Turbo models to rate short-answer reading
comprehension questions on a new English dataset (Ghana Dataset) collected from students in Ghana. The study’s most striking finding is
that GPT-4 performs very close to expert human evaluators (QWK = 0.92 on a 3-class task) with few-shot guidance. This study stands out
from other research in that it provides an important dataset, particularly for low-resource educational environments with limited technical
expertise, and demonstrates the practical potential of generative LLMs for formative assessment in such settings. Similarly, [32] investigated
rubric-based scoring using open-source LLMs, including Llama-2, Mistral, and Zephyr models. Their approach extracts criteria, conditions, and
weightages from marking rubrics and maps student responses accordingly. Testing 200 answer sheets across Python Programming, Introduction
to IT, and Project Management courses, they found that Zephyr-7b achieved the best performance, with an MAE of 0.76 and an RMSE of 1.07,
demonstrating that smaller open-source models can effectively perform rubric-based assessment tasks.
Expanding on model adaptability, [33] demonstrated GPT-4’s ability to transfer grading skills across diverse disciplinary contexts. Most recently,
pushing beyond static prompting, [34] introduced a Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) framework (GradeHITL). By actively leveraging GPT-4o
to query human experts on ambiguous rubric criteria, the study emphasizes that this interactive, LLM-driven optimization can significantly
outperform fully automated methods by aligning the model more closely with human reasoning.

2.2. Hybrid and Retrieval-augmented Approaches
The literature also shows that innovative approaches are being developed to enhance LLM performance further. Wang and Ormerod [35] proposed
a new pipeline that combines Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) with generative language models (GPT, Claude) to improve ASAG
accuracy. This approach, tested on the SemEval-2013 dataset (BEETLE and SciEntsBank), has achieved significant performance improvements
(up to 29.44% in macro F1) over the state of the art, particularly on SciEntsBank tasks. This work contributes to the literature by presenting
a novel RAG-based ASAG method and highlighting the importance of optimizing the information retrieval component and effective prompt
engineering. In addition, [36] developed a modular RAG-based system using the DSPy framework and ColBERT retrieval, achieving 9% higher

2.3. Comparative and domain-specific evaluations
Comparative analyses of different LLMs and understanding their behavior are also important research focuses. Kundu and Barbosa [38]
compared the composition-scoring effectiveness of ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo) and LLaMA (LLaMA-2-70B-chat, LLaMA-3) models on the
ASAP dataset, using human evaluations. They found that LLMs generally scored lower than humans, and that LLaMA-3 showed a higher
correlation with human scores than the others. This study differs from similar studies by analyzing in detail the relationship between scores and
features such as composition length and conjunction usage. It highlights aspects of LLMs that differ from human evaluation processes (e.g.,
more consistent detection of spelling errors). Similarly, Kortemeyer [39] evaluated the “out-of-the-box” performance of a pre-trained GPT-4
model on standard ASAG datasets (Sci- EntsBank, BEETLE) compared to specially trained models. He found that GPT-4’s performance is
similar to that of manually designed models but lower than that of specially trained LLMs, and that a reference answer can affect performance.
This study is important because it highlights the practical applicability and current limitations of general-purpose LLMs, particularly when no
additional training or fine-tuning is performed.
The scoring performance of LLMs can also vary depending on the domain and language to which they are applied. Gao et al. [40] examined
the GPT-4o model’s ability to grade conceptual questions in an undergraduate mechanical engineering course automatically. They found that
GPT-4o strongly correlated with human evaluations when clear grading criteria were provided. However, it struggled with nuanced answers,
and fine-tuning with a few examples did not constantly improve performance in this context. This study highlights LLMs’ potential and
domain-specific challenges in a specific and technical field like engineering. In the domain of computer programming, [41] provided a nuanced
comparison, evaluating Llama 3.2 against GPT-4o. Their results indicated that while open-source models align closely with human grading,
premium models (GPT-4o) achieve statistically higher consistency and equivalence to expert graders, particularly in structured code-based
assessments.
Grevisse [42] investigated the effectiveness of GPT-4 and Gemini 1.0 Pro in undergraduate medical education and multilingual (French, English,
German) environments and highlighted that Gemini 1.0 Pro’s scores were more consistent with human scores and emphasized the importance
of high-quality rubrics. This study is noteworthy as one of the first to examine LLM-based assessment in a specialized field, such as medical
education, and on a multilingual dataset, using Gemini 1.0 Pro. Li and Liu [43] examined the automatic scoring of compositions by Japanese as a
second-language learners using GPT-4 and found that GPT-4 outperformed other models (BERT, Open-Calm). The most significant contribution
of this research is its emphasis on the critical importance of prompt engineering in LLM-based AES, particularly for GPT-4; it demonstrated that
well-designed prompts significantly improve performance.

2.4. Cognitive and pedagogical dimensions of LLM scoring
It is increasingly important to understand LLMs’ final scores, their scoring processes, and how they align with human expectations. Wu et al. [44]
focused on understanding the thought processes of LLMs by examining the analytical rubrics generated by the Mixtral-8x7B-instruct model for
science tasks and their alignment with human rubrics. They found that presenting examples of analytical rubrics and holistic rubrics increased
human alignment with LLM rubrics, but surprisingly, presenting graded student responses led LLMs to take shortcuts, reducing alignment. This
study offers a process-oriented new perspective, emphasizing the importance of understanding the underlying mechanisms of LLM scoring and
developing rubrics aligned with human expectations.
Similarly, Lai et al. [45] introduced SAS-Bench, a Chinese-language dataset and benchmark, to comprehensively analyze the performance of
LLMs on short-answer scoring tasks. This study stands out by going beyond the overall score and introducing new, granular evaluation metrics
such as the Collaborative Consistency Score (CCS) and Error Consistency Score (ECS) to measure LLMs’ reasoning processes, error detection
capabilities, and justification consistency. They also note that guidance with few examples and scoring rubrics improves LLM performance, but
challenges persist in fields such as the natural sciences.
While these studies generally reveal the technical performance of LLMs, it is evident that validity, reliability, and fairness dimensions have not
been systematically examined in an educational context. This study aims to fill this gap.

3. Method
This study aims to examine the performance of large language models in short response evaluations by relating them to measurement-evaluation
theories. In this context, Messick’s [29] integrated validity model, Kane’s [28] interpretive validity arguments, and Williamson et al.’s [30]
automated scoring validity framework provide the theoretical basis for the methodological approach. Model consistency, the effect of rubric
support, and error direction analyses represent these theories’ reliability, construct validity, and consequential validity dimensions. The integration
of rubric-based evaluation criteria in LLM-assisted scoring, as proposed by [32], provides a systematic approach to ensure objectivity and
minimize human bias in the assessment process. Thus, LLMs’ assessment capabilities have been evaluated in terms of technical performance and
their capacity to produce valid, reliable, and fair scoring processes within the epistemological principles underlying measurement and assessment
theories.
Addressing the scarcity of automated scoring research in non-English contexts, the present research specifically focuses on Turkish short-answer
evaluation, employing a quantitative comparative model evaluation approach to examine the performance of large language models (LLMs).
Such an approach is widely used to systematically compare the effectiveness and efficiency of different models on a specific task (ASAG in this
study) [46, 47]. The study examined various versions of four model families: GPT, LLaMA, GEMINI, and GEMMA. Models were selected from
those offering high or unlimited request capabilities per minute, considering the real-time nature of the automatic scoring process. Additionally,
criteria such as accessibility, architectural differences, and parameter diversity were also considered during the selection process. This diversity
enables a comprehensive evaluation of LLM performance on the Turkish ASAG task across language, architecture, and parameter scale.

3.1. Dataset
The dataset used in this study is a Turkish dataset developed by Çınar et al. [1] for an undergraduate physics course and is available on the
Kaggle platform. Two questions with different scoring systems were selected from this dataset in line with the research objectives. The relevant
questions and scoring methods, along with rubric explanations, are provided in Appendix 1. Specifically, item Q2 uses a multi-criteria analytic
rubric that incorporates conditional logic based on specific physics concepts (e.g., mass, height, energy conservation) to distinguish between
partial scores (1-3) and full mastery (4).
The first selected question, Q1, is scored on a binary scale between 0 (incorrect) and 1 (correct), with 254 responses for this question. The
second question, Q2, uses a multiple-scoring system ranging from 0 to 4, and 145 student responses have different scores for this question. The
characteristics of items Q1 and Q2 are presented in Table 1.

3.2. Model performance evaluation process and prompt structure
All models were reported using their official model snapshot aliases/IDs, independent from API transport endpoints. GPT models (GPT-3.5-Turbo
(snapshot: 1106), GPT-4o (snapshot: 2024-08-06), and GPT-4.1 (snapshot: 2025-04-14)) were accessed via the OpenAI Chat Completions API.
LLaMA models (LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instant, LLaMA-3-70B with capacity snapshot 70B-8192, and LLaMA-3.3-70B-Versatile) and Gemma-29B-IT were accessed via the Groq API using an OpenAI-compatible Chat Completions proxy interface. Gemini models (Gemini-2.0-Flash
and Gemini-1.5-Pro) were accessed via Google’s Gemini GenerativeModel API interface, which uses a distinct request product separate from
OpenAI-compatible transport. All scoring experiments were executed sequentially during May–June 2025, and only the numeric score was
parsed and stored for evaluation. For open-weight models, versioning follows the official provider release tags and capacity snapshots passed in
the API request (e.g., 70B-8192), as these models do not expose date-parameterized snapshot aliases.
Model APIs were used in Python via the Google Colab web platform. The overall research framework is illustrated in Figure 1. Each model
was evaluated on two question types (Q1 and Q2) using both zero-shot and one-shot techniques. Zero-shot learning relies solely on the
model’s general understanding without prior examples, while one-shot learning provides a single expert-validated input-output pair to guide the
model [48].
While Figure 1 outlines the general experimental framework, the specific internal mechanism of the scoring engine is detailed in Figure 2. As
shown in Figure 2, this architecture incorporates a three-loop independent inference system with an auto-retry mechanism to handle API rate
limits and invalid format errors.
Student answers were scored using a unified prompt template structured as [System Role] + [Rubric] + [Student Answer], enforcing a “score-only
numeric output” constraint to facilitate parsing. To ensure strictly numeric outputs, the system prompt explicitly instructed the model: “You are a
physics teacher... Return only the numeric score. Do not provide explanations.”
Specifically, the Zero-shot strategy relied solely on the rubric. In contrast, the One-shot strategy injected a single expert-validated reference
example, comprising an answer, a score, and a rationale (e.g., “Score 4 was assigned because...”), into the context to demonstrate the reasoning
logic. For each student response, the model performed three independent, stateless inference runs (test-retest) at a temperature of 0.1 to minimize
hallucinations. The temperature was fixed at 0.1 to minimize stochasticity without relying on a version-dependent random seed. All scoring
experiments were executed sequentially (item-by-item, without request batching) to ensure complete isolation between evaluation sessions.
Furthermore, an auto-retry mechanism (implemented as a 3-attempt loop) was integrated into the API workflow to automatically handle rate
limits and invalid formats (e.g., non-numeric outputs) without data loss, enforcing a controlled pacing delay between requests to prevent API
congestion and ensure stability.
Final scores were derived primarily using majority voting, an ensemble approach widely recognized for enhancing consistency and reliability
across repeated measurements [49]. When consensus was not reached across the three runs, the median score was used as a fallback to filter

3.3. Data analysis
Reliability analysis to evaluate the model’s stability against stochastic variations, and the agreement between the three independent inference
runs was analyzed. Since the specific aim was to measure the model’s robustness over repeated trials rather than agreement between different
human raters, this process was operationalized as a “test-retest stability” check. Fleiss’ Kappa (κ) was utilized as the statistical metric to quantify
this stability, as it robustly accounts for chance agreement. Fleiss’ Kappa was calculated using the formula:

κ=

P̄ − P̄e
1 − P̄e

where P̄ represents the mean of the observed agreement among the independent repeated runs, and P̄e represents the expected agreement by
chance.
The metrics used to evaluate model performance were considered as statistical indicators and tools for generating evidence regarding different
dimensions of validity. The Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) coefficient indicates the extent to which the model shares the same scoring
logic as human raters, reflecting interpretive validity. Accuracy was used as a complementary performance indicator regarding the reliability of
observed scores [28, 30]. This study reported QWK as the primary metric for expert agreement, while accuracy was reported as a complementary
summary indicator. Additionally, the F1 score, precision, and recall values were calculated to support the overall classification performance of
the models.
Finally, a directional error analysis was conducted to examine, in greater depth, the systematic tendencies and potential biases in the models’
scoring behavior. This analysis focused on the direction of differences between model predictions and expert assessments: positive differences
were classified as overestimation, while negative differences were classified as underestimation. Thus, going beyond global metrics such
as RMSE or MAE, which only show the average error magnitude, the direction and frequency of the models’ errors were evaluated [20].
Furthermore, to examine how error dynamics vary across different proficiency levels and answer characteristics (e.g., partial correctness), a
stratified error analysis (calculating MAE for each score level) was conducted. This combined analysis relates to Messick’s [29] consequential
validity and Williamson et al.’s [30] utilization dimensions, because a model’s tendency to consistently assign high or low scores directly affects
the fairness and predictability of scoring.
Using this comprehensive analytical approach, the performance of different model families in the Turkish short-answer assessment was
systematically compared based on statistical reliability and accuracy criteria and the validity principles of measurement and assessment theory.

3.4. Limitations
First, the dataset is limited to two specific questions in undergraduate physics. While this focus enabled a deep psychometric analysis of specific
rubric structures (analytic vs. holistic), the findings regarding validity, fairness, and model behavior may not be directly generalizable to other
domains (e.g., the social sciences) or to question types that require divergent thinking. Second, regarding the prompt engineering strategy, the
“one-shot” examples were specifically selected as pedagogical anchors based on their theoretical completeness (“Scoring Rationale”). However,
we did not conduct a sensitivity analysis using multiple different examples or random selection. Consequently, it is difficult to fully disentangle
the model’s inherent reasoning capabilities from potential biases introduced by the specific linguistic style or structure of the chosen anchor
examples. Third, our error analysis primarily focused on the directionality of scores (lenient vs. harsh) and overall agreement. We did not
systematically quantify the impact of specific answer characteristics, such as response length, linguistic complexity, or conceptual density, on the
models’ scoring errors. Fourth, regarding the reference standard, we utilized the single set of expert scores provided in the published benchmark
dataset as the absolute “Ground Truth”. Since the primary objective of this study was to benchmark LLM alignment (e.g., QWK, Fairness)
against established archival data rather than to re-validate the dataset itself, we did not employ multiple new human raters. Consequently, while

5. Discussion
5.1. Model self-consistency and decision reliability
Research results show that LLMs exhibit a high level of self-consistency in Turkish short-answer evaluations. High Fleiss’ κ coefficients,
obtained from three repeated evaluations, indicate that models can produce similar scoring decisions for the same responses. Grevisse [42]
reported similarly high decision reliability in short-response evaluations across different languages and that model behavior is quite stable in
certain task types. Similarly, Yeung et al. [51], and Khan et al. [52] emphasize that models exhibit higher scoring consistency and reliability in
tasks with low classification complexity. In this regard, the high stability observed in item Q1 can be explained by the task’s low classification
complexity.
However, the decline in consistency levels of some models under one-shot conditions (e.g., Gemini 2.0 Flash) indicates that model behavior is
sensitive to examples. Pecher et al. [53] note that models guided by a single example can increase decision variance by overreacting to, for
example, its linguistic form and explanation style. Yoshida [54] also emphasizes that model performance is critically sensitive to sample selection
and that inappropriate samples can systematically distort model decisions. This result suggests that sample-based prompting can undermine the
model’s test-retest stability and create an excessive link between the form of the example and its cognitive representation. Therefore, while LLMs
generally exhibit reliable scoring tendencies, these tendencies are sensitive to the form of the prompt, the nature of the sample, and contextual
variables.

5.2. Model performance and sample support
The results for the second research question show that example support (one-shot condition) significantly affects model performance. The rubric
was kept constant in both conditions; only in the one-shot scenario was an example student response presented to the model along with the rubric.
This difference directly affected how the model interpreted the rubric’s principles and integrated them into the scoring process. Kortemeyer [38]
notes that exemplified prompts can sometimes create excessive guidance or deviation from context in the model’s interpretation process. Despite
this, the GPT-4.1 model achieved the highest human–model alignment (QWK ≈ 0.95) in both tasks, with the LLaMA 3 and Gemini 2.0 Flash
models demonstrating similar levels of success. Similarly, Meyer et al. [55] report that the GPT-4 family outperforms other models on various
short-answer datasets and achieves lower error rates.
Beyond these performance metrics, the observed discrepancy between lower accuracy (exact match) and higher QWK (weighted agreement)
carries significant measurement-theoretic and pedagogical implications. From a measurement perspective, accuracy treats all errors equally;
misclassifying a high-scoring response (Score 4) as completely incorrect (Score 0) is penalized the same as a minor deviation (Score 3). It is
emphasized that QWK is a much stronger measure in complex situations [56]. Results, the high QWK values indicate that the models’ errors are
predominantly ‘near-misses’ (adjacent categories) rather than random or gross failures. As emphasized by [57], weighted kappa serves as a
critical metric in such contexts because, unlike simple accuracy, it accounts for the severity of the disagreement between the predicted score and
the ground truth. Pedagogically, this suggests that while LLMs may lack the precision for exact grading in borderline cases, they can still capture
the ordinal progression of student proficiency. For formative assessment purposes, this ability to reliably rank student performance and avoid
large magnitude errors is arguably more valuable than perfect exact-match accuracy, as it ensures that feedback directionality remains consistent

with student ability. This sensitivity to error magnitude may be particularly vital in applications where the consequences of misclassification vary
with distance from the true score.
The high QWK values observed in item Q2 indicate that providing detailed rubrics and explanatory examples together strengthens the model’s
cognitive alignment (the degree to which it aligns with teachers’ scoring logic). Jonsson and Svingby [58] emphasize that clear and well-structured
rubrics reduce interpretation differences among evaluators and make scoring decisions more consistent. The results of this study align with this
view and show that rubric clarity brings the model’s decision logic closer to that of human raters. Xavier et al. [59] also note that clear and
well-defined rubrics increase the model’s assessment accuracy more effectively than example demonstrations. These results indicate that rubric
transparency is critical for teachers to confidently utilize artificial intelligence support in formative assessments.
However, the fact that the accuracy values are not as high as QWK indicates that the models show relative consistency but have limited absolute
decision consistency. Kane [28] emphasizes that in the interpretive validity approach, a score should be interpretable in a manner consistent with
the measurement purpose, not only based on statistical proximity. From this perspective, the performance increase observed under one-shot
conditions reflects the model’s capacity to interpret scoring meaningfully within the rubric context, beyond statistical matching.
In morphologically rich languages such as Turkish, differences in model performance may stem not only from the rubric structure but also
from the syntactic complexity of the language. Chamieh et al. [60] note that pre-training data with high linguistic diversity supports model
generalization, but that the capacity for meaning resolution may remain limited in morphologically complex languages. In this context, it is
important to develop prompt strategies (e.g., chain-of-thought or self-consistency) that are more resilient to linguistic variations so that the model
can correctly interpret the rubric principles in Turkish short-answer assessments.

5.3. Structural error transformation and consequential validity
The results of the error-direction analysis revealed systematic tendencies in the models’ scoring behavior. Under zero-shot conditions, most
models (especially GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5 Pro) exhibited a tendency toward underestimation, while some models (e.g., LLaMA 3.1 8B) showed
overestimation. Under one-shot conditions, the general trend reversed, with the rate of overestimation decreasing and that of underestimation
increasing. Chang and Ginter [61] explain this trend by suggesting that example-based guidance makes models more cautious, leading them
to avoid giving high scores. Similarly, Sun et al. [62] note that language models can develop systematic underestimation tendencies when
generalizing from examples.
These results are closely related to Messick’s [29] component of consequential validity. Messick considers the impact of a measurement tool’s
results on educational decisions to be an integral part of the validity argument. The tendency toward systematic under-scoring may raise concerns
about fairness and predictability for students. Williamson et al. [30] also emphasize that pedagogical reliability in the utilization dimension of
model-based grading systems can be evaluated not only by technical accuracy but also by the impact of the results on the learner. In this context,
the model’s tendency to consistently assign high or low scores is a critical warning sign for ethical evaluability and validity.
A comparative analysis between zero-shot and one-shot conditions reveals that providing a single example induces a structural transformation
in model behavior, acting as a “calibration anchor” rather than merely improving linear accuracy. In the multi-class task (Q2), the zero-shot
condition exhibited a chaotic profile characterized by high and irregular error variances, particularly at the score extremes. For instance, the
dramatic error recorded by GPT-3.5 on full-credit responses suggests that, without a contextual anchor, the model struggles to recognize the

(fully correct and fully incorrect). Yet paradoxically, this stabilization made the models’ deficiency in evaluating intermediate proficiency levels
more pronounced, resulting in a distinct “inverted-U” error pattern, with errors peaking at the partial credit level (Score 2).
From a pedagogical perspective, this failure to distinguish intermediate proficiency levels poses a risk to formative assessment, as the “grey
area” of partial understanding is often where constructive feedback is most needed for student growth. This observation aligns with the
“knowledge-understanding gap” described by [62], which corresponds precisely to this domain of partial correctness. [37], emphasize partially
matched responses as the points where students’ misconceptions and knowledge gaps are most concentrated. Thus, while one-shot prompting
improves broad classification, the reliability of LLMs diminishes exactly at the pedagogical margins where human nuance is most essential.
A similar tension was observed in the binary task (Q1). At the same time, the reference example helped reduce errors for some models by
clarifying semantic variations; however, some models, like GPT-4o, retained a “strictness bias,” rejecting valid, correct answers. This indicates
that while few-shot guidance can calibrate scoring severity, it does not fully resolve the fairness concern regarding the validation of diverse
student reasoning.

5.4. Pedagogical and practical implications
The results indicate that LLMs can be integrated into educational settings as auxiliary graders to assist with assessment processes. Still, they also
highlight the absolute necessity of human oversight. Moreover, the identification of the ‘inverted-U’ error pattern necessitates a redefinition of
the human-in-the-loop workflow. Rather than a random audit, teachers should adopt a ‘targeted intervention’ strategy, specifically focusing their
verification efforts on responses graded as ‘partial credit’ (e.g., Score 2), where model reliability is lowest.
Williamson et al. [30] emphasize that pedagogical responsibility principles in the utilization dimension are decisive for the safe use of automated
grading systems. Accordingly, the use of LLMs in education should be evaluated not only based on accuracy but also on the principles of
transparency and explainability.
Formulating rubrics with clear and concrete performance indicators will increase the predictability of scoring decisions for both teachers and
models. Furthermore, as noted by Chamieh et al. [60], testing linguistically consistent prompt strategies (e.g., chain-of-thought, self-consistency)
is recommended to ensure rubric–model alignment in linguistically rich languages.
In conclusion, when supported by properly structured rubrics, LLMs can serve as tools that save time in education, reduce teacher workload,
and speed up feedback processes. However, ethical oversight, human judgment, and pedagogical responsibility are indispensable in the final
evaluation process. Beyond the technical evaluation of model accuracy, this study contributes to the pedagogical discourse on fairness and
validity in AI-supported educational assessment.

6. Conclusion
This study examined the performance of LLMs in Turkish short-answer assessments based on both technical criteria and assessment theory. The
results reveal that the models generally exhibit high internal consistency, but this consistency is sensitive to contextual variables such as prompt
format and example quality [42, 51].
The GPT-4.1 model achieved the highest human–model alignment in both tasks and provided the most stable results in terms of interpretability,
with a QWK ≈ 0.95 value. The LLaMA 3 and Gemini 2.0 Flash models showed similar performance. Jonsson and Svingby [58] noted that
rubric clarity increases evaluator consistency; similarly, this study shows that detailed rubrics and example support bring the model’s scoring
decisions closer to the logic of human raters.
However, the relatively low accuracy values indicate that, although the models can follow rubric principles at the conceptual level, they struggle
to apply them consistently. Kane [28] explains this situation within the scope of interpretive validity as insufficient statistical similarity; for
model scores to be meaningful, they depend not only on agreement rates but also on the suitability of the reasons underlying the scoring to the
measurement purpose.
Error direction analysis, consistent with the results of Chang and Ginter [61], revealed that one-shot scenarios made models more conservative
and reduced the tendency for over-scoring. However, the increase in under-scoring rates in some models indicates that the systematic tendency
for low scoring should be carefully evaluated in terms of outcome validity. As Messick [29] emphasized, the impact of measurement results on
pedagogical decisions is an integral part of the validity discussion; therefore, the model’s ability to produce fair and predictable decisions is as
important as technical accuracy.
Crucially, the stratified error analysis refines our understanding of model reliability beyond general accuracy metrics. It reveals that while
one-shot prompting serves as a necessary ‘calibration anchor’ to stabilize scoring at the extremes (fully correct/fully incorrect), it paradoxically
exposes a structural deficiency in evaluating intermediate proficiency levels, resulting in an “inverted-U” error pattern. This implies that LLMs,
in their current state, are adept at binary classification of mastery but struggle with the pedagogical nuance required to assess partial knowledge.
Furthermore, the ‘strictness bias’ observed in some models, where valid reasoning is rejected, highlights that ‘fairness’ in AI scoring is not just
about avoiding bias against demographics, but also about preventing the systematic penalization of divergent but correct student thinking.
Overall, this study demonstrates that LLM-based scoring is not merely a technical achievement but a multi-layered evaluation process that
requires the simultaneous consideration of validity, reliability, and fairness dimensions.

Question Text
Sinan are preparing the report of the experiment they did in the physics laboratory
class that day. The question in the test report is:
“If there is a single bulb in an electrical circuit, in which case two bulbs of the same
power are connected in series, compare the brightness of the bulbs and explain the
reason for your answer.”
Sinan rethinks their experiments in the laboratory and answers the question as
follows:
“While there is only one light bulb in an electrical circuit, the light bulb is quite
bright. When a second bulb is added in series to this bulb, both bulbs light up
equally, but less brightly. The reason is that the single bulb draws all the current
flowing through the circuit. When there are two bulbs in the circuit, the bulbs
share the current, and therefore they are equal but less bright.”
Sinan thinks he understands very well what is happening in the experiment, but he
makes a big mistake. Can you find this error?
You drop an apple from a certain height without speed, and it hits the ground with
a certain velocity. Assume that you cut the same apple in half and drop half of
it at the same height. (Assume no air friction, please express your answers only
verbally without writing formulas)
a) What is the speed of the cut apple in half according to the speed of the whole
apple?
b) Explain the reason by considering the factors that affect its speed for the answer
to option a.
c) Explain the reason, taking into account the energy conservation laws, for your
option answer a.

Scoring Key
0 point: If the student did not answer or gave an incorrect answer.
1 point: Sinan thinks that the current passes through both bulbs, and the bulbs
share this current. However, the same current passes first through the first bulb
and then through the other. The two bulbs are dimmer than a single bulb. Because
the increased resistance in the circuit reduces the current. Sinan considers that the
bulbs are connected in parallel.

a) 0 point: If the student did not answer or gave an incorrect answer (in this case,
the answers of b and c are ignored).
1 point: Speed does not change. / Speeds are the same.
b) 0 point: If the student did not answer or gave an incorrect answer.
2 points (if option a is correct): only if height is specified in option b, 2 points (if
option a is correct): only if mass is specified in option b,
3 points (if option a is correct): In option b, if both height and mass are specified:
The objects that are allowed to fall free from the same height have different masses,
but their velocities are the same. The velocity of an object that makes free-fall
movement is related to height, but it is independent of mass.
c) 0 point: If the student did not answer or gave an incorrect answer.
4 points (if options a and b are correct): According to energy conservation laws, if
an object has only potential energy when it is at a certain height and the object is
allowed to fall freely from the height, the height decreases as it falls to the ground
as soon as it is allowed to fall, but the object starts to accelerate. In this case,
the kinetic energy increases while the potential energy decreases. As soon as the
object hits the ground, its kinetic energy increases, and the total mechanical energy
is preserved.
