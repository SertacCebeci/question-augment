A BSTRACT
Rubric-based Reinforcement Learning (RL) has recently shown promise in improving Large Language Models (LLMs) on open-ended tasks. A widely recognized
limitation of rubric-based RL is limited exploration: criteria that no rollout manages
to satisfy (Unexplored Criteria) receive no optimization signal. Recent methods
address this by incorporating rubric information as external guidance during rollout
generation, yet they introduce a train-inference mismatch: the policy is optimized
on rollouts produced under external guidance while this guidance is absent at
inference time, causing error accumulation through autoregressive decoding. Moreover, these exploration-focused approaches overlook a fundamentally different
failure mode that we term Suppressed Criteria—criteria that are satisfied by some
rollouts yet whose learning signals might be lost during optimization because
scalar reward aggregation assigns them non-positive aggregate advantages. Our
analysis reveals that suppressed criteria constitute a persistent and non-negligible
failure mode—over 25% of samples exhibit this issue throughout training. To simultaneously address both unexplored and suppressed criteria without introducing
training-inference mismatch, we propose Criterion-Distilled Policy Optimization
(CriPO), which enhances rubric-based RL via on-policy self-distillation. For
unexplored criteria, CriPO constructs a behavior-injection teacher and computes a
filtered forward-KL loss to inject missing behaviors into the policy. For suppressed
criteria, CriPO uses a counterfactual teacher to locate criterion-relevant tokens in
negative-advantage rollouts, and corrects their advantages in GRPO to preserve
useful patterns. Experiments on medicine and science benchmarks demonstrate that
CriPO outperforms existing rubric-based RL methods, e.g., achieving an average
gain of 3.3 points over GRPO on Qwen3-4B.

I NTRODUCTION

In recent years, Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), instantiated by methods
such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), has emerged as a prominent paradigm for improving the reasoning
capabilities of large language models (DeepSeek-AI, 2025; Cui et al., 2026). RLVR has achieved
notable success in verifiable domains such as mathematical reasoning and code generation, where
exact answers or executable test cases provide stable and reliable supervision (He et al., 2024;
Wei et al., 2025). However, in open-ended scenarios such as medical consultation and long-form
writing, model outputs typically do not admit a unique gold answer and are difficult to verify with
deterministic rules. To extend RLVR to such domains, recent work has explored rubric-based rewards,
which decompose response quality into interpretable evaluation criteria, use LLM-as-a-Judge (Gu
et al., 2024a) to assign multi-dimensional scores, and aggregate them into a scalar reward for RL
training (Gunjal et al., 2025; Viswanathan et al., 2025; Dineen et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026a).

While rubric-based RL has shown promising results in these open-ended domains, it still faces a
fundamental challenge of limited exploration: since GRPO only optimizes over behaviors present in
the sampled rollout group (Yue et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2025), criteria that no rollout manages
to satisfy, termed Unexplored Criteria, receive no effective optimization signal. Recent methods
address this issue by using rubrics as external guidance during rollout generation. For example,
RuscaRL (Zhou et al., 2025) uses rubric criteria as explicit scaffolding to elicit more diverse and
higher-quality rollouts, while HeRL (Zhang et al., 2026b) leverages failed trajectories and their
unmet criteria as hindsight guidance for generating revised rollouts. However, these explorationenhancement methods still have two important drawbacks. First, they introduce a training-inference
mismatch: during training, rollouts are generated with privileged information, so each token is
optimized under externally guided prefixes; at inference time, however, the model must generate
solely from its own prefixes. This discrepancy constitutes exposure bias (Agarwal et al., 2024;
Song & Zheng, 2026; Li et al., 2026b), where early generation error that could be avoided during
guided training may propagate through subsequent autoregressive decoding. Also, these mixed-policy
optimization pipelines further lead to longer training times; see Section C.2 for more details.
Second, these methods overlook another fundamentally different failure mode beyond unexplored criteria: Suppressed Criteria—already explored criteria whose learning signals might be systematically
lost during GRPO optimization. This occurs because GRPO aggregates criterion-wise scores into a
single scalar reward and broadcasts it uniformly to the entire response. Consequently, rollouts that
satisfy certain criteria but underperform on others receive negative or negligible aggregate advantages,
causing useful criterion-satisfying behaviors to be penalized or ignored rather than reinforced. For
example, in Figure 1 (left), y2 and y3 satisfy criterion C3, but their advantage sum is negative (-0.46).
Our statistical analysis in Figure 1 (right) reveals that suppressed criteria remain a non-negligible
issue in practice: when training Qwen3-4B on RaR-Medicine, over 25% of samples contain suppressed criteria throughout training, with more than 1.2 suppressed criteria on average among samples
containing SC. While some prior works (Chen et al., 2026; Lan, 2026; Xu et al., 2026) also recognize
that scalar reward aggregation can obscure individual criterion contributions, they do not provide a
systematic analysis of when and why criterion-level signals are lost, and they also fail to address the
complementary challenge of unexplored criteria.
To address both unexplored and suppressed criteria without introducing training-inference mismatch,
in this paper, we propose Criterion-Distilled Policy Optimization (CriPO), which enhances rubricbased RL via On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026; Ye
et al., 2026). Unlike the approaches that inject knowledge or behaviors by producing rollouts
under privileged information, OPSD constructs a privilege-conditioned self-teacher and provides
token-level supervision on the model’s own on-policy rollouts, thereby obtaining learning signals
without a distributional gap between training and inference. Specifically, CriPO adopts GRPO as the
stable, reward-grounded optimization backbone and uses the following two OPSD-derived signals
for targeted criterion-level correction: for unexplored criteria, CriPO constructs a behavior-injection
self-teacher and computes forward-KL loss with contribution-based token filtering as an auxiliary
loss to inject missing criterion-specific behaviors into the policy; for suppressed criteria, CriPO
constructs a counterfactual self-teacher to locate suppressed-criterion-relevant tokens in negative2

advantage rollouts, and flip or mask their GRPO advantages to preserve useful patterns. Experiments
on five medicine and science benchmarks demonstrate that CriPO outperforms five baselines in both
in-domain and cross-domain settings (e.g., achieving an average gain of 3.3 points over GRPO on
Qwen3-4B), and further analysis shows that CriPO effectively reduces the occurrence rates of both
unexplored and suppressed criteria. Note that CriPO requires at most one additional forward pass
per rollout, resulting in about a 7% increase on average in training time compared with GRPO, and
CriPO also improves optimization efficiency, reaching GRPO’s converged performance in no more
than half the optimization steps and less than half the wall-clock training time.

Failure in Rubric-based RL: Unexplored and Suppressed Criteria. Although rubric-based
rewards provide criterion-level supervision, two inherent limitations of GRPO prevent this supervision
from being fully exploited: (i) limited exploration: GRPO only optimizes over behaviors present in
the sampled rollouts, so that criteria with no rollout managed to satisfy receive no optimization signal;
and (ii) reward ambiguity: aggregating multiple criteria into a single scalar reward obscures criterionspecific token contributions and may penalize useful behaviors. We show that these limitations lead
to two types of failure criteria:

On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026)
has recently emerged as a promising paradigm for converting privileged information into dense
token-level supervision while avoiding the training-inference distribution mismatch (Agarwal et al.,
2024; Gu et al., 2024b). Given an on-policy rollout y ∼ πθ (· | x), OPSD compares two nexttoken distributions produced by the same model: a student distribution conditioned only on the
original context, and a self-teacher distribution conditioned additionally on privileged information ξ.
Specifically, the policy is trained to match the privilege-conditioned prediction through a token-level
divergence at each token position t (reverse-KL instantiation):

In rubric-based RL, rubric criteria can naturally serve as the privileged information ξ for OPSD. By
conditioning the self-teacher on rubrics, OPSD can expose criterion-level supervision, making it
well suited to address the two limitations discussed above. However, directly relying on OPSD as
the primary optimization objective may introduce unreliable token-level supervision due to issues
such as privileged-information leakage or entropy collapse (Yang et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026b;
Zhu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Kim et al., 2026a). To this end, we retain GRPO as the stable,
reward-grounded optimization backbone and use OPSD-derived signals only for targeted
criterion-level correction, which follows the emerging paradigm of combining OPSD with RLVR
rather than relying on standalone OPSD (Li et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026a; Wang
et al., 2026a; Liu et al., 2026b).

4.1

E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP

Datasets and Evaluation. We evaluate CriPO on both medicine and science QA tasks, where
RaR-Medicine and RaR-Science (Gunjal et al., 2025) are adopted as the training datasets for medicine
and science tasks, respectively. We use their own test split for in-domain evaluation, and for crossdomain evaluation, we use HealthBench (Arora et al., 2025) and LLMEval-Med (Zhang et al.,
2025) for medicine tasks, and ResearchQA (Yifei et al., 2025) for science tasks. We use Qwen332B (Team, 2025) as the rubric judge during training and GPT-4o-mini (OpenAI, 2023) as the judge
for evaluation. For every evaluated response, the evaluation score is computed using the scalar
rubric-reward aggregation defined in Section 2. More details about the datasets and evaluation
settings are provided in Appendix B.
Baselines and Models. Our main experiments are conducted on Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B, and
the baselines include: SFT, which fine-tunes the model to fit synthesized targets generated by rubric
guidance; OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026), an on-policy self-distillation baseline that distills the full rubric
into the model; GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), the standard group-relative policy optimization baseline;
RuscaRL (Zhou et al., 2025), a rubric-based RL method that uses rubric scaffolding during rollout
generation and policy optimization; and HeRL (Zhang et al., 2026b), a rubric-based RL method that
leverages hindsight feedback from failed trajectories and unmet rubrics to enhance exploration. All
methods are trained on the same data and evaluated under the same protocol for fair comparison.
More implementation details of baselines and CriPO are provided in Appendix B.

M AIN R ESULTS

CriPO outperforms existing baselines. As shown in Table 1, SFT underperforms the base model
on most benchmarks, suggesting that fitting rubric-guided targets does not generalize reliably. Standalone OPSD is also unsatisfactory without a stable reward-grounded backbone. GRPO consistently
improves the base model, yielding average gains of roughly 4–5 points across the two model scales,
confirming the benefit of reward-grounded optimization. RuscaRL and HeRL further strengthen
GRPO through rubric-guided exploration. Built on GRPO, CriPO raises the average score from 61.2
to 64.1 on Qwen3-1.7B (+2.9) and from 69.1 to 72.4 on Qwen3-4B (+3.3). It also achieves the
best overall result, outperforming the strongest baseline by 1.1 on Qwen3-1.7B and 1.2 on Qwen3-4B.
These results demonstrate the effectiveness of our on-policy framework that simultaneously addresses
the two failure modes in standard rubric-based RL.
Efficiency and Convergence Analysis. Figure 4 compares the optimization trajectories of CriPO
and GRPO over 400 training steps. GRPO improves rapidly in the early stage, but its subsequent gains
become slower and less consistent, whereas CriPO exhibits more sustained improvement. Across
all model-domain settings, CriPO reaches or exceeds the best score attained by GRPO within the
first 150 to 200 steps and continues toward a substantially higher final score, demonstrating more
efficient use of training samples and policy updates. In terms of the training time, CriPO requires at
most one additional forward pass per rollout, resulting in about a 7% increase on average in training
time compared with GRPO. Detailed comparisons of wall-clock training time as well as peak GPU
memory are provided in Appendix C.2.

Training Dynamics. To examine how the two modules in CriPO affect optimization, Figure 5
compares GRPO, CriPO-U (behavior injection only), and CriPO over 400 steps of training Qwen3-4B
on RaR-Medicine and RaR-Science. As shown in the left column, CriPO-U consistently follows a
higher reward trajectory than GRPO, while adding advantage correction generally yields the highest
reward, demonstrating the complementary benefits of the two modules. The middle column shows
that GRPO undergoes rapid entropy collapse, whereas behavior injection substantially slows this
decline and thereby preserves exploration. The full CriPO maintains even higher entropy through
most of training, suggesting that advantage correction further protects diverse criterion-relevant
patterns from negative updates. As the base OPSD coefficient β is linearly decayed toward zero in the
final training stage, the entropy of both CriPO variants also decreases, allowing the policies to settle
into more stable output distributions. The right column further shows that CriPO produces longer
responses than GRPO in both domains. Behavior injection encourages the model to cover missing
criterion-specific behaviors, while advantage correction preserves content associated with suppressed
criteria; the latter produces a particularly clear additional length increase on RaR-Medicine. Together
with the reward gains, these trends suggest that the increased length reflects richer rubric-relevant
content rather than mere verbosity. Appendix D.1 provides an illustrative example in which CriPO
gives more complete clinical reasoning and satisfies more rubric criteria than GRPO.

C ONCLUSION

In this work, we study rubric-based RL for improving LLMs on open-ended tasks and identify two
prevalent criterion-level failure modes: unexplored criteria, where no rollout satisfies a criterion, and
suppressed criteria, where criterion-satisfying behaviors are penalized or ignored due to non-positive
9

Preprint

aggregate advantages. To address both issues, we propose Criterion-Distilled Policy Optimization
(CriPO), an on-policy framework that retains GRPO as a reward-grounded backbone while using
OPSD for targeted criterion-level correction. Specifically, CriPO injects unexplored behavior through
a localized forward-KL distillation loss and preserves useful suppressed behaviors by locating
relevant tokens and correcting their advantages. Experiments on medicine and science benchmarks
demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of CriPO against existing rubric-based RL methods. We
hope our work can inspire the community to further investigate how to jointly address both limited
exploration and reward ambiguity in standard rubric-based RL.

R ELATED W ORK

Rubric-based RL. Rubric-based RL extends RLVR to open-ended tasks whose quality cannot
be verified by exact answers or executable tests. By decomposing response quality into explicit
dimensions such as factuality, completeness, safety, evidence grounding, and task utility, they provide
more interpretable and controllable supervision than holistic judgments (Arora et al., 2025; Du et al.,
2025). Existing rubric-based RL methods typically use an LLM judge to score individual criteria
and aggregate these scores into a scalar reward for GRPO (Gunjal et al., 2025; Viswanathan et al.,
2025; Wu et al., 2025; Dineen et al., 2025). Subsequent work improves this paradigm through better
reward aggregation, criterion reweighting, and fine-grained credit assignment, making rubric signal
more discriminative and robust (Chen et al., 2026; Lan, 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a).
A parallel line of work uses rubric feedback to enhance exploration. RuscaRL (Zhou et al., 2025)
conditions rollout generation on rubric guidance, while HeRL (Zhang et al., 2026b) uses hindsight
feedback from failed trajectories to construct revised rollouts. Although these methods help discover
behaviors associated with previously unmet criteria, they rely on privileged information during rollout
generation, introducing a training-inference mismatch, and also overlook another crucial failure of
suppressed criteria. In contrast, CriPO recovers criterion-level supervision for both failure modes
through an on-policy framework, without relying on externally guided rollouts.
On-Policy Self-Distillation. On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) uses the current policy under
privileged or feedback-augmented contexts as a self-teacher, converting auxiliary information into
dense token-level supervision while avoiding off-policy teacher mismatch (Agarwal et al., 2024; Zhao
et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026). Despite its promise, dense OPSD is often unstable due to noisy
token-level gradients, teacher–student inconsistency, and privileged-information leakage (Yang et al.,
2026; Kim et al., 2026b; Zhu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Kim et al., 2026a). Recent work therefore
increasingly combines OPSD with RLVR, using reference solutions, verified traces, or environment
feedback to improve sparse-reward optimization (Yang et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2026).
Most of these studies focus on verifiable domains such as mathematical reasoning, where privileged
supervision is relatively well aligned with the objective.
Recent works have also begun exploring OPSD for rubric-based post-training in open-ended
tasks (Rezaei et al., 2026; Gu et al., 2026), while they treat self-distillation as the primary optimization objective rather than retaining a reward-grounded RL backbone. Concurrent work (Wang
et al., 2026b) combines OPSD and GRPO sequentially, using OPSD before RL to obtain a more
knowledgeable initialization. In contrast, CriPO integrates self-distillation directly into GRPO
training and uses it only for targeted criterion-level correction.

More Implementation Details for CriPO. To keep advantage correction targeted, we use two
cutoffs. Let κ = 2048 be the maximum response length eligible for advantage correction and ζ = 10
the maximum number of key tokens selected per rollout. If |yi | > κ, we leave its token advantages
unchanged. Otherwise, among the tokens satisfying the teacher-probability threshold in Eq.(7), we
retain at most the ζ tokens with the lowest teacher-assigned probabilities. Advantage correction can
increase response length by preserving or reinforcing selected tokens; correcting too many tokens
could amplify this effect and destabilize training. Longer responses also make criterion-specific token
localization less reliable, motivating the response-length cutoff. Together, these cutoffs constrain the
scope of advantage correction while retaining its most salient token-level signals.
We apply further safeguards to the behavior-injection self-teacher. We retain at most the three
highest-weighted unexplored criteria when constructing the teacher prompt. We also clamp each
token’s KL value to a maximum of 10 before contribution-based filtering and gradient updates to limit
outliers. Finally, we observe larger KL values near the beginning and end of responses (Figure 7).
Early tokens may reflect the teacher instruction, such as a preamble before revising the response. At
the end, the teacher is conditioned on a suboptimal student prefix and may favor post-hoc corrections
rather than reliable continuation. We therefore mask the first and last 1% of response tokens before
contribution-based filtering. Overall, we use the same settings for the above parameters and
γ, α, τflip , β, κ, and ζ across all model and dataset settings, without per-setting tuning; see
Appendix C.9 for a detailed hyperparameter analysis.

C.2

C OMPUTATION C OST A NALYSIS

Comparisons on Training Time. Figure 9 compares the wall-clock training time and maximum
GPU memory of CriPO with GRPO and rubric-guided baselines. With at most one additional forward
pass per rollout, the total training time of CriPO remains close to that of GRPO: Averaged over the
four settings, CriPO requires 18.6 hours, only about 7% more than GRPO (17.4 hours), while being
substantially faster than RuscaRL (27.5 hours) and HeRL (29.7 hours). The runtime differences arise
from the distinct training pipelines: RuscaRL assigns a large weight to rubric-guided generation
during the early training stage, which substantially increases both rollout entropy and response length.
Because autoregressive generation cost grows with sequence length, these longer rollouts lead to
higher wall-clock time. HeRL incurs a different source of overhead: each sample passes through
two rollout stages and requires two calls to the LLM judge. Switching between rollout modes and
performing the additional judge evaluation both add substantial latency. In contrast, CriPO reuses the
original on-policy rollout and primarily adds one extra actor forward pass to obtain the self-teacher
signal, explaining why its total runtime remains much closer to GRPO.
More importantly, the faster convergence of CriPO offsets its additional per-step cost. As indicated
by the hatched regions in Figure 9 (left), CriPO reaches GRPO’s final performance in only 5.6, 4.9,
4.3, and 11.0 hours across the four settings. These values correspond to a 2.2×–3.6× wall-clock
speedup over the time required to fully train GRPO. CriPO is also the fastest non-GRPO method to
reach the GRPO reference performance in three of the four settings; the exception is Qwen3-1.7B
on RaR-Medicine, where HeRL reaches it in 2.5 hours. The above computation cost comparison
shows that CriPO provides a favorable time-to-quality tradeoff rather than merely improving final
performance through a larger computation budget.

Comparisons on GPU Memory. Figure 9 (right) shows that this efficiency comes with a moderate
memory overhead. The maximum per-GPU memory of CriPO is 26.4, 36.5, 28.0, and 37.3 GB,
compared with 22.3, 25.7, 23.2, and 33.0 GB for GRPO. This increase mainly arises from the top-k
vocabulary approximation used to compute forward KL, which requires retaining probabilities or
logits for the selected vocabulary entries during the actor forward pass. Nevertheless, peak memory
remains below 40 GB in every setting and is generally comparable to RuscaRL and HeRL. Overall,
CriPO introduces manageable memory overhead while delivering substantially faster convergence
and stronger final performance.

R ESULTS USING D IFFERENT LLM J UDGES

The main results in Table 1 use GPT-4o-mini as the evaluation judge. To examine whether the
conclusions depend on this evaluator, we further evaluate the same set of trained models using
Qwen3-32B. As shown in Table 6, the results lead to the same overall conclusions as those obtained
with GPT-4o-mini: SFT and standalone OPSD are less reliable, reward-grounded RL improves the
base models, and CriPO achieves the strongest average performance at both model scales.
Specifically, SFT and standalone OPSD underperform the corresponding base models on average.
GRPO raises the average score from 41.1 to 45.3 on Qwen3-1.7B (+4.2) and from 53.4 to 60.0 on
Qwen3-4B (+6.6), confirming the benefit of reward-grounded optimization. RuscaRL and HeRL
provide further improvements through rubric-guided exploration. Built on GRPO, CriPO further
increases the average score to 48.3 on Qwen3-1.7B (+3.0 over GRPO) and 63.6 on Qwen3-4B
(+3.6). It also outperforms the strongest competing baseline by 1.3 and 1.1 points, respectively. The
consistent advantage of CriPO under both GPT-4o-mini and Qwen3-32B indicates that the benefits of
targeted criterion-level correction are robust to the choice of evaluation judge.

retains 79.3 on IFEval and improves IFBench to 27.3, outperforming DAPO by 0.9 and 3.0 points,
respectively. We conjecture that the improved generalization comes from the fine-grained nature
of CriPO: its corrections are concentrated on localized, criterion-relevant tokens instead of broadly
altering the entire response distribution. These results highlight the benefit of token-level credit
assignment and show that CriPO can improve both task performance and generalization when adapted
to a more exploration-oriented RL objective.

Hyperparameter Sensitivity. Table 13 shows that the behavior-injection hyperparameters control
a clear trade-off between evaluation performance and training dynamics. Setting γ = 1.0, which
removes contribution-based token filtering, lowers performance on both benchmarks. Increasing the
strength for CriPO w/o selection with a larger β of 0.1 does not improve its performance, indicating
the effectiveness of contribution-based token filtering does not simply stem from increasing the
magnitude of the loss. Reducing γ concentrates supervision on fewer high-contribution tokens,
but using γ = 0.9 or 0.5 with a comparatively large distillation coefficient produces substantially
higher entropy and longer responses and causes CUDA OOM during training. Reducing the behavior
injection loss coefficient β stabilizes these settings: γ = 0.9, β = 0.025 approaches the default
performance with similar entropy, whereas the more aggressive γ = 0.5, β = 0.005 remains weaker.
The β sweep exhibits the same pattern. A small value of 0.01 provides insufficient improvement,
while β = 0.1 achieves higher evaluation scores at the cost of a large increase in entropy and response
length. The default γ = 0.95 and β = 0.05 therefore provide a more balanced operating point.
The advantage-correction hyperparameters are less sensitive around the default setting. Increasing

L IMITATIONS

Despite its gains, CriPO inherits several constraints of rubric-based RL. Its criterion-level signals
depend on the quality and coverage of the rubrics and their judgments: missing, ambiguous, or
incorrectly scored criteria can distort the identification of unexplored and suppressed criteria, and
consequently misdirect behavior injection or advantage correction. Our experiments use curated
rubrics in medicine and science, so the robustness of CriPO to lower-quality rubric settings remains to
be established. Furthermore, the rubrics are fixed throughout training, although the policy’s strengths
and failure modes change. Criteria that were informative early on may become less useful, while
newly emerging weaknesses may remain uncovered. We leave the study of CriPO under low-quality
rubrics and the development of adaptive rubrics that evolve with the policy to provide more targeted
supervision to future works.
