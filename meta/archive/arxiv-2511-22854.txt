ABSTRACT
When people reason about cause and effect, they often consider
many competing “what if” scenarios before deciding which explanation fits best. Analogously, advanced language models capable
of causal inference can consider multiple interventions and counterfactuals to judge the validity of causal claims. Crucially, this
type of reasoning is less like a single calculation and more like
an internal dialogue between alternative hypotheses. In this paper, we make this dialogue explicit through a dual-agent debate
framework where one model provides a structured causal inference, and the other critically examines this reasoning for logical
flaws. When disagreements arise, the agents attempt to persuade
each other, challenging each other’s logic and revising their conclusions until they converge on a mutually agreed answer. To take
advantage of this deliberative process, we specifically use reasoning language models, whose strengths in both causal inference and
adversarial debate remain under-explored relative to standard large
language models, which do not employ multi-step logical inference to the same degree. We evaluate our approach on the CLadder
dataset, a benchmark linking natural language questions to formally defined causal graphs across all three rungs of Pearl’s ladder
of causation. With Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1 as debater agents,
we demonstrate that multi-agent debate improves DeepSeek-R1’s
overall accuracy in causal inference from 78.03% to 87.45%, with
the counterfactual category specifically improving from 67.94%
to 80.04% accuracy. Similarly, Qwen3’s overall accuracy improves
from 84.16% to 89.41%, and counterfactual questions from 71.53% to
80.35%, showing that even stronger models can still benefit greatly
from debate with weaker agents. Our results highlight the potential
of reasoning models as building blocks for multi-agent systems
in causal inference, and demonstrate the importance of diverse
perspectives in causal problem-solving. Our code is available at
https://github.com/finnvamosi/CRAwDAD.

INTRODUCTION

Causal reasoning is one of Homo sapiens’ greatest evolutionary
advantages, enabling humans to think rationally about how our
actions and the actions of others affect the world around us [13, 36].
From analyzing failed prehistoric hunts to designing scientific experiments, humans naturally ask “What if things had been different?”, and imagine counterfactual scenarios according to their

causal model of the scenario. Often, this reasoning occurs collectively through dialogue, as people challenge assumptions and debate alternative explanations. This capacity for counterfactual thinking has been formalized in statistics and artificial intelligence as
"causal inference", which studies how interventions and hypothetical changes influence observed outcomes [33, 34]. Because this
process depends on manipulating explicit causal models [32] rather
than linguistic correlations, what is intuitive for humans remains
challenging for most Large Language Models (LLMs) [16]. Newly
developed Reasoning Language Models (RLMs) [9, 39], however,
are designed for step-by-step, methodical problem-solving, which
makes them promising candidates for testing whether stronger
internal reasoning can support causal inference.
This deliberative nature of RLMs also suggests that they may be
especially well-suited to collaborative dialogue between competing
hypotheses, but recent surveys of the literature revealed that no
prior work has explored whether multi-agent debate (MAD) can
improve causal inference skills in either LLMs or RLMs [23, 29, 44,
45, 50]. In a MAD paradigm, multiple agents engage in structured
discussion, proposing solutions, critiquing each other’s arguments,
and refining their reasoning in response to feedback. This approach
has demonstrated significant improvements over single-agent performance across many non-causal domains [3, 5, 6, 10, 12, 22, 25, 27,
48, 51, 58]. While debate seems naturally suited to the hypothesistesting inherent in causal inference, it is crucial to evaluate on a
rigorous dataset to properly assess any improvements.
Jin et al. [16] argued that previous datasets [56, 57] for evaluating commonsense causality understanding in language models
lacked formal construction rules, making it impossible to determine
whether models were performing true causal reasoning or taking
a shortcut known as "amortized causal reasoning" – essentially
acting as "causal parrots" that repeat training data patterns [54].
To address this lack, Jin et al. constructed the CLadder dataset,
which ties each natural language question to a formally defined
causal model, with correct answers computed by an oracle inspired
by Pearl’s causal inference engine [35]. Some of those questions
intentionally contradict real-world knowledge, while others use
randomly generated variables, forcing language models to rely on
formal reasoning rather than memorization. In their evaluations, Jin
et al. found that ChatGPT-4 [30] achieved 70.40% overall accuracy
using chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting [49], but only 62.05% for
counterfactual questions.
Since CLadder’s release in 2023, the emergence of RLMs offers
new opportunities to revisit these benchmarks. Unlike traditional
LLMs that generate responses in a single forward pass, RLMs
are trained using reinforcement learning with human feedback
(RLHF) to engage in extended deliberation before producing final
answers [9, 39]. This training paradigm explicitly rewards models
for reasoning steps rather than immediate conclusions, encouraging
them to explore multiple solution paths, backtrack when encountering contradictions, and refine their thinking iteratively [19]. Such
behaviours complement both causal inference and MAD: by supplying more coherent and self-consistent reasoning chains, RLMs
provide a stronger foundation for agents to articulate, evaluate,
and refine competing causal hypotheses. This, in turn, increases
the likelihood that MAD between RLMs will converge on more
accurate and interpretable conclusions.

To the best of our knowledge, there has only been one prior
attempt so far to evaluate and compare the formal causal inference
capabilities of RLMs in a single-agent setting [53]. More precisely,
DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI RLMs were included alongside a selection of standard LLMs in evaluations on CLadder. However, due
to "cost and time constraints", the analysis and comparison of the
RLMs investigated was restricted to only 100 random samples from
the total of 10,112 questions available in the dataset. The RLM
performance results on these 100 samples are considerably higher
than the performance of ChatGPT-4 [30], with DeepSeek-R1 [9],
OpenAI o1 [31], and OpenAI o3-mini achieving an overall accuracy of 92.0%, 89.0%, and 92.2%, respectively. However, because the
CLadder dataset is stratified into three categories with vastly different difficulties, the overall accuracy can be a misleading metric
without providing detailed information about the composition of
samples. Therefore, further study is required to rigorously evaluate
RLMs on the full dataset, both in single-agent settings and in MAD
frameworks.
In this paper, we make the dialogue between hypotheses during
causal inference explicit through the implementation of debates between RLMs, and apply this framework to the full CLadder dataset.
To encourage more direct, adversarial engagement between models, we adopt a dual-agent approach, and select the most advanced
open-source RLMs available: DeepSeek-R1 [9] and Qwen3 [39].
Our results demonstrate that collaborative reasoning through debate substantially improves performance on causal inference tasks,
with the most significant gains observed on the most challenging
counterfactual queries. By conducting the first comprehensive evaluation of RLMs on the full CLadder dataset and establishing strong
baselines for both single-agent and multi-agent performance, we
provide a benchmark for future work at the intersection of causal
inference and collaborative AI systems.
Thus, the main contributions of this paper can be summarized
as follows:
(1) We performed an in-depth analysis and comparison of the
causal inference capabilities of two open-source RLMs on
the full CLadder dataset, categorizing performance by Rung.
(2) We implemented a novel dual-agent debate framework that
encourages more direct engagement between RLMs, and
includes confidence estimation, an early-stopping strategy,
and refined prompting.
(3) We evaluate key outcomes across all debates to explore
whether dual-agent debate improves final answer accuracy
across all categories of the CLadder dataset.

RELATED WORK

There have been many studies to date investigating the single-agent
performance of LLMs in causal tasks [1, 7, 15, 17, 20, 50, 60]. To our
knowledge, however, the only prior work applying multi-agent debate to formal causality does so for the sake of causal discovery, not
causal inference [21]. However, the authors acknowledge that their
method is limited to observational data and cannot reason about
interventions, restricting its applicability in practice. Moreover, recent critiques have questioned the use of standard LLMs specifically
for causal discovery, noting that their correlation-driven training
and optimization makes them unreliable for determining causal

graph structures on their own [52]. In contrast, our work focuses
on causal inference, a distinct problem where the task is to answer
interventional and counterfactual queries based on a known causal
graph.
A related line of work applies multi-agent debate in a causalityadjacent context, focusing on evaluating causal consistency in reasoning chains generated by standard LLMs [43]. In this framework,
evaluator agents judge the logical coherence of arguments from
multiple reasoners. Importantly, these agents are not RLMs, and
the evaluation was conducted on commonsense and knowledgeintensive benchmarks, which do not require formal causal inference
over interventions or counterfactuals, as is the focus of our work.
Furthermore, both of these papers use the same base model for
all agents, which may lead to shared biases in the dialogue [21, 43].
To overcome this limitation in our work, we took inspiration from
recent studies that demonstrated the significant benefits that can
be achieved through debate using diverse base models, as opposed
to relying on a debate of the same base models (such as GPT-4) for
all agents [4, 47].
Most MAD approaches described so far require debates to go
through predetermined number of discussion rounds before reaching a conclusion. However, prior work has shown that by initiating
a debate only when the confidence of the first speaker is below a
certain threshold, a similar final answer accuracy can be achieved
with drastically fewer model calls [11]. In our work, we adapt this
idea, but use a novel setup that always elicits at least one response
from each agent, and only initiates a debate if they disagree on the
answer. This setup forms a key component of our methodology,
which we detail in the following sections.

3 MATERIAL AND METHODS
3.1 Dataset
The CLadder dataset that we use for evaluation in this work consists of 10,112 questions, each asking a formal causal inference
question that requires careful reasoning, arithmetic, and knowledge of causality concepts and formulas in order to answer them
correctly. The questions in this database are primarily split into
three Rungs of causation [35], which increase monotonically in
difficulty. There are 3,792 samples that fall in Rung 3, while the
Rung 1 and 2 categories each consists of 3,160 samples.
Rung 1 ("seeing") questions ask about statistical associations
between variables, such as "is there a positive relationship between
student coffee consumption and test scores?". Rung 2 ("doing")
questions require active intervention in the world, for example,
asking "if students are made to drink coffee before their exam, will
their scores improve?". Lastly, Rung 3 ("imagining") questions are
about counterfactuals, which requires reasoning about alternatives
scenarios that contradict reality. In other words, they ask about
ways in which the world could have been different. A question of
this kind could be: "Bob got a 85% on his test after drinking coffee.
Would his score have been lower if he had not drank coffee?".
The secondary way the data is split is through three alignments
to commonsense. The first commonsense alignment is that of normal, empirical questions that were sourced from the causality literature. In the second commonsense alignment, one variable is
randomly replaced with a concept that has no correlation to the

other causal variables in the statement: for example, this may produce prompts stating that “ear shape affects lung tar deposits” or
“full moon affects vaccination rate”. Lastly, in the third commonsense alignment category, all causal variables are replaced with
random nonsensical words: for instance, the model may be told
that “zory affects gwet”.

3.2

Debate Setup

As described above, the most common MAD configurations described in the literature consist of three debater models, or two
debater models and one judge model [23, 45]. However, because
we specifically wanted to explore the extent to which RLMs are
able to persuade each other to change their answer, we decided
to only include two agents that are directly engaging with each
other. This setup allows for more adversarial interactions between
the agents and more specific language addressing faults in logic or
calculation. While the use of a judge is not incompatible with this
goal, recent studies have suggested that language models express
several biases when assigned as a judge [25, 40], such as a tendency to favour the final speaker [28]. Furthermore, it was recently
demonstrated that forcing models to defend assigned stances may
degrade performance, while allowing them to change their opinions in response to opposing views may lead to better outcomes,
without requiring a judge [18]. Therefore, we elected to not include
a judge agent, allowing the agents to debate directly and change
their answers if persuaded by the other model. This paradigm also
obviates the computational overhead introduced through a third
agent, and circumvents the need to define which model must act as
judge.
With the content of the dataset and the general structure of our
debate framework established, we now describe the specific details
of our implementation. Naturally, each question spurs its own debate, and the first debate round must begin by randomly selecting
one model as first speaker, in an attempt to more fairly assess the
performance of each RLM. The debate format is not explained to
the model at this point in order to keep the prompt setup as similar as possible to that used in Jin et al.’s original paper [16]. Their
prompting strategy, which they refer to as CausalCoT, provides
details of every step of the formal causal inference the RLM must
follow in its final answer, including formalizing the causal graph
and identifying the query type out of a list of options. After these
instructions are provided, the RLM is provided with the background
information (such as causal relationships) and probabilities that are
associated with the question in the CLadder dataset. The model is
also instructed to give a final yes/no answer, and, in a divergence
from CausalCoT, asked to produce an estimation of their confidence,
as is done in several other MAD approaches [2, 26]. Furthermore,
we instruct this first model to be more self-confident and accept
that answers may go against their knowledge of commonsense due
to the nature of the dataset. This setup was motivated by preliminary experiments, in which we found that models had a tendency
to over-think the questions and doubt their answers, which led to
substantially longer response times.
Once this full prompt (shown in Appendix B) is received, the
first RLM begins following the steps of causal inference, parsing the
data provided in the question, materializing the formula from its

pre-trained knowledge base, and executing the needed arithmetic
and causal do-calculus [41]. Most of this deliberative process happens in an internal "reasoning trace", but because of its verbosity
and inclusion of discarded information, we only send the agent’s
concise rationale and final answer to their opponent. However, we
still saved this reasoning trace for later manual analysis. With the
first speaker’s response complete, the second round begins. To encourage more direct engagement with the first model’s work, the
opposing model is not guided to follow all the steps of formal inference again. Instead, it is presented information about the format
of the debate and the new expectations that come with it, namely
to scrutinize the opponent’s response carefully to find any flaws
in reasoning, and form a suitable rebuttal to their response (full
prompt shown in Appendix C). However, we do not force the second model to find a flaw in the first model’s response, if none exists.
Thus, if the two models agree with each other, then that answer
will be accepted as final.
However, if the second model disagrees with the answer of the
first model, round 3 begins, and the critique is passed back to the
first model. We do not force the first model to adhere to its previous
stance as it is simply instructed to consider the critique, and may
either defend its initial answer or revise it based on the opponent’s
argument. If the first model rejects the critique and defends their
original answer, then this refutation is sent back to the second
model for round 4, whose subsequent response will be logged as
the final answer to the question, even if the models are still in
disagreement. Two examples of a debate between agents going to
four rounds are provided in Figure 1 and Appendix D. In initial
experiments, we found that additional rounds lead to little benefit,
since when both models have made their case and remain steadfast
in their answers, it is unlikely that further discussion changes their
response. This is in line with previous research on standard LLMs
that showed that continued discussion past a certain threshold leads
to a stagnation or even degradation of answer accuracy [3].

3.3

Models

In accordance with principles of open science and reproducibility,
we restricted our evaluation to open-source RLMs with publicly
available weights, excluding proprietary models from OpenAI, Anthropic (Claude), Google (Gemini), and similar providers. At the
time of implementation, there were only three RLMs available on
the free Ollama platform: Qwen3, DeepSeek-R1, and Magistral. Due
to hardware limitations, we chose Qwen3-32B and DeepSeek-R1Distill-Qwen-32B for this work, which provided the optimal balance
of reasoning capabilities and VRAM requirements. Preliminary tests
confirmed that Magistral-24B exhibited substantially lower performance than the two 32B models, further supporting this selection.
It is important to note that the DeepSeek-R1-Qwen-32B model used
is derived from a Qwen2.5 base model, which was fine-tuned on
the reasoning outputs of the DeepSeek-R1-671B model. However,
Qwen3 was trained on twice as many tokens as Qwen2.5, and underwent several advancements in model architecture, which should
introduce substantial heterogeneity between the two models. As
established in previous works [4, 47], such model diversity is critical
for effective debate, and we believe this is especially true when addressing causal inference, as it increases the likelihood that agents

will propose competing causal hypotheses rather than reinforcing
shared biases.
In preliminary tests, we observed that the two RLMs did not
respond well to requests for structured outputs. More precisely,
they either neglect that part of the prompt, or bypass the thinking
stage entirely if Ollama’s structured output functionality is instated.
This finding is in line with recent research that found that reasoning
models are worse at following instructions than standard LLMs [24,
42]. In response, we added a Granite3.3-2B model to our pipeline to
extract final yes/no answers and confidence estimations from the
primary RLMs responses. In a manual inspection of 50 randomly
sampled debate outputs, the Granite model was always able to
faithfully extract the appropriate outcomes.

3.4

Experiments

Our primary evaluation compares model accuracy before and after
debate, with accuracy measured as the proportion of RLM responses
matching ground truth labels in CLadder. To provide a more comprehensive assessment of debate efficacy beyond accuracy alone,
we performed additional analyses on the full set of model outputs
as discussed below.
Persuasion dynamics. To assess whether debate improves or
corrupts initial responses, we tracked answer changes from the first
model’s initial response to the final consensus answer across all
questions requiring debate (3+ rounds). For each model, we classified outcomes into four categories: correct-to-correct (maintained
accuracy), incorrect-to-correct (persuaded toward truth), correctto-incorrect (corrupted by peer), and incorrect-to-incorrect (failed
to improve). We stratified these results by both model and Rung to
identify whether persuasion patterns differ by model capability or
question difficulty, revealing which model is more influential in the
debate process. Next, we conducted a sentiment analysis on model
responses to determine the average positivity or negativity associated with the language they use; both when formulating an initial
answer, and when debating with their peer. To do so, we removed
all formatting from model outputs and passed them through the
SentimentIntensityAnalyzer of the popular VADER library [14].
Confidence calibration. For each model, we compared confidence distributions for correct versus incorrect answers, both
in initial responses and after being challenged by their peer. This
analysis addresses three questions: whether confidence aligns with
correctness (calibration), how question difficulty (Rung) affects
this alignment, and whether facing a highly confident opponent
increases or decreases the likelihood of changing one’s answer.
Response characteristics. We measured response length distributions for both initial answers and debate rounds to assess engagement depth and identify potential asymmetries in argumentative
efforts between models. Finally, we visualized the distribution of
rounds required to reach consensus across difficulty levels (Rungs),
as well as the consensus rate by the final round, providing insight
into debate efficiency and the extent to which question complexity
affects deliberation time.

Acc. by Commonsense Alignment

Persuasion

Figure 2 illustrates the net positive effect that the debate framework
has on final answer accuracy, when models disagree on the initial
answer. Briefly described, the results suggest that it is far more
likely for the debate to change an incorrect answer to a correct one,
than vice versa. The results also show that DeepSeek-R1 is more
susceptible to persuasion, as it is more likely to accept Qwen3’s
answer when challenged. Nonetheless, Qwen3 still benefits greatly
from debating with DeepSeek-R1, correcting many of its initially
wrong responses while doing a much better job of defending its
correct answers. This helps to explain the finding illustrated in
Table 1 showing that the DeepSeek-R1 model had lower initial
scores than Qwen3, while also gaining more benefit from the debate
process. In other words, the Qwen3 model seems better at both
reasoning and persuasion.

Confidence

Figure 3 shows that both models are often extremely confident,
with confidence never falling below 60% for either model. This is
reasonable given our explicit instruction to be confident, but we
also see that the more difficult the Rung is, the less confident the
models tend to be initially, which suggests that they do not suppress
their uncertainty entirely. There seems to be no evident difference
between the commonsense alignment of the question and a model’s
confidence (i.e., anti-commonsense questions do not make them
more cautious). It is also notable that both models seem reluctant
to output a confidence in the 65-80% range, and have a similar
confidence distribution, although Qwen3 tends to be slightly more
confident.

4.3

Rounds Taken

Further analysis, shown in Figure 6, revealed that the harder Rungs
demanded more debate rounds on average than the first Rung,
where in 93% of all cases both models agreed upon an answer immediately. In contrast, approximately a quarter of Rung 3 questions
were disputed, although most of these disputes were settled in the
third round, with the first speaker convinced by the opponent’s
argument. Interestingly, it was found that Rung 2 questions were
resolved more quickly when Qwen3 was the first speaker compared
to DeepSeek-R1 being the first model. However, this is the only
Rung where such a correlation between the first speaker and rounds
taken was found. Out of the 10112 questions in the dataset, only
148 ended without the models reaching consensus on an answer.
Therefore, even if both models could agree on the correct answer in
these cases (e.g., through more discussion, or a better tie-breaking
strategy), the total accuracy would only increase by 0.55%.

4.4

Response Length

The results of the evaluation showed that the DeepSeek-R1 model
has a tendency to provide extremely short responses during debate.
More precisely, we observed that 22.60% (1412/6247) of DeepSeekR1’s debate responses were 100 characters or less, compared to 0.40%
(11/5986) for Qwen3. As illustrated in Figure 7a, the DeepSeek-R1
model has much shorter responses on average, even when removing
these very short responses. More precisely, DeepSeek-R1 provided
responses with a median of 249 characters compared to Qwen3’s 739
characters. Figure 7b shows that DeepSeek-R1’s initial responses do
not produce such short outputs, although they are still shorter on
average, with a median of 735 characters compared to Qwen3’s 1012.
Interestingly, we found that the underlying reasoning traces of both
models actually have very similar distributions of character counts
(Figure 7c). The manual inspection of a few selected reasoning
traces showed that DeepSeek-R1 is indeed considering both the
problem and its opponent’s response, but inexplicably ignores the
prompt’s instructions when generating its final response, providing
no external elaboration of its reasoning. This likely contributes to
why DeepSeek-R1 is worse at persuading Qwen3 than vice versa,
even if it has the right answer. This may also be the reason for
the reduced efficacy of the debate as a whole, as DeepSeek-R1 is
providing no substantive content or argument in these specific
cases.

DISCUSSION

Our results demonstrate that debate between RLMs can considerably increase the accuracy of their final answer, with the largest
gains being associated with the hardest problems. Based on our
results, it may be concluded that Qwen3 is generally better-suited
for this causal inference task compared to DeepSeek-R1. However,
it is interesting that arguing with DeepSeek-R1 still improves its
answers, and in the case of Rung 3 questions, quite considerably.
Equally intriguing is how the DeepSeek-R1 model’s accuracy does
not improve to the performance levels of the Qwen3 model, despite
the debate rounds being roughly equal. This finding suggests that
the initial answer has an effect on the subsequent outcome, which
would skew final answers in favour of the first given. Another potential reason could be that the models tested, similar to humans,
may tend to glance over a proof and support it rather than trying
to find a specific error in the labelling, logic, or arithmetic. Nevertheless, this demonstrates the benefit of the debate setup that
even a weaker model is able to contribute to a stronger model’s
improvement, and the stronger model can improve the weak one
past its own initial performance.
While our results show substantial improvement of RLMs relative to the performance achieved by the trillion-parameter GPT-4
model as described in the original 2023 CLadder paper, and Rung
1 and 2 questions are largely solvable, it seems that open source
reasoning models of this size still struggle with Rung 3 questions.
Moreover, while the Qwen3 model was able to match human performance in Rung 1 queries after debating with DeepSeek-R1, it fell
slightly short in Rung 2, even after debate, and very short in Rung
3, with the DeepSeek-R1 model performing even worse. While the
human accuracy reported in prior work [53] is not split by Rung,
it seems that RLMs still cannot match the human ability to reason about counterfactuals. If this is a limitation inherent to the
correlation-based nature LLMs and RLMs, one possible solution
would be the creation of an external tool that models can query
for the precise calculation of counterfactual scenarios, such as the
one recently implemented by Verma et al. [46]. While our results
show that LLMs and RLMs cannot yet be trusted to answer counterfactual questions without the use of such a tool, we nonetheless
demonstrate the potential of reasoning models as building blocks
for multi-agent systems in causal inference, and highlight the importance of diverse perspectives in causal problem-solving.
The disparity of DeepSeek-R1’s accuracy between our study and
Yu et al.’s is considerable. Their improved performance suggests
that they either evaluate on the larger DeepSeek-R1-671B model,
or that their random sampling primarily pulled from Rung 1 questions, where this accuracy would be on par with our evaluation of
DeepSeek-R1’s accuracy before debating with Qwen3. However,
our work shows that reasoning models still struggle with Rung 3
questions, even after debating with a peer.
The results of the confidence analysis show interesting alignments to human tendencies. Firstly, when a model agrees with the
opposing model, it is much more likely to be confident in its final
answer. Secondly, the models investigated seem to take the opposing model’s confidence into account when making a decision of
whether or not to change their answer in response. The models’
aversion to estimating their confidence to be in the 65-80% range

might suggest a lack of these confidences in their training data, or a

conventional approach of predetermined debate length.

5.1

Limitations

Given CLadder’s creation date of 2023 [16], we cannot discount the
possibility that DeepSeek-R1 or Qwen3 (which were both released
in early 2025) were exposed to its contents during their training.
While we considered generating an entirely new set of data using
Cladder’s generation code, we were concerned that this might compromise the direct comparison to Jin et al.’s results in a different
way if we generated easier or harder questions by chance.
Another possible limitation of our study is related to CLadder’s
synthetic nature: its questions are simplified, clean, and textually
uniform compared to real-world causal reasoning problems, which
often involve messy natural language, ambiguous causal structures,
or incomplete data. Without synthetic data, however, it would be
much more difficult to establish precise ground truth and reliably
measure model correctness at this granularity.
Next, combining the lengthy response times of RLMs with the
added model calls inherent to MAD introduces considerable additional resource and time costs for our model, with the 10,112
questions of CLadder taking 380 hours in total to answer. Due
to this time constraint, we were unable to conduct potentially informative ablation studies, including: the substitution of standard
LLMs, using three or more RLMs, implementing a judge framework,
omitting confidence estimation, and removing instructions to be
more confident. However, using our framework, all of those can be
investigated in future.
Furthermore, because of limited options among open-source
models, we only experiment with one pair of agents. Thus, it is
possible that different combinations of agents will yield different

results from debate, and, of course, different models will achieve
different initial accuracies. In particular, our lack of evaluation on
OpenAI’s models hinders direct comparison to other works that
use a single-agent paradigm.

5.2

Future Work

Due to hardware limitations, 32B parameter models were the largest
we could use for our experiments. Thus, future work should evaluate the larger models in the Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1 families to
investigate whether a scaling law exists between model size and
formal causal inference capabilities, as previously found in several
other domains [8, 38, 55, 59].
Future studies on the intersection of causal inference and multiagent systems should also investigate different frameworks for
debate or collaboration. Using three agents, or two agents and
a judge, are both straightforward starting points, though more
complex paradigms may lead to further gains.

6

CONCLUSION

In this paper, we demonstrated that debates between RLMs improve
their collective ability to formally reason about causality in the
CLadder dataset. We found that, even when applied independently,
comparatively lightweight open-source reasoning models surpass
GPT-4’s 70.40% accuracy baseline, and when encouraged to critique
each other’s reasoning, the overall accuracy of Qwen3-32B rises
to 89.41%, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B to 87.45%. These
promising results encourage further research on the implementation of multi-agent frameworks for addressing causal reasoning
tasks.

TIME TAKEN

Figure 8 shows the trend of median time to generate responses
decreasing after the first round, with considerably more time needed
to answer Rung 3 questions across both models. We also see that
the Qwen3 model takes longer to generate a response on average,
and takes longer to respond when speaking last compared to round
two and three, with DeepSeek-R1 showing no such change. We also
note that the two models occasionally got stuck in loops, with three
responses each taking between 40 and 46 minutes to generate. There
were a further six responses that took between 20 and 30 minutes

were from Qwen3 and 10 from DeepSeek-R1. Exploring the raw
outputs for these outliers, it was found that the models were not
only simply deliberating at length, but were repeating the exact
same snippets over and over again.
