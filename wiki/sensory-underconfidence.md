# Sensory underconfidence

> In two-alternative sensory discrimination the Uppsala laboratory reports
> confidence falling **below** proportion correct even at low accuracy — where
> the general-knowledge corpus shows overconfidence at the same accuracy
> levels ([[hard-easy-effect]]) — while the Ottawa laboratory finds the
> ordinary hard–easy pattern in the same domain, and after a
> display-controlled replication the two still disagree.

## Definition / Mechanism

**The phenomenon.** Björkman, Juslin & Winman (1993, *Perception &[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s4][^s5]
Psychophysics* 54, 75–81) "document a very pervasive underconfidence bias in
the area of sensory discrimination": in pair comparisons of physical stimuli,
subjects "perform better than they express in their confidence assessments."
Their *subjective distance theory* derives this from Thurstone's law of
[[recursive-comparative-judgment|comparative judgment]] with confidence an increasing function of the perceived
distance between the two stimuli, and concludes that "due to the fixed
sensitivity of the sensory system, this underconfidence bias is practically
impossible to avoid." Their Experiment 2 reports that 160 trials of outcome
feedback had no effect on it.

**The counter-result.** Baranski & Petrusic (1994, *P&P* 55, 412–428) ran two
perceptual-comparison experiments and one visual gap-detection experiment and
report that "as in the general knowledge domain, subjects were underconfident
when judgments were easy and overconfident when they were difficult" — the
hard–easy pattern, in a perceptual task. Three further results are theirs:
*resolution* fell as decision difficulty rose, whether difficulty came from
lower discriminability or from speed pressure; trial-by-trial response
feedback on difficult tasks "improved resolution but had no effect on
[[calibration]]"; and subjects could report their own errors accurately with the
confidence rating.

**The artefact exchange.** Ferrell (1995, *P&P* 57, 246–254) argues that the
subjective distance theory is a special case of the decision-variable
partition model, that the model does not inherently predict underconfidence,
and that the hard–easy effect does appear in sensory discrimination — one
framework for both domains. Olsson & Winman (1996, *P&P* 58, 374–382) reply to
Baranski & Petrusic that their overconfidence came from features of the
stimulus display and of the experimental setting, and report a large
underconfidence bias once presentation is subject-controlled, with
overconfidence rarely occurring "even with low accuracy rates."

**The unresolved end.** Baranski & Petrusic (1999, *P&P* 61, 1369–1383)
reanalysed their 1994 data and ran a new experiment controlling the stimulus
display characteristics Olsson & Winman named. Their conclusion is that "the
difference between the Canadian and the Swedish data is real and, thus, may
reflect cross-national differences in confidence in sensory discrimination."
The disagreement was not resolved into a measurement error; it was relocated
into the population.

## Known as

| Field | Name there | Match |

| Psychophysics, the Ottawa line (Baranski & Petrusic) | realism of confidence in sensory discrimination | close — names the measured quantity, the sign and size of the bias, without asserting that it is negative |
| JDM / general-knowledge calibration | underconfidence | close — there the same word names the sign of the bias on *easy* item samples within one difficulty range, not a bias attributed to a whole task domain |

Not this concept: the **[[hard-easy-effect]]** is the *slope* of
over/underconfidence against difficulty measured across item samples, and
Baranski & Petrusic report it *inside* the perceptual domain — a different
quantity from the domain-level sign this note is about. **Resolution** (the
discrimination component of the Brier decomposition) is a third quantity,
which Baranski & Petrusic moved with feedback while calibration stayed put.

## Why it matters

Stall 10 asks how far an answerer should commit.

1. **A miscalibration correction may not be assumed to carry.** The same
   two-alternative structure and the same confidence-rating format yield
   opposite directions of error across the two laboratories' settings, and the
   disputants themselves could not localise the difference in the stimulus
   display — Baranski & Petrusic (1999) attribute it to the respondent
   population instead. Whichever of question class, apparatus and population
   turns out to fix the sign, a correction fitted in one setting is not
   known to have the right sign in another.
2. **Undercommitment is a measured failure mode, not a hypothetical one.**
   Under subject-controlled sensory pair comparison (Björkman et al. 1993;
   Olsson & Winman 1996) the modal error is reporting *less* confidence than
   accuracy warrants, so it exists as a condition to be designed for. The
   wiki's commitment machinery — [[warning-as-debiasing]], [[abstention]] —
   is built to pull commitment *down*; where the bias runs this way those
   interventions cost information ([[constraint-as-information]]) and buy
   nothing.
3. **Feedback separates into components here too, independently.** 160 trials
   of outcome feedback moved nothing (Björkman et al. 1993) and trial-by-trial
   feedback moved resolution but not calibration (Baranski & Petrusic 1994) —
   two perceptual replications of the null in [[outcome-feedback]] and of the
   component-specificity in [[calibration-training]], in a domain neither of
   those notes covers.
4. **It answers the question that was asked of it weakly, and that is the
   finding.** Whether the hard–easy pattern holds for perceptual tasks is
   *contested between laboratories* after a controlled attempt to reconcile
   them, so the perceptual literature neither licenses nor refutes placing a
   near-chance visual task on the general-knowledge line.

## Transfer

| | |
|---|---|
| **Home field** | undergraduates comparing two physical stimuli (line length, weight, a visual gap) under laboratory-controlled or subject-controlled presentation, confidence given as a rating with the correct answer objectively fixed |
| **Here** | an answering agent reporting confidence on a question whose uncertainty is in its own perception of the given material rather than in what it knows about the world |
| **What survives** | that there exist measured conditions — Uppsala-style subject-controlled sensory pair comparison — under which the modal error is *under*commitment, including at low accuracy where the general-knowledge corpus is overconfident; that one and the same two-alternative rating format yields opposite signs across settings, so a miscalibration correction may not be assumed to carry from the setting it was fitted in; and the two feedback nulls |
| **What does not** | any magnitude — no effect size in this note was read from a full text; the attribution of the sign to the *question class*, since Baranski & Petrusic (1999) relocate the difference into the respondent population after controlling the display, leaving class, apparatus and population unseparated; the mechanism, since subjective distance theory derives the bias from a fixed sensory sensitivity that has no counterpart in an agent with no sensory channel; and the domain label itself, since the two laboratories' data disagree about whether the class behaves differently at all |

## Limits

- **Every claim here is read from the published abstracts; no full text of
  any of the five papers was obtained** [?]. That is why this note states
  directions and quoted conclusions and no effect sizes: the claim it rests
  on is "the reported direction of the bias differs between the two
  laboratories' settings," which the abstracts do support, and nothing
  quantitative.
- **The cross-national explanation is the disputing authors' own inference
  about their own non-replication** (Baranski & Petrusic 1999) and no
  independent test of it is cited here. Read as a finding it is a
  non-replication with an untested cause, not a demonstrated population
  difference — which is also why this note claims no selector for the sign.
- **Difficulty is again the observed proportion correct.** The linear
  dependency that [[hard-easy-effect]] shows is sufficient on its own to
  manufacture a hard–easy slope applies identically to Baranski & Petrusic's
  perceptual slope, and nothing read here reports that correction being made —
  so their "underconfident when easy, overconfident when difficult" is
  uncorrected in the same way the general-knowledge corpus was.
- No source here measures a machine answerer, and no source here classifies a
  task into the two domains by anything other than the experimenter's design
  ([[origins-of-uncertainty-in-judgment]]).
