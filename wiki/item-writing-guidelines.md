# Item-writing guidelines

> The field that has been writing rules for question wording longest keeps 31
> of them — and grades every rule against two separate evidence sources, which
> disagree often enough that several rules are carried as contested rather
> than as rules.

## Definition / Mechanism

Haladyna, Downing & Rodriguez (2002) revise a taxonomy of **31[^s1]
multiple-choice item-writing guidelines**, grouped by what they constrain:

```text
content concerns      8   one specific behaviour per item; non-trivial content;
                          novel material; item independence; no trick items
formatting            2   item format; vertical layout
style                 3   editing, grammar, minimise reading load
writing the stem      4   clear directions; central idea in the stem;
                          no window dressing; positive wording
writing the choices  14   number of options; one right answer; independent,
                          homogeneous, equal-length, plausible distractors;
                          none/all-of-the-above; clue avoidance
```

**The validation is the point.** Each guideline is judged against two sources:
whether each of 27 educational-measurement textbooks cites it and cites it
*supportively*, and what 27 research studies and reviews published since 1990
find. The authors then record a summative judgement per rule. Their Table 2 is
a per-rule tally of For / Uncited / Against percentages across the textbooks:

| Guideline | For | Uncited | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central idea in the stem | 100% | 0 | 0 |
| Avoid clues to the right answer | 96% | 4 | 0 |
| Word the stem positively | 63% | 19 | 18 |
| Use *[[catch-all-consequence|None-of-the-above]]* carefully | 44% | 7 | **48%** |
| Avoid *All-of-the-above* | 70% | 7 | 22% |
| Use humour sparingly | 0% | 85 | 15% |

The predecessor review (Haladyna & Downing, 1989b, reported in this paper)
examined 93 empirical studies and found they bore on **about half** the
guidelines, the other half unstudied — and the authors defend some of the
unstudied ones as consensus values that do not need research.

## Why it matters

1. This is the shape an operational check on a finished question actually
   takes when a field maintains one for a century: a numbered, grouped list
   in which **each line carries its own warrant**, some empirical, some
   consensus, some contested. Not a criterion, and not derived from a theory
   of the item — Haladyna, Downing & Rodriguez validate the list against what
   textbooks endorse and what studies have happened to examine, and report
   that roughly half of it has never been studied at all.
2. It licenses the refusal to state one criterion for a good question, and it
   names the alternative: record, per rule, whether it is evidence or
   agreement, and let the two disagree in public.
3. Fourteen of the 31 rules govern the **choices**, four the stem. This
   wiki's reading (not the authors' claim): the offered answer set is where
   the field found the error to live — which is the same relocation
   [[survey-quality-predictor]] makes from a different direction, and which
   [[question-as-partition]] treats as free.

## Transfer

| | |
|---|---|
| **In its home field** | a written test item — a stem plus three to five choices — scored right/wrong against a key and administered to a class; the criterion for a rule is its effect on item difficulty, discrimination, or test-score validity |
| **Here** | an agent wording one question to one answerer, with no key, no score and often no fixed option set |
| **What survives** | (a) the object: a finite grouped rule list with a per-rule warrant, instead of a single quality criterion; (b) the choice rules — independence, non-overlap, homogeneity, equal length, plausible distractors, no clues — which apply to any question that offers the answerer a closed set; (c) the discipline of recording whether a line is evidence or consensus |
| **What does not** | the criterion, since difficulty and discrimination need many test-takers and a scoring key, so no rule can be re-validated in a one-answerer setting; and the format and style rules (vertical layout, proofing, humour), which are artefacts of printed classroom tests |

## Limits

- The percentages above are **citation frequencies across 27 textbooks**, not
  effect sizes. A rule endorsed by 100% of textbooks may still rest on no
  experiment; the authors say so and keep such rules deliberately.
- The declared setting is classroom assessment. The authors note implications
  for large-scale testing as an extrapolation, and this note's use of the
  taxonomy outside testing altogether is a further one.
- None of the 31 rules addresses presupposition, premise falsity or
  suggestive wording ([[presupposition-accommodation]],
  [[false-premise-question]]) — the defects that matter most where the
  answerer is not being scored. The list covers clarity, format and distractor
  construction.
- The claim in point 3 above, that 14-of-31 locates the error in the option
  set, is this wiki's inference from the taxonomy's shape. Haladyna et al.
  make no such claim; the count is theirs, the reading is ours.
