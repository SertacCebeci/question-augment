# Levels of information need

> The question an answerer receives is not the asker's need — it is the need
> already recast, by the asker, in anticipation of what this particular
> answerer is thought to stock.

## Definition / Mechanism

Taylor (1962; restated with interview evidence in 1968) places the question[^s1][^s2]
that reaches an information system at the end of a four-step deformation. He
calls the four "convenient points along a continuum" — the *question
spectrum* — not discrete stages:

| | Level | Taylor's description |
|---|---|---|
| $Q_1$ | **visceral** | the actual but unexpressed need; "may be only a vague sort of dissatisfaction… probably inexpressible in linguistic terms"; not yet a question |
| $Q_2$ | **conscious** | a conscious within-brain description of an ill-defined area of indecision; "an ambiguous and rambling statement" |
| $Q_3$ | **formalized** | a qualified and rational statement of the question — the area of doubt in concrete terms, with no thought yet for the answerer's constraints |
| $Q_4$ | **compromised** | "the question is recast in anticipation of what the files can deliver"; the asker now thinks in terms of the answerer's organisation and its discrete deliverable packages |

Two consequences carry the note.

**The received input is $Q_4$ by default, and the deformation is a function of
the asker's model of the answerer.** Taylor: "Unless the inquirer knows the
information specialist well, he is inclined to pose his first question in
positive and well-defined terms, even to the point of specifying a particular
package ($Q_4$). If the specialist is accepted as a colleague, the negotiation
process can start earlier." The answerer's declared capability is therefore an
input to the question, not just to the answer. The stated job of the answerer
is to work the asker back to $Q_3$, "possibly even to the conscious need
($Q_2$)", and only then to translate into a search.

**A $Q_4$ is a command, and it rests on two assumptions of which one is
false.** Taylor separates *command* (a request for a named item the asker has
already assumed will satisfy the need) from *question*. A command assumes the
asker knows what he wants and can label it, and that he knows the functional
organisation of the system — "the rules of the game". His conclusion from the
interviews: only the first assumption may be valid; the second, with some
exceptions, is not. The compromise is made against the asker's *belief* about
the answerer's inventory, which is usually wrong.

What the 1968 interviews add is what must be reconstructed to undo the
compromise — five filters: (1) determination of subject; (2) objective and
motivation; (3) personal characteristics of the inquirer; (4) relationship of
the inquiry description to file organisation; (5) anticipated or acceptable
answers. Filter 5 is itself unstable: the asker's *a priori* picture of an
acceptable answer changes during the exchange as capabilities become visible.

## Why it matters

1. **"As worded or as meant" is a ladder position, not a binary.** Stall 9's
   open cell asks which of the two an answerer should serve. Taylor's model
   replaces the pair with four, and names the direction of loss: answering as
   worded is answering $Q_4$, a projection of the need onto what the asker
   guessed was stockable.
2. **Over-specificity is a symptom, not a constraint.** Because the compromise
   runs toward a nameable package, a precise request is evidence of
   compromise. Taylor's interviewees report that inquirers "seldom ask at
   first for what they want" and that an unambiguous request for a directory
   address became a search on molds.
3. **The answerer can edit the question before it is asked.** The compromise
   is against the asker's model of the answerer; that model is built from what
   the answerer displays. No other note on stall 9 gives the answerer a lever
   that acts *prior* to the utterance.
4. **The acceptable answer form is part of comprehension.** Filter 5 puts the
   asker's expected answer shape inside stall 9 rather than stall 10, and
   makes it revisable by feedback.

## Transfer

| | |
|---|---|
| **In its home field** | a human reference librarian at a desk, negotiating face to face with an inquirer over a card catalogue and a physical collection whose organisation is opaque to the user |
| **Here** | any answerer — human or agent — receiving one already-formulated question, deciding what it is a compromised rendering of |
| **What survives** | the ordering of the four levels; the claim that the received wording is compromised against the asker's *model* of the answerer rather than against the answerer; the five filters as the categories that must be recovered; the identification of over-specification with compromise |
| **What does not** | the [[recovery-postulate|recovery]] method. Taylor recovers levels by 60–90 minutes of dialogue with a librarian who knows the clientele personally (filter 3); an answerer with one turn and no client history has neither. Nor does the model supply a test for *which* level a given question sits at — it is a classification of the process, not an instrument on the utterance |

## Limits

- The claim in point 3 — that displaying capability changes the level received
  — rests on Taylor's single interview-derived observation about being
  "accepted as a colleague". It is not measured; no comparison of question
  levels across answerer presentations exists in the paper.
- Taylor states outright that quantitative data on the negotiation process is
  non-existent, and offers the model as a gross classification and a set of
  testable hypotheses. The evidence is open-ended unstructured taped
  interviews (60–90 minutes) with special librarians and information
  specialists, plus roughly twenty undergraduates in one Lehigh course of whom
  four searches are discussed. No coding scheme, no rater agreement.
- The model's force depends on the asker's model of the answerer being wrong.
  Where it is accurate — a documented API, a familiar collaborator — the
  compromise may be lossless, and Taylor gives no criterion separating the two
  cases.
- Read in the 2015 *College & Research Libraries* reprint (76(3):251–267);
  original 1968, 29(3):178–194. `taylor-1962` is cited for the origin of the
  four levels on the strength of Taylor's own 1968 restatement and the
  journal's abstract; the 1962 text was not read this pass.
