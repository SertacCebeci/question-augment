# Contrastive explanation

People do not explain an event per se. They explain it relative to an event that did not occur: "Why P rather than Q?", where P is the fact and Q the foil, even when Q is left implicit in the question.[^c1] Most authors surveyed argue that all why-questions are of this kind, and that people are good at inferring the foil from language and tone.[^c2]

## Mechanism

**The foil fixes which cause is relevant.** "Why did Elizabeth open the door?" has many foils (leave it closed, open the window, someone else opens it), and each has a different explanation. "Because she was hot" answers the first foil but not "rather than turning on the air conditioning", since heat explains both the fact and the foil.[^c3] An answer is therefore good only relative to the foil the asker holds.

**Contrast makes answers cheaper.** Lipton, as reported in the survey, argues that contrastive explanations are in general easier to derive than complete explanations for plain-fact questions.[^c4] A classifier's "beetle rather than spider" needs only the six legs, not eyes, wings or stingers; a non-contrastive "why spider" would need every cause (this example is the survey's reading of Lipton, not a measurement). The survey concludes that the cognitive burden of complete explanations is too great, so people request contrastive ones.[^c5]

**A taxonomy of questions.** Van Bouwel and Weber distinguish plain fact ("why does a have P") from three contrastive forms: P-contrast (P rather than Q on one object), O-contrast (a has P while b has Q) and T-contrast (P at time t, Q at time t').[^c6] They reject that plain-fact questions carry an implicit foil; they hold such questions are asked from curiosity, while contrastive ones follow unexpected events.[^c7] This is a dispute inside the source, not settled.

**Why the foil exists.** The survey ties explanation-seeking to abnormal or unexpected events: the observed event is the fact, the expected one is the foil. Lipton adds that a sensible contrast needs fact and foil with largely similar histories, so the differences stand out. This suggests the similarity of histories as a cue to the asker's true foil (the survey's inference, not tested there).

## Limits

The survey is a review; the contrastive thesis rests on cited philosophy and psychology, and the text itself notes disagreement over plain-fact questions. It gives no experiment on how well foils are recovered, and nothing on language models. The cheapness claim is illustrated by one toy classifier. It also separates "foil" from the counterfactual used in causal inference (a counterfactual to the cause versus to the outcome); the two should not be conflated.
