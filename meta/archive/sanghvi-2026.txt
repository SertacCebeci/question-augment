Abstract

Models are provided complete case descriptions,
often exam-style questions such as USMLE, and
evaluated on their ability to predict the correct
diagnosis (Jin et al., 2021; Singhal et al., 2023).
Although LLMs perform strongly on such benchmarks, in some cases matching or exceeding physicians (Wang et al., 2025b; Singhal et al., 2025), the
task lacks ecological validity.
Recent work (Tu et al., 2025; Chiu et al., 2026;
Almansoori et al., 2026) has begun exploring multiturn doctor-patient interactions using LLM agents
for disease diagnosis. However, several limitations remain. First, there is no standardized diagnostic benchmark that combines diverse clinical
cases with reliable diagnoses. Existing datasets are
fragmented across formats (e.g., clinical vignettes,
EHRs) and specialties (e.g., dermatology, cardiology), and mix diagnoses with unrelated tasks. Second, existing agentic approaches remain limited.
Open systems often use simplistic paradigm of a
single “doctor” agent that iteratively questions the
patient until it determines enough information has
been gathered for diagnosis (Chiu et al., 2026; Li
et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2024), whereas the sophisticated multi-agent systems are closed-sourced and
cannot be reproduced or compared (Tu et al., 2025;
Nori et al., 2025). Third, several setups leak diagnostic information and thereby weaken the task:
For example, some initialize the doctor with part
of the case (e.g., by passing the first sentence of the
patient’s case description) (Li et al., 2024), some
reduce diagnosis to multiple-choice selection (Li
et al., 2024; Jia et al., 2025; Rose et al., 2025),
some allow the patient agent to generate synthetic
information beyond the case description using the
ground truth diagnosis (Nori et al., 2025; Shang
et al., 2025; Rose et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a),
and some convert case descriptions into structured
fields that occasionally contain information revealing diagnosis (Almansoori et al., 2026). These
choices can artificially inflate accuracy. Fourth,

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for health-related decision support.
Yet most evaluations treat diagnosis as a singleshot task with complete information provided
upfront, often as a multiple-choice selection.
This diverges from clinical practice, where diagnosis is interactive and open-ended, involving sequential hypothesis refinement through
targeted questioning. We address this gap. We
build MeDxBench, a large-scale benchmark
of 4,421 clinical cases across 20 specialties.
We further propose MeDxAgent, a multi-agent
consultation system for interactive diagnosis,
and systematically study its prompt-, flowand agent-level design choices. MeDxAgent
achieves a 10.3% accuracy gain over the baseline on MeDxBench, closing 52.3% of the gap
to a full-information oracle. We find that specific design choices: collecting demographics
first, passing summarized dialogue for diagnosis, and feeding candidate diagnoses for targeted questioning, improve accuracy, mirroring how physicians reason, though their effect
emerges fully only in combination. Code and
dataset will be released upon publication.

Introduction

LLMs are increasingly used for real-world decision support, with healthcare emerging as one of
the most prominent application domains (CostaGomes et al., 2025, 2026). A substantial fraction
of LLM usage involves health-related queries, with
users relying on them to interpret symptoms and
determine next steps. However, LLMs largely operate in a single-shot setting, producing diagnoses
from incomplete symptom descriptions without iterative information gathering. In contrast, clinical
diagnosis is inherently interactive: physicians ask
targeted questions, iteratively refine hypotheses,
and perform differential diagnosis.
Despite this, most benchmarks for medical diagnosis evaluate LLMs in static task formulation.

We study this design space systematically. We
first construct MeDxBench, a large-scale diagnosis
benchmark of 4,421 clinical cases collected from
five public sources, spanning 20 specialties and
ranging from common to rare diseases. Our simulated patient agent answers only from the case
description, preventing diagnosis information leaks
during conversation. We then propose MeDxAgent,
a multi-agent consultation system for interactive diagnosis. We evaluate its prompt-, flow-, and agentlevel design choices independently and in combination, treating each choice as a hypothesis about
the capabilities required for effective diagnosis.
MeDxAgent improves diagnostic accuracy by
10.3% over the baseline on MeDxBench, and recovers 52.3% of the gap between the baseline and a
full-information oracle. Our analysis reveals that
three capabilities prove particularly important: collecting patient demographics first, summarizing
doctor-patient dialogue into a clinical problem representation, and feeding diagnostic reasoning to
guide subsequent questioning. Further, we reveal
two broader findings. First, the effectiveness of an
intervention depends strongly on when it is applied.
For example, we introduce differential questioning,
which directs the agent to ask questions that distinguish among competing diagnoses. Applying
this strategy from the second versus the tenth turn
produces an 18.1% variation in diagnostic accuracy.
Second, components that hurt performance in isola-

tion can become beneficial in combination. Three
of the four agents reduce accuracy individually,
but the full system integrating all four outperforms
every partial configuration. A standard incremental ablation study would have incorrectly rejected
these components.
Contributions.
• A large-scale medical diagnosis benchmark,
MeDxBench. We standardize 4,421 clinical
cases from five public datasets, spanning 20
specialties and a range of disease prevalence.
• A multi-agent consultation system, MeDxAgent. Our system gathers information through
targeted questions, summarizes clinical evidence, maintains competing hypotheses, and
commits to a diagnosis (similar to a physician). It recovers 52.3% of the gap between a
baseline agent and a full-information oracle,
and the gains transfer across model families.
• A systematic study of design choices for
interactive diagnosis. We evaluate prompt, flow-, and agent-based variations independently and in combination, showing that both
intervention timing and component interactions critically shape performance.

MeDxBench Benchmark Dataset

MeDxBench is a large-scale benchmark for medical diagnosis containing 4,421 cases spanning 20
clinical specialties (Table 6). Each case includes a
unique identifier, case description, source dataset,
specialty, and ground-truth diagnosis label(s).
Sources. We define a diagnostic case as a patient
case description paired with a ground-truth diagnosis. We build MeDxBench by aggregating and standardizing cases from five public datasets that meet
this criterion: CRAFT-MD (Johri et al., 2025) (200
cases), DiagnosisArena (Zhu et al., 2025) (897),
MedMCQA (Pal et al., 2022) (847), MedQA (Jin
et al., 2021) (1,498), and PubMed cases curated by
VivaBench (Chiu et al., 2026) (988).
Statistics. The dataset spans diverse case formats, difficulties, and specialties. Case formats include exam-style questions (MedQA, MedMCQA),
free-text clinical vignettes (CRAFT-MD), and peerreviewed case reports (DiagnosisArena, PubMed).
Its 4,113 unique disease labels comprise 655 common (high prevalence, > 1 in 100 globally), 1,098
uncommon (moderate prevalence, 1 in 100 to 1 in
2,000) and 2,360 rare (< 1 in 2,000) conditions.
It spans 20 specialties, with the largest proportions from Infectious Diseases (11.1%), Neurology
(9.7%) and Emergency Medicine (8.9%). Case
descriptions range from 212 to 9,848 characters
(mean 1,785 ± 1,955), and 546 cases have more
than one ground-truth diagnosis labels.
Construction. From the five datasets, we keep
only diagnostic cases with sufficient clinical detail. We remove cases focused on medical knowledge, treatment recommendations, or other nondiagnostic tasks. We keep only descriptions longer
than 200 characters. Because our work studies
text-only diagnosis, we exclude cases requiring image/video interpretation, while retaining those containing textual imaging reports. We also remove

Simulation Framework

Our framework simulates multi-turn doctor-patient
conversations using three agents: (i) a Patient agent
that answers from a case description, (ii) a Doctor agent that conducts the consultation and (iii) a
Judge agent that evaluates the final diagnosis. All
agents are implemented through prompt engineering; full prompts are provided in Appendix C. The
simplest configuration serves as our baseline. Its
Doctor agent comprise of two components: a Question agent that interacts with the patient and a Diagnosis agent that predicts candidate diagnoses after
each turn. Our full system, MeDxAgent, adds a
summarizer, specialist ensemble, knowledge-graph
module, and evidence-gap finder (Section 5).
At initialization, the Patient receives the case description and the Judge receives the ground-truth
diagnosis. The Doctor begins with a standard opening question (“What brings you in today?”), to
which the Patient responds with the primary concern. At each subsequent turn, the Doctor asks a
question conditioned on the conversation history,
and the Patient responds strictly from the case description, returning “I don’t know” if the requested
information is not present. After each turn, the Doctor outputs three candidate diagnoses, each with a
short rationale and a confidence score. The interaction continues until either a diagnosis reaches a
95% confidence threshold, selected on a 300-case
validation split, or a 20-turn limit is reached (following (Li et al., 2024)). In the latter case, the
highest-confidence diagnosis from the last turn is
selected. The final prediction is then evaluated by
the Judge. All experiments use temperature = 0.
Patient Agent. The Patient answers strictly
from the case description, without fabricating or
inferring unstated details, responding with “I don’t
know” otherwise. This prevents diagnosis leakage,
since the Patient has access only to the case description and not the ground-truth label. Cases are
typically written retrospectively (e.g., as clinical
notes), so we instruct the agent to respond as if
currently experiencing the condition, in the first
person, and in plain language.

MeDxAgent System

We study how design choices affect multi-turn
diagnosis. We treat each design choice as a hypothesis and group them by where they intervene:
(1) Prompt-based choices modify the instructions
provided to the Question agent, (2) Flow-based
choices change the interaction flow, and (3) Agentbased choices introduce additional agents for summarization, reasoning, or knowledge grounding.
We evaluate each choice incrementally(Table 2).
The Patient and Judge agents remain fixed across
all variants, ensuring that performance differences
arise only from the design choice under study. We
use clinician analogies to motivate these design
choices, not to claim physician-like reasoning.
5.1

Prompt-Based Variations

We evaluate two prompt modifications to the baseline, as well as their combination.
Turn Awareness. We provide the Question
agent with the remaining question budget and turnspecific guidance: early turns (1-5) focus on chief
complaints and high-yield screening questions,
4

5.3

middle turns (6-15) gather objective evidence and
differentiate symptoms, and late turns (16-20) prioritize the most discriminative questions. Because
early questions shape the utility of later follow-ups,
question ordering compounds over the consultation.
We test whether turn budget awareness helps the
agent plan and allocate its questions better.
Demographics First. We require the Question
agent to collect age and sex in the first turn. This
mirrors clinical practice, where demographic information helps anchor the differential diagnosis
(Chang et al., 1998). In the baseline, the agent is
encouraged, but not required, to ask about demographics. We test whether fixing demographics at
the start orients the diagnostic process by ruling out
age- and sex-dependent implausible candidates.
Combined Prompt. We combine both modifications; apply turn awareness at every step and
require demographic collection in the first turn.
5.2

Agent-Based Variations

Building on the best Flow variant, Differential
Questioning (Turn 10), we introduce four agents.
Summarizer. Instead of passing the raw conversation to the Diagnosis agent, a Summarizer
converts it into a structured clinical representation.
A physician distills an encounter into a concise
problem representation that organizes findings and
activates relevant diagnosis (Bowen, 2006). Additionally, LLMs are trained primarily on case-style
medical text (e.g., clinical notes, USMLE-style vignettes) rather than conversational transcripts, making summarized representations better aligned with
their training data. We test two formats: (a) a
free-form paragraph-style clinical narrative, and
(b) a structured SOAP-style schema (Wong et al.,
2025) with fields such as symptoms_present,
medical_history, and test_results.
Specialist Ensemble. We use an ensemble of
ten physician agents: nine specialists (Cardiology,
Pulmonology, Gastroenterology, Neurology, Endocrinology, Nephrology, Rheumatology, Haematology, and Dermatology) and one general physician. Each agent reviews the conversation and
predicts its top three diagnoses with confidence
scores and reasoning, producing a diverse candidate pool. A separate Selector-SE agent, designed
as a senior physician, selects the final top three diagnosis by weighting specialists according to their
relevance to the current case rather than by majority voting. This follows multidisciplinary clinical
practice, where diverse specialists contribute to a
single case. We test whether such diversity surfaces
hypotheses missed by a single generalist agent.
Knowledge Graph. Structured medical knowledge sources can surface relevant candidates and
improve the factual grounding of predictions (Gao
et al., 2025). We therefore ground candidate diagnoses generation in the Unified Biomedical Knowledge Graph (UBKG) (Stear et al., 2024). At each
turn, we query UBKG using symptoms revealed
by the Patient and aggregate the diseases it returns. Diseases are scored based on the number
of matched symptoms, and the top three are passed
to the Selector-KG agent. As UBKG does not incorporate clinical evidence such as test results, the
base Diagnosis agent also generates its own top
three predictions using the conversation history.

final top three based on consistency with the conversation. We test whether knowledge graph ground-

Flow-Based Variations

Building on the best prompt variant (Table 2), Combined Prompt, we study flow-based modifications.
No Early Stopping. The baseline ends a consultation once the Diagnosis agent reports ≥ 95% confidence. Here, we remove this and run all consultations for the full 20 turns, and select the highestconfidence diagnosis from the final turn. We test
whether gathering more evidence before committing improves diagnostic accuracy.
Differential Questioning. In the baseline, the
next question is generated independently of the
current diagnosis predictions. Here, the Question agent additionally receives the candidate diagnoses, confidence scores, and supporting reasoning,
and is instructed to ask questions that differentiate competing hypotheses. This is motivated by
hypothetico-deductive reasoning, in which physicians form hypotheses early and gather evidence to
confirm or reject them (Elstein et al., 1978). This
strategy has a known failure mode: conditioning
too early on weak hypotheses can lead to anchoring (Graber et al., 2005; Chiu et al., 2026). We
therefore activate differential questioning only after a fixed turn threshold, testing switch points at
turns 2, 5, and 10. Activating at turn 2 enables it
almost from the start, since the first turn collects
demographics; switching at later turns allow more
general evidence to accumulate before conditioning
future questions on diagnostic hypotheses.
Combined Flow. We combine no early stopping
with differential questioning activated at turn 10.

Evidence Gap. Clinicians gather information
selectively, directing questions toward the evidence
that would most change the differential diagnosis (Bowen, 2006). The Evidence Gap agent follows this principle, identifying information still
needed to support or reject a diagnosis. It identifies: (i) diagnosis-specific gaps that help differentiate current candidate diagnoses, and (ii) general
gaps covering broadly informative clinical details
(such as vitals, lab results). The agent outputs up to
three gaps per type, expressed as short, actionable
statements, ranked by estimated diagnostic utility.
Before turn 10, when candidate diagnoses remain
unused by the Question agent, it reports only general gaps using the dialog history. From turn 10,
when Differential Questioning is active, it also receives the top three candidates and reports both gap
types. The Question agent is prompted to prioritize these gaps when forming the next query. We
test whether explicitly modeling missing evidence
improves information gathering.
Combined Agents: MeDxAgent. It integrates
all four agents (Figure 1). The Summarizer runs
at every turn, converting dialogue into a structured
clinical representation. During early turns (1-9),
the Question agent gathers information broadly,
guided only by general evidence gaps. From turn
10, the system enters a differential diagnosis phase.
The Knowledge Graph retrieves symptom-matched
diseases, the Specialist Ensemble proposes additional specialty-specific hypotheses, and the Selector consolidates these candidates into a final top
three. The Evidence Gap agent now reports both
general and diagnosis-specific gaps, enabling the
Question agent to ask question based on current
hypotheses and missing evidence.

Discussion

What worked and why. Three design choices
account for most of the gains, and each aligns
with established principles of clinical reasoning.
First, collecting demographics first orient the differential diagnosis because age and sex strongly
shape disease priors. For instance, in 39 pediatric
cases, demographics were the key differentiating
factor in diagnosis: the baseline defaulted to adult
presentations and missed age-specific conditions,
whereas the demographics-first variant correctly
7

identified them. This matches prior reports of demographic biases in medical LLMs (Rawat et al.,
2024) and shows a single-line prompt intervention
can help mitigate them in an agentic system. Second, Differential Questioning helps, but only after
sufficiently strong hypotheses have formed. This
mirrors hypothetico-deductive pattern of clinical
reasoning, in which physicians form hypotheses
and gather evidence to confirm or rule them out (Elstein et al., 1978), and its associated failure mode,
anchoring, where weak early hypotheses bias subsequent reasoning (Graber et al., 2005). Our experiments show that the same instruction and interaction flow can improve or reduce performance
depending solely on when it is activated. Third, the
Summarizer improves accuracy by changing how
evidence is represented rather than what evidence
is available. Clinicians refer to this as problem
representation, and prior work links higher-quality
representations to more accurate diagnosis (Bowen,
2006). Also, summarization may help because
structured clinical summaries more closely resemble the case-style medical text on which LLMs are
predominantly trained.
Why three agents fail in isolation. The remaining three agents lower accuracy individually, each
for a different reason. The Specialist Ensemble
and Knowledge Graph (KG) variants are prone to
premature termination (Table 7). In the Specialist
Ensemble, repeated predictions across specialists
inflate aggregate confidence during ensembling. In
the KG variant, the Selector-KG overweights KGderived candidates. These causes early stopping
before sufficient evidence is gathered, reducing accuracy. The Evidence Gap agent appears to fail
due to confirmation bias. Although it extracts more
information, as reflected by a sharp reduction in “I
don’t know” answers relative to the baseline (Figure 5), it tends to direct questions toward confirming the leading hypothesis. When that hypothesis
is incorrect, the system gathers increasingly detailed evidence for the wrong disease, lowering
performance. Despite this, each agent contributes
positively in MeDxAgent. Leave-one-out ablations
(Table 5) show that removing any agent from the
full system lowers accuracy. We do not claim that
the combined system fully resolves these issues;
however, we provide empirical evidence for why
the combined setup leads to improved accuracy.
Components work in combination. The strong
compositional effects arise because the modules
are designed to operate jointly. The Summarizer

produces a structured representation, which helps
the Knowledge Graph and Specialist Ensemble generate ‘diverse’ diagnostic candidates. These candidates enable Evidence Gap to identify missing
evidence more reliably, which in turn helps the
Question agent to ask more targeted questions and
reduce "I don’t know" responses. As discussed,
individually, the Knowledge Graph and Specialist Ensemble tend to be overconfident, leading to
premature termination. When combined, however,
their predictions are balanced, reflected in lower
early stopping rates (Table 7). Similarly, the Evidence Gap agent alone is prone to confirmation
bias, but benefits from the more diverse candidate
set produced by the combined system, reducing
anchoring effects. Overall, each agent in isolation
perturbs a system not built to absorb it, often reducing accuracy. Together, the agents become complementary, and removing any one lowers accuracy.
Prior multi-agent medical systems typically report
aggregate gains over single-agent baselines without
isolating individual agent contributions (Nori et al.,
2025; Kim et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024), leaving
such compositional effects unexplored. We highlight this phenomenon empirically, though we do
not claim it generalizes to all multi-agent systems.
Real-world deployment. Despite promising
performance, MeDxAgent should be viewed strictly
as a decision-support tool for clinicians, not a replacement for medical professionals. It is not suitable for autonomous use by non-medical personnel. Incorrect or overconfident diagnoses could delay treatment, trigger unnecessary interventions, or
cause serious patient harm, particularly in high-risk
clinical settings. Moreover, conversational diagnostic systems may inherit biases (e.g., demographyrelated) and reasoning failures from their underlying LLMs. In practice, such systems may be most
appropriate for assisting physicians with differential diagnosis, surfacing alternative hypotheses, or
double-checking clinical decisions.

8

Conclusion

We present MeDxBench, a benchmark of 4,421 clinical cases across 20 specialities for open-ended interactive diagnosis and MeDxAgent, a multi-agent
system built from a systematic study of prompt-,
flow- and agent-level design choices. Beyond these,
our contribution is methodological: we isolate the
impact of individual design decisions and reveal
interaction effects.

First, our evaluation is limited to text-only diagnosis, while real-world clinical diagnosis often depends on multimodal evidence such as imaging.
Second, the Patient agent answers strictly from the
case description, which prevents diagnosis leakage,
but reduces the natural variability and ecological validity of real patient interactions, as patients rarely
respond with repeated "I don’t know" responses.
Finally, although we systematically study several
design choices, a broader sweep over prompts, interaction flows, and agent configurations would
strengthen the empirical conclusions; though such
sweeps are computationally expensive in multi-turn
agentic settings. Future work can address these limitations and evaluate whether the design choices
that help in simulation also transfer to supervised
clinical use, with multimodal inputs, and learned
questioning policies beyond prompt engineering.

Accuracy of the Patient Agent

We evaluate the Patient agent using an LLM-asa-judge over all responses generated by MeDxAgent, totaling 84,739 question-answer pairs across
4,421 cases. The Patient agent achieves an overall
response accuracy of 97.2%. Among the 2,387 inaccurate responses (2.8%), we identify four error
types: withholding (1,513 responses; 63.4% of errors), where the Patient omits information present
in the case description, typically failing to mention
a detail when asked a broad question; fabrication
(457; 19.1%), where the Patient introduces clinical
details not present in the case; contradiction (368;
15.4%), where the response conflicts with information in the case; and oversharing (48; 2.0%), where
the Patient provides information not solicited by
the doctor’s question. Overall, these results suggest
that the Patient agent remains highly faithful to the
underlying case descriptions.

Evidence Gap Agent

The Evidence Gap agent identifies clinically informative missing evidence from the current case state.
In early turns, it highlights general missing information (e.g., vitals, labs, history details), while
after differential questioning begins, it additionally
identifies diagnosis-specific gaps that would help
distinguish among the current top candidate diagnoses. These gaps are then used by the EvidenceGuided question agents (EG-QA and EG-DQA,
Section C.2) to guide subsequent question selection.
