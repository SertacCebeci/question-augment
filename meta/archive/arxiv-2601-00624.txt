Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini increasingly act as generalpurpose copilots, yet they often respond with unnecessary length on simple requests adding redundant explanations, hedging, or boilerplate that increases cognitive load and inflates tokenbased inference cost. Prior work suggests that preference-based post-training and LLM-judged
evaluations can induce systematic length bias, where longer answers are rewarded even at comparable quality.
We introduce YapBench, a lightweight benchmark for quantifying user-visible over-generation
on brevity-ideal prompts. Each item consists of a single-turn prompt, a curated minimal sufficient
baseline answer, and a category label. Our primary metric, YapScore, measures excess response
length beyond the baseline, in characters, so results are comparable across models without relying
on any specific tokenizer. Further, we summarize the YapScores of each model via the YapIndex,
a uniform-weighted average of category-level median YapScores.
YapBench contains over three hundred English prompts spanning three common brevity-ideal
settings: (A) minimal/ambiguous inputs where the ideal behavior is a short clarification, (B)
closed-form factual questions with short stable answers, and (C) one-line coding tasks where a
single command/snippet suffices. Evaluating 76 assistant LLM models, we observe an order-ofmagnitude spread in median excess length, and distinct category-specific failure modes (vacuumfilling on ambiguous inputs and explanation/formatting overhead on one-line technical requests).
We release the benchmark and maintain a live leaderboard for tracking verbosity behavior over
time.

Introduction

Preference-based post-training (e.g., Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang
et al., 2022) and related alignment pipelines) is now routine in training assistant large language models (LLMs) (Bommasani, 2021). A recurring concern is that these methods can induce length bias:
longer outputs are systematically favored, even when additional text does not improve correctness
or helpfulness. Evidence for this effect appears in reward-model and LLM-judge preferences (Saito
et al., 2023), in observations that Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) can increase response length
(Rafailov et al., 2023; Ivison et al., 2023), and in findings that models sometimes produce extra text
as a response to uncertainty (Zhang et al., 2025). This motivates the following evaluation question:
Research question: On brevity-ideal prompts, how strong is length bias in assistant LLMs,
measured as verbosity beyond a minimal sufficient answer?
Or in plain English: when a short answer is sufficient, how much additional text do assistant
LLMs generate beyond a minimal sufficient response?
This question also captures a recurring phenomenon in assistant-style interactions: models often
add text beyond what is required for correctness or sufficiency in simple requests. This extra content
can take the form of prompt restatement, generic caveats, or boilerplate preambles (e.g., ‘‘Great
question!’’ and ‘‘As an AI language model’’). This avoidable verbosity carries at least three
concrete costs:
• User experience cost. Extra tokens translate into extra reading and scrolling, which can
reduce satisfaction and trust—especially on simple tasks where the desired response is short and
direct. Empirically, users often prefer short answers for simple tasks (Juneja et al., 2024), and
lower cognitive load in chatbot interactions is associated with more positive user attitudes and
stronger intentions to continue using the system (Chou and Hsu, 2021).
• Energy and environmental cost. Inference cost in LLMs scales roughly linearly with the
number of generated tokens, so unnecessary verbosity directly increases energy use. Recent
measurements estimate per-token inference energy on the order of joules, implying that largescale overgeneration can produce measurable electricity consumption and associated emissions
(Samsi et al., 2023). At current estimates, generating on the order of tens of unnecessary tokens
can waste energy comparable to keeping an LED light bulb on for a few seconds negligible once,
but material when repeated at scale (Jegham et al., 2025).
• Economic cost. Commercial LLM APIs are typically priced per token, so even modest verbosity multipliers increase per-request cost and can materially raise operating expenses in highthroughput applications. For instance, OpenAI reported that users showing gratitude and being
nice to ChatGPT by adding ”Please” and ”Thank you” to their prompts, incurred costs in the
range of 10s of millions of dollars (Caramela, 2025). The same logic applies to the verbosity of
an LLM’s answer.
Despite these costs, most existing evaluation suites emphasize accuracy, robustness, and safety,
with limited attention to verbosity as a first-class outcome. At the same time, human-factors evidence
is increasingly clear that more text is not always better: people prefer simple explanations for simple
events and more complex explanations only when the underlying phenomenon demands it (Juneja et al.,
2024). For straightforward information-seeking interactions, recent studies similarly point toward a
preference for direct, concise responses over drawn-out dialogue (Chou and Hsu, 2021).
In this paper, we introduce YapBench, a lightweight benchmark and metric suite designed to quantify verbosity specifically in settings where brevity is broadly preferred. The core ideas are:
• We curate prompts that are brevity-ideal by construction: each can be satisfied with a short,
self-contained minimal sufficient answer.
• For each prompt, we define a minimal sufficient answer : the shortest response that raters still
judge as correct, clear, and sufficient.
• We define a primary measure, YapScore, which measures how much longer a model’s output is
than the minimal sufficient answer while remaining easy to interpret.

YapBench measures a specific but practically important component of assistant quality: producing the minimal sufficient information without redundant text on brevity-ideal prompts. While it
does not cover all aspects of assistant performance, it can serve as a proxy for utility in workflows
where concision, latency, and cost are first-order objectives. Concretely, we contribute YapBench

diagnostics and an empirical evaluation across a broad set of assistant LLMs with a maintained live
leaderboard.

Related Work

Prior work has documented multiple mechanisms and manifestations of verbosity/length bias in assistant LLMs, as well as mitigation strategies for length-sensitive evaluation.
Verbosity measurement and correlates. Zhang et al. (2025) introduce the verbosity compensation (VC) score, showing that models often answer with more words than necessary even when
explicitly instructed to be concise. They demonstrate that VC is pervasive across models and datasets
(e.g., GPT-4 shows ≈50% VC frequency on some tasks). They also observe that verbose answers are
often less accurate, with large performance gaps between concise and verbose settings, and report that
verbosity correlates with uncertainty signals such as higher perplexity and related uncertainty metrics.
Length bias in preference learning and LLM-judged evaluation. Shen et al. (2023) investigate length bias in RLHF reward models, showing that vanilla reward models can learn a shortcut
where longer responses receive systematically higher scores, even when additional text does not improve helpfulness. Relatedly, when GPT-4 is used as a proxy judge, it tends to favor longer answers
over shorter ones at comparable quality (Saito et al., 2023; Dubois et al., 2024), inducing a lengthcorrelated bias that advantages verbose models in win-rate–based evaluations. To address this, Dubois
et al. (2024) introduce a length-controlled win rate that removes the length term from the preference
regression, improving robustness to verbosity manipulation and increasing correlation with LMSYS
Chatbot Arena rankings (Chiang et al., 2024). Hu et al. (2024) further analyze length bias in LLM-asa-judge settings by decomposing win rate into length-independent desirability and length-dependent
information mass (quantified via conditional entropy), and argue that longer responses gain higher
win rates primarily through increased information mass. Building on this view, they introduce AdapAlpaca, a variant of AlpacaEval that dynamically matches the length of reference and test responses
to mitigate length bias in automatic preference evaluation.
Over-reasoning as a failure mode of over-generation. Chiang and Lee (Chiang and Lee,
2024) study over-reasoning in LLMs using GSM8K-Zero, where questions can be answered directly
from the prompt text without any calculations. They show that RLHF-trained models nonetheless
produce lengthy, unnecessary chains of calculation on these trivial problems, often introducing errors
and lowering accuracy compared to short, direct answers. Using GPT-4 as proxy reward models, they
further find that these judges systematically prefer long answers even when the long answer is wrong

of redundant reasoning.
Reference-free conciseness metrics. Ghafari et al. (2025) show that conciseness is positively
correlated with human judgments of redundancy and verbosity across several tasks. They propose
ConCISE, a reference-free conciseness metric that estimates redundancy by generating compressed
variants of an answer (abstractive, extractive, and pruned) and using an LLM judge to verify semantic
and entity preservation. However, this approach requires multiple additional LLM calls, is sensitive
to the choice of helper model and prompts, and treats the original answer as the reference rather
than anchoring conciseness to task-specific minimal sufficiency or distinguishing brevity-focused from
explanation-heavy settings.

3

YapBench

YapBench is a benchmark for measuring user-visible over-generation on brevity-ideal prompts. Each
benchmark item is a triple (pi , bi , ci ): a single-turn prompt pi , a short reference baseline bi , and a
category label ci . The baseline is the minimal sufficient answer : the shortest response that is still
correct, clear, and sufficient for a typical user. The YapBench benchmark asks a question: when
a short answer is enough, how much longer does the model answer than this baseline?
Informally, YapBench compares assistant LLMs on how much they over-generate (”yap”) beyond
a prompt-specific minimal sufficient answer on prompts where brevity is ideal.
Formally, YapBench is designed to measure unnecessary verbosity in settings where a short answer
is clearly sufficient. The benchmark is built around three goals:
1. Isolation of verbosity. Evaluate verbosity separately from other axes (e.g., correctness, safety),
rather than collapsing them into a single scalar.
2. Brevity-ideal scope. Focus core metrics on prompts where annotators broadly agree that
short, direct answers are preferred.
3. Practical simplicity. Enable evaluation with standard APIs and offline scoring from logged
model outputs.

3.1

Prompt set and categories

We group prompts into a small number of categories to keep analysis interpretable. Different models
“yap” for different reasons (e.g., filling the vacuum on nonsense, or adding explanations around a
one-line command). For the initial version of the benchmark we selected three prompt categories:
Prompt Category A: minimal / ambiguous input. Category A is designed to stress-test assistants on low-information turns, where the safest and most useful behavior is to ask for clarification (or
give a minimal acknowledgment), not to guess intent. Concretely, the category includes (i) pure noise
inputs with no semantic content (empty/whitespace-only, punctuation spam, keyboard mashes, emojionly), and (ii) underspecified openers that signal a problem but provide no details (e.g., help, error,
fix this, how?, it doesn’t work). We also include short phatic tokens (e.g., ok, cool, thanks)
and bare digits (e.g., 1, 42) as ambiguous turns that commonly trigger over-interpretation. Baselines
therefore use minimal templates: a short clarification request (e.g., Please rephrase., What do you need
help with? ) or a minimal acknowledgment (e.g., OK., You’re welcome.). The goal of Category A
is to measure how much models over-generate when the prompt is unclear: do they stay
minimal, or do they produce extended, unsolicited content despite insufficient context?
Prompt Category B: short factual Q&A (closed-form). Category B targets prompts where
the user is clearly asking for a single fact and a one-token (or very short) answer is sufficient. The
prompts are intentionally closed-form: capitals, simple arithmetic, basic geometry, chemical symbols
and formulas, atomic numbers, common biological facts, and canonical authorship/date questions.
We also include a small number of “confusion-prone” but still unambiguous items (e.g., What is the
capital of Australia? ) to capture a frequent assistant behavior: adding disclaimers or extra context
around a straightforward fact. The goal of Category B is to measure over-generation in the

3.2

Minimal sufficient baselines

For each prompt pi , we define a baseline bi that represents the minimal sufficient response. Baselines
are intentionally short: they are not “best possible” answers, only the minimum needed to satisfy the
prompt.
Baseline rules (operational).

Each (pi , bi ) pair is curated to satisfy:

• Unambiguous intent. The prompt targets one task and one correct answer in standard usage
(up to trivial surface variants).
• Minimal sufficiency. The baseline is as short as possible while still correct and clear; removing
any token should break correctness or clarity.
• Atomic and self-contained. No reliance on prior turns or hidden context.
• Standard practice. When multiple short correct answers exist, choose the idiomatic/common
one (especially in code/commands).
• Reproducible. Items that produce disagreement about “minimal sufficient” are revised or
removed.

• A: a minimal clarification (e.g., Please rephrase., What do you need help with? ) or a minimal
acknowledgment for phatic inputs (e.g., OK., You’re welcome.). This is why Category A includes
multiple baseline types.
• B: a single token or short phrase (e.g., Paris, 12, H2 O, Yes).
• C: exactly one line of code, command, or pattern, with no additional symbols (e.g., Markdown
formatting).
Baseline construction and review. Baselines are written to be minimal rather than exhaustive:
the shortest answer that remains correct, clear, and sufficient for a typical user. We iteratively refined
(pi , bi ) pairs using internal review: for each prompt, we edited the baseline down to the shortest form
that still preserves correctness and clarity, and removed or rewrote prompts where “minimal sufficient”
produced repeated disagreements. This process emphasizes reproducibility: if a prompt does not admit
a stable minimal answer under standard usage, it is excluded or corrected in the future versions of the
benchamrk.

3.3

Measures: YapScore, YapIndex, and YapTax

In this subsection, we define quantitative measures for unnecessary verbosity on brevity-ideal prompts.
The key idea is to measure how much extra text a model produces beyond a prompt-specific minimal
sufficient answer.
Definition 1 (Per-prompt YapScore (Excess Length)). Let i ∈ I index a prompt with baseline answer
length Bi ∈ N, measured in characters. For a model M , let Li (M ) ∈ N denote the character length of
its response to prompt i. The per-prompt YapScore is defined as
YapScorei (M ) = max{0, Li (M ) − Bi }.

(1)

Interpretation. YapScorei (M ) measures the number of extra characters generated by model M
beyond the minimal sufficient answer for prompt i. A score of zero indicates a baseline-length response.
Larger values indicate increasing amounts of unnecessary verbosity. By construction, this metric is
robust to extremely short baselines and requires no smoothing or tuning parameters.
Definition 2 (Category and Aggregate YapIndex). Let Ic ⊆ I denote the set of prompts belonging to
category c ∈ C, where C is the set of benchmark categories (e.g., C = {A, B, C}). The category-level
YapIndex is defined as
Yc (M ) = median{YapScorei (M ) : i ∈ Ic }.
(2)

Evaluation Protocol and Parameters

Access, prompting, and decoding. All models are queried via the OpenRouter API with absent
system prompt. We set temperature to 0 whenever the model endpoint exposes this control; for endpoints that do not allow temperature configuration (e.g., GPT-5 variants), we use the provider default,
the rest of hyperparameters are default to each provider. All prompts are evaluated independently in
a single-turn setting with no prior context.
Length units. The primary YapScore is computed in characters to enable tokenizer-agnostic comparison across providers. We also neglect the markdown characters from the YapScore calculations.
Correctness and sufficiency. YapBench v0.1 is intentionally constructed so that Category B
(closed-form factual items) and Category C (atomic one-line commands/snippets) are trivial to answer correctly for essentially any instruction-tuned model. Consequently, we do not apply an explicit
correctness gate in the main results; the benchmark is designed to isolate over-generation behavior
rather than task difficulty. Category A items are likewise evaluated primarily for verbosity behavior
on underspecified inputs, where the desired behavior is a minimal clarification or acknowledgment
rather than substantive content.
Reasoning-model ablations. For models that expose explicit “reasoning” modes or verbosity controls, we run paired evaluations with these features enabled and disabled to measure their impact on
YapScore and category-level behavior. Importantly, YapScore is computed only from the user-visible
final answer : we exclude any hidden or separately reported reasoning traces (e.g., chain-of-thought
tokens) from length and token counts whenever the API provides them separately.

5.3

Results: Category-Level Behavior

Category medians reveal distinct verbosity failure modes:
Category A (underspecified / ambiguous inputs). Category A can be a major source of
excess length: several models generate large median overhead on low-information prompts, consistent with a tendency to “fill the vacuum” with unsolicited content rather than issuing a minimal
clarification.
Category B (short factual Q&A). Median excess in Category B is low for some models but
dominates for others, especially on confusion-prone yet closed-form questions, indicating that many
assistants add avoidable context or caveats even when a single fact is sufficient.
Category C (one-line code tasks). Category C frequently drives the largest median excess
for many models, reflecting formatting and explanation overhead (e.g., headings, prose, or duplicated
code blocks) around atomic commands or snippets where a one-line answer would suffice.
Lastly, while YapIndex summarizes typical behavior via medians, over-generation can be highly
variable across prompts; we therefore complement median-based reporting with tail statistics (e.g.,
percentiles) to characterize worst-case “verbosity bursts” that are most disruptive for user experience
and cost.

Discussion

Brevity-optimal behavior is not aligned with model recency or perceived capability. A
central finding of YapBench is that response verbosity on brevity-ideal prompts is not monotonic in
model generation or perceived frontier capability. In particular, a model from 2023 gpt-3.5-turbo
achieves the best overall YapIndex (23) in our evaluation (Table 4), outperforming a range
of newer frontier assistant models (Gemini-Pro, GPT-5, etc). This result suggests that unnecessary
verbosity is not an inherent consequence of scale or general competence. Instead, it appears to be
a behavioral property that is influenced by post-training and assistant-style response priors. From
a deployment perspective, this decoupling implies that selecting models solely based on conventional
capability benchmarks can yield suboptimal user experience and higher inference cost in brevity-ideal
settings.
Verbosity is heterogeneous across interaction regimes, motivating category-level analysis. YapBench was designed to separate distinct regimes where brevity is typically preferred, and
the results confirm that models exhibit markedly different behavior across categories. The category
leaderboards (Table 5) show that some models can be near-minimal on closed-form factual queries
(Category B), while simultaneously producing substantial over-generation on underspecified inputs
(Category A) or one-line technical coding requests (Category C). This heterogeneity indicates that a
single global verbosity score is insufficient to characterize assistant behavior in practice. Reporting
category-level outcomes is therefore essential to identify which interaction types drive excess length
and to diagnose failure modes that are likely to be product-critical.
Underspecified inputs bring out systematic over-generation that is better explained
as a policy prior than a knowledge limitation. Category A prompts contain low-information
turns for which the minimal sufficient behavior is a short clarification request or acknowledgment.
Despite the absence of actionable task information, many models generate extended content, which
we interpret as a systematic tendency to produce proactive, generic assistance rather than requesting
missing context. Because Category A baselines are intentionally minimal, excess length in this regime
directly quantifies an assistant prior to respond substantively even when the prompt does not support
it. This behavior is particularly salient from a human factors and cost perspective, since so-called
”vacuum-filling” responses can become long while providing limited incremental utility.

One-line technical requests expose formatting and explanation overhead as a dominant
source of excess length. Category C isolates cases where the user request admits an atomic solution.
The results suggest that over-generation in this regime often arises from presentation and pedagogy
defaults rather than task requirements. Common patterns include multi-line formatting around a
one-line command, redundant restatement of the request, and additional explanatory text that is not

output policy that can likely be reduced through targeted post-training objectives or inference-time
constraints.
Closed-form factual prompts still trigger avoidable text, consistent with length-favoring
preference mechanisms. Although Category B is constructed so that short answers are sufficient,
many models still add context, qualifiers, or restatements. The category-level top performers demonstrate that minimal behavior is attainable, yet the broader spread in Category B suggests that some
assistants adopt a default of providing additional narrative even when the prompt is unambiguous.
This pattern is consistent with prior evidence that preference modeling and LLM-judged evaluation
can reward responses that appear more thorough, even when additional content does not improve correctness or sufficiency. YapBench provides a direct measurement of this effect under conditions where
brevity is normatively preferred.
Reasoning modes do not uniformly increase typical verbosity, but they plausibly increase worst-case over-generation. A common expectation is that explicit reasoning modes necessarily induce longer outputs. Our category-level results indicate a more nuanced picture: several
reasoning variants remain competitive on median excess length in specific categories (Table 5).

7

Future Work and Limitations

Future work. We plan to add auxiliary prompt sets that isolate other common sources of undesirable
length, while keeping the core benchmark brevity-ideal:
• Safety / Refusal. Harmful or disallowed requests where the desired response is a short, firm
11

refusal (avoiding pages of boilerplate).
• Misconception traps. Prompts that invite agreement with a falsehood (“X is true, right?”)
where the desired response is a brief correction.
• Legitimate verbosity (control). Tasks where detail is genuinely helpful (e.g., step-by-step
instructions, teaching, drafting a careful message), used as a control to ensure the benchmark
does not reward pathological brevity.
Limitations. Baseline selection is inherently a source of variance: there is no universal “gold”
standard for response length. YapBench mitigates this via criteria and annotator agreement, but
residual subjectivity remains. In addition, some prompts can become non-stationary over time (e.g.,
facts that change) and must be periodically audited. Finally, very short or empty inputs are handled
differently across providers; we therefore recommend using explicit blank-input proxies (e.g., <EMPTY>)
for Category A to avoid benchmarking API preprocessing rather than model behavior.

8

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced YapBench v0.1, a lightweight benchmark for measuring user-visible overgeneration on brevity-ideal prompts using curated minimal sufficient baselines. To quantify verbosity
in a simple and interpretable way, we defined three complementary measures: YapScore, a per-prompt
excess-length metric in characters; YapIndex, a category-balanced aggregate based on category-level
medians; and YapTax, a cost-oriented estimate of the marginal token-priced overhead attributable to
over-generation. Across 76 assistant models, we find substantial and structured differences in verbosity,
with distinct category-level failure modes and large variation even among highly capable systems.
We release YapBench and maintain a live leaderboard to support ongoing tracking of verbosity
behavior over time and to encourage training and evaluation methods that optimize for minimal
sufficient responses when brevity is ideal.
