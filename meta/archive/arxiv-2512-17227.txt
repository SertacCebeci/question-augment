Abstract

1. Introduction
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) represent
a significant step toward artificial general intelligence,
enabling systems to jointly process and reason over visual and textual modalities in complex, real-world scenarios [10, 15, 25, 32, 33]. The remarkable success of Large
Language Models (LLMs) in reasoning tasks, demonstrated
through chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting [17, 39] and
more recently through reinforcement learning with veifiable rewards (RLVR) approaches [16, 30], has naturally
motivated the extension of reasoning capabilities to the multimodal domain.
Despite recent progress, MLLMs exhibit notable weaknesses in long-chain visual reasoning tasks [22, 53]. A critical issue arises in the form of the shortcut phenomenon:
models frequently bypass visual analysis, relying instead
on linguistic priors to infer answers [40, 41]. This leads to
pronounced visual forgetting, succinctly captured by the
notion “think longer, see less” [31, 34, 35]. As reasoning
chains lengthen, models increasingly lose visual grounding, ultimately defaulting to degenerate solutions. We argue
that this brittleness arises from a failure in current training
paradigms to disentangle two essential cognitive skills: (1)
abstract logical reasoning (“how-to-think”) and (2) strategic
visual perception (“when-to-look”). Standard reinforcement
learning frameworks optimize for answer accuracy but do
not explicitly penalize visual disengagement. As a result,
models tend to converge to solutions where minimal visual
processing is sufficient to satisfy the reward signal.
To mitigate these issues, several approaches have been
proposed. Global captioning methods, such as VisionaryR1 [40] and Perception-R1 [41], prepend image descriptions, providing superficial context but failing to enforce
fine-grained reasoning Reflection-based approaches [9, 31,
35, 43] interleave implicit re-focusing steps, but these operate post-hoc and cannot prevent initial visual disengagement.

significant potential but remain brittle in complex, longchain visual reasoning tasks. A critical failure mode is
“visual forgetting”, where models progressively lose visual
grounding as reasoning extends, a phenomenon aptly described as “think longer, see less”. We posit this failure
stems from current training paradigms prematurely entangling two distinct cognitive skills: (1) abstract logical reasoning (“how-to-think”) and (2) strategic visual perception

deficiency–—weakening abstract reasoning–—and a strategic perception deficit, as models lack a policy for when to

framework to disentangle these skills. First, we introduce a
disentangled Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) curriculum that
builds a robust abstract reasoning backbone on text-only

timing as a reinforcement learning problem. We design a
Pivotal Perception Reward that teaches the model when to
look by coupling perceptual actions to linguistic markers
of cognitive uncertainty (e.g., “wait”, “verify”), thereby
learning an autonomous grounding policy. Our contributions include the formalization of these two deficiencies
and the development of a principled, two-stage framework
to address them, transforming the model from a heuristicdriven observer to a strategic, grounded reasoner. Code:

However, these strategies share two fundamental limitations. First, they prematurely entangle the training of
abstract reasoning and visual grounding, which we term
the foundational cold-start deficiency. Multimodal supervised fine-tuning (SFT) often uses data that is “easier than
text-only sets”, resulting in MLLMs with weaker textual reasoning than their LLM-only counterparts. By forcing models
to learn “how-to-think” and “what-to-see” simultaneously,
these approaches fail to establish a robust abstract “System 2”
backbone for reasoning. This deficiency, rooted in the SFT
stage, creates a cold-start problem that subsequent RL cannot
fully overcome. Prior work shows that RL can only refine
or compose behaviors already latent in the base model’s
distribution [13, 48]. For example, Gandhi et al. [13] identified key cognitive behaviors in text-only settings—such
as sub-goal setting, backtracking, and verification—that are
prerequisites for RL-driven self-improvement. Models lacking these behaviors must first be bootstrapped via SFT on
curated reasoning traces.
Second, existing methods do not learn when to look;
instead, they impose perception through fixed heuristics,
such as global captions at the outset [40, 41] or visual anchors at random steps [28]. This creates a fundamental
limitation: the model learns to perceive but not when to
perceive. Constrained to imitate predetermined grounding
patterns, it cannot adapt to its own emerging cognitive state
or question-specific demands. The result is either inefficient over-perception or critical under-perception, with no
mechanism to acquire strategic timing.

In this paper, we propose a novel curriculum-based framework that disentangles the learning of these two skills. To
remedy the foundational cold-start deficiency, we introduce a
disentangled SFT curriculum. First, we conduct text-only
SFT on high-difficulty reasoning data, forcing the model to
acquire robust abstract logical templates and cognitive behaviors without visual confounding. We then anchor vision
through a novel Perception-Grounded Chain-of-Thought
(PG-CoT) paradigm, where a teacher model annotates finegrained, step-relevant perception segments into existing reasoning traces. This two-phase curriculum builds a strong
textual reasoning backbone first, then teaches it to bind to
visual evidence, establishing the robust antecedent skills
necessary for strategic self-improvement.
To resolve the strategic perception deficit, we formulate perception timing as a reinforcement learning problem. While SFT teaches the structure of grounded reasoning,
RL learns when to invoke it. Our key innovation is a Pivotal Perception Reward that evaluates perception placement
against linguistic markers of cognitive uncertainty. We operationalize the hypothesis that strategic perception should
be triggered by metacognitive signals (e.g., “wait, I was
mistaken”), structural transitions (e.g., “however”), and explicit re-examination cues (e.g., “verify this”). Our reward
analyzes perception actions at the sentence level, measuring
their coupling to these pivotal keywords while regularizing against heuristic overfitting. This compels the model to
develop a nuanced, context-aware policy that binds visual
grounding to genuine cognitive uncertainty.

2. Related Works
Multimodal Reasoning. Deepseek [16] introduced the
GRPO training approach to equip large language models
with chain-of-thought reasoning abilities, enabling them to
think before answering. Building on this, many subsequent
works have focused on enhancing GRPO and applying it to
MLLMs to improve their reasoning skills. VL-rethinker [36]
proposes Selective Sample Replay to address the diminishing
advantages of GRPO, and Forced Rethinking to encourage
self-reflection in vision-language models during RL training. WE-MATH 2.0 [27] enhances MLLMs’ visual mathematical reasoning through a five-level knowledge system,
3D difficulty-modeled datasets, and a two-stage progressive RL framework with dynamic scheduling. ThinkLiteVL [38] first collects 70k samples, uses Monte Carlo Tree
Search (MCTS) to select challenging ones via iterative reasoning, and then applies Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT)
on the filtered 11k samples without knowledge distillation.
ARES [7] leverages window entropy as exploration triggers
and difficulty-aware rewards to dynamically adjust reasoning effort for multimodal large reasoning models via adaptive training. However, most RL-based MLLM research
has mainly focused on boosting models’ textual reasoning
abilities, while the enhancement of perceptual capabilities
remains largely overlooked.
Perception Enhancement in Multimodal Reasoning. The
pervasive shortcut phenomenon, where models over-rely on
linguistic priors and suffer from visual forgetting [34, 40],
has motivated diverse grounding strategies. Global captioning methods (e.g., Visionary-R1 [40], Perception-R1 [41])
prepend holistic image descriptions to provide contextual
priming, yet they fail to enforce fine-grained, step-relevant
grounding and cannot prevent mid-reasoning visual disengagement. Reflection-based approaches [31, 35, 43] interleave post-hoc re-examination tokens (e.g., back) to encourage visual revisitation, but these operate reactively and cannot preempt initial disengagement. More structured are visual anchor methods: Point-RFT [23] and Visual CoT [29]

3. Methodology
We propose a curriculum-based framework that disentangles
the acquisition of abstract reasoning from visual grounding.
Our approach comprises two principled stages: (1) a disentangled supervised fine-tuning curriculum that establishes a
robust reasoning foundation, and (2) a reinforcement learning phase that learns strategic perception timing. Figure 4
provides an overview of our framework.

3.1. Disentangled SFT for Foundational Visual
Grounding
The shortcut phenomenon fundamentally stems from conflating two distinct learning objectives: acquiring abstract
reasoning patterns and grounding them in visual evidence.
We address this through a two-step curriculum that sequentially develops these capabilities.
3.1.1. Phase 1: Cognitive Warm-up via Text-Only SFT
To build a strong foundation for abstract reasoning, we first
train the vision-language model without the confounding
influence of visual inputs. This approach allows the model
to learn abstract logical templates, problem decomposition,
and other cognitive behaviors (e.g., verification, subgoal
setting, backtracking) purely from high-difficulty, text-only
reasoning data.

A fundamental limitation of prior methods [28] addressing
visual forgetting is their reliance on randomly inserted perception segments—either through rigid heuristics or humanannotated grounding points. While effective at reintroducing
visual evidence, these approaches impose a fixed perception schedule that cannot adapt to the model’s emerging
reasoning patterns or question-specific demands. This rigidity creates a critical gap: the model learns to perceive but not
when to perceive, remaining a passive executor rather than
an autonomous agent.
We close this gap by formulating perception timing as
a strategic decision-making problem that must be learned.
While supervised fine-tuning teaches the model to imitate
the structure of grounded reasoning, it produces a rigid policy that cannot adaptively decide when visual grounding is
necessary. The key insight is to reward not just what the
model perceives, but when it chooses to perceive.

3.1.2. Phase 2: Anchoring Logic with PerceptionGrounded SFT
Building upon πθT , we anchor abstract reasoning to visual
evidence through a novel Perception-Grounded Chain-ofThought (PG-CoT) paradigm. This stage teaches the model
to interleave perceptual verification with reasoning steps.

operates in three steps: (1) analyze the original CoT to identify logical breakpoints where visual evidence is required,

visual elements, and (3) insert perception segments while

4.1. Implementation Details
Training Details. Experiments were conducted on eight
NVIDIA H100 GPUs. During the SFT stage, we utilized
the LLaMA-Factory framework [51] and froze the vision

emergence of visual behaviors and the number of samples.

We isolate the impact of our PG-CoT data paradigm by

the structural scaffolding for grounded reasoning, while PRL
learns when to invoke perception by coupling it to linguistic
markers of cognitive uncertainty.

5. Conclusions
In this work, we addressed the critical “think longer, see
less” pathology in MLLMs, where progressive visual forgetting undermines long-chain reasoning. We argued this
stems from entangled training, creating both a foundational
cold-start deficiency (weak abstract reasoning) and a strategic perception deficit (no “when-to-look” policy). To solve
this, we proposed a framework to disentangle these skills.
Our disentangled SFT curriculum first builds a robust textonly reasoning backbone before grounding it with novel
Perception-Grounded CoT (PG-CoT) data. Subsequently,
our Pivotal Perception Reward trains an RL agent to learn
when to look by coupling perceptual actions to cognitive uncertainty. By decomposing the problem—learning to think,
then to ground, and finally when to look—our work provides a curriculum for building more robust and strategic
multimodal reasoners.

C. Details for Perception Grounded CoT Data Generation
We transform existing multimodal reasoning datasets into PG-CoT format using a teacher MLLM, i.e., Seed1.5-VL [15]. The
data generation pipeline operates in three steps: (1) analyze the original CoT to identify logical breakpoints where visual
evidence is required, (2) generate fine-grained observations anchored to specific visual elements, and (3) insert perception
segments while preserving reasoning fidelity. The prompts for these three steps are as follows:
