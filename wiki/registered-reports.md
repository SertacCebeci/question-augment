# Registered reports

A registered report is a two-stage publication format. In Phase 1 the authors submit a study plan (the question, design, protocol and analysis methods) and "reviewers evaluate the scientific question, experimental design, analysis methods" before any data exist.[^r2] If the plan is agreed, the journal gives it in-principle acceptance: "the journal agrees to publish the completed study regardless of the results, as long as the study adheres to" the plan.[^r1] In Phase 2 the finished manuscript is checked only for adherence to the plan, then published.

The format is an argument about incentives, not a new statistical test. Preregistration of the same plan is treated as a weaker remedy: it is good practice, but preregistration as such "does not carry strong enough incentives" to be adopted by the community.[^r3] What a registered report changes is the payoff. The text's stated primary benefit is "the realignment of incentives away from positive findings that encourage engagement in QRPs".[^r4] Because acceptance no longer depends on the result, a significant p-value brings no reward (my inference from the format's rule), and the text says the format "removes the incentives to p-hack, HARK, selectively report, and engage in QRPs more generally."[^r5] The practices named are the ones described in [[evaluation-questionable-research-practices]] and in the psychology literature behind it.

The format does not forbid flexibility. "Registered reports do not aim to prohibit analytical or experimental flexibility"; exploratory analyses may appear in a separate section.[^r6] What is required is that readers can tell which tests were preregistered, and that deviations which "increase type 1 error rates (e.g., multiple testing without correction, stopping or continuing data collection based on statistical significance" are not hidden.[^r7]

There is some measured contrast in outcomes, all from psychology. Scheel et al. compared registered reports with a random sample of standard reports: "44% of results in registered reports" were positive, against "were positive, compared with 96% in standard reports."[^r8][^r9] The text reads this as the incentive effect, but the comparison is between two samples of papers, not a controlled experiment on the same studies.

On replicability the text is careful. "Whether the replicability of registered reports (or preregistered studies in general) is higher than standard reports is an open question."[^r10] The nearest evidence is a different design: a replication of 16 studies with preregistration, high statistical power and open methods, in which "86% of replications had statistically significant effects in the same" direction as the originals.[^r11] That is a study of practices in combination, not of the registered-report format.

## Limits

- The evidence on outcomes is from psychology. The text's argument for neuroscience and animal research is a contention ("I contend", "I fully support"), not a measurement. It cites the EEGManyLabs project as the only neuroscience replication effort, still in progress when the text was written.
- The 44% and 96% figures are cross-sectional. My inference: the contrast does not separate the format from what kinds of studies enter it, from journal policy, or from the time at which papers were sampled.
- The format is argued to fail to protect against bad plans. The text does say that a Phase 1 review can refuse underpowered designs, and that this "would raise the standard for statistical power in neuroscience research."[^r12] Whether reviewers actually enforce a power standard is not reported.
- Registered reports do not measure effect sizes better than standard reports by themselves; the text's claim is about which results get published, not how precisely effects are estimated.
