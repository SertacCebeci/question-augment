# Conversational-history effect on a fixed request

The same final request can be answered differently depending on what the conversation has already said before it. The measurement isolates this by holding the final prompt fixed and varying only the context. Each scenario is run twice: as a sequence of five prompts with the history kept, and as the final prompt alone in a stateless call, which "isolat[es] the request from its context".[^c1] The difference between the two rates is therefore read as the effect of the preceding turns. The paper reports it as the percentage-point change in attack success rate (ASR) for each model and attack type.

The size of the effect depends on the model. For GPT-4o Mini on illegal activities, the rate "surged from 0.70% to 33.50% when history" was kept.[^c2] Google's Gemini 2.5 Flash is at the other end: its multi-turn result is "our multi-turn attacks (0.10% average ASR with history)", and the paper calls it "nearly immune".[^c3] Claude 3 Haiku shows "a minor increase with context (+1.00 percentage points)".[^c4] The direction is not fixed either: "for some GPT models, conversational history actually decreased the" success rate on offensive content.[^c5]

The authors read the GPT result as a priming effect: a benign opening "can be primed by a benign pretext, causing it to misclassify the final harmful request".[^c6] The design they use is an operationalisation of foot-in-the-door sequencing, in which "a small initial request paves the way for a more" significant one.[^c7] The paper builds on that principle and does not test it separately; what it adds is the measured difference between the two conditions.

For the asker's side of the transformation, the point is that the wording of the final request is held constant, and what changes is the frame set up before it. A question's answer, on this evidence, depends on the frame earlier turns have built, not only on the question's own words. Whether this carries over to ordinary questions, rather than to requests that are harmful, is my inference: the paper tests only harmful-request scenarios.

## Limits

- The outcome is harmful compliance on a harm-category benchmark. The paper does not measure answer quality or accuracy on benign questions, so the effect is not shown to transfer to them.
- The scenarios were generated by a model and "chosen after an extensive optimization process".[^c8] The rates describe optimised attacks, not conversations drawn from real use.
- The judge is "Gemini 1.5 Flash as an LLM judge".[^c9] It was checked against human labels on "a stratified random sample of responses (comprising approximately 20% of the total dataset".[^c10] It reached "98.0% overall agreement with human evaluation".[^c11] That check covers one sample; the agreement is not shown per model.
- The paper reports "95% confidence intervals (CIs)" for the rates.[^c12] The text read does not show how the intervals were used to rank models, so the cross-model ordering rests on the point estimates.
- Seven models were tested, and "seven models" are drawn from a few families, with the GPT family supplying five of them.[^c13] The mechanism is not shown to be a family property.
- The target was run at "Target Model Temperature: 0.5".[^c14] The effect is measured at that setting, and other sampling settings are not reported in the text read.
- The paper's own proposed defence, which removes the earlier turns before judging the final request, is a suggestion in its discussion. The text read does not test it.
- The design changes only whether prior turns are present. It does not separate the pretext from the rest of the earlier exchange, so the mechanism behind the effect is not identified.
