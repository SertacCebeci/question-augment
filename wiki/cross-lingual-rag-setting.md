# Cross-lingual retrieval-augmented generation

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) usually assumes that the question and the retrieved documents share a language. Cross-lingual RAG drops that assumption. The XRAG benchmark states the task as follows: "We define the task of cross-lingual RAG as follows: given a question q, the LLM is prompted" to answer in the question's language, from a collection of articles that includes at least one article in another language.[^x1][^x2] The setting is defined by the language mismatch between the question and the evidence, not by the retriever or the model.

The paper separates two variants. In the monolingual variant, the evidence is one language, English, and the question is in another. The authors place it where "users in multiple locales are served by" a single system whose knowledge base is English-only.[^x3] In the multilingual variant the evidence contains "articles in both the question language and other languages to answer a question."[^x4] The first asks the model to cross the language boundary at generation time. The second asks it to combine sources written in two languages.

The two variants fail in different places, as the authors state. For the monolingual variant the reported problem is the language of the response, which is treated separately in response-language-correctness. For the multilingual variant the authors write that "the primary challenge for LLMs does not lie in nonEnglish generation, but in reasoning over retrieved information across languages."[^x5] So the same benchmark puts the difficulty in the output language in one case and in the reasoning over the evidence in the other.

Construction matters for reading the numbers. Each question has two supporting articles and six distractors. In the multilingual variant the distractors are drawn from "both English and the question language."[^x6] Distractors are restricted to articles "published at least two weeks before the supporting articles" so that they cannot answer the question.[^x7] The retriever is a tool for building the benchmark; the paper says its focus is "and not on the document retrieval task itself."[^x8]

Relation to what the wiki holds: the visual-question version of the same mismatch shows that non-English evidence costs more than non-English instructions, see [[evidence-language-integration-gap]]. Retrievers themselves favour documents in the query's language, see [[monolingual-query-document-alignment]]. The XRAG setting is where a generator meets what such a retriever returns. Whether the text-only result matches the visual one is not tested in the paper (my inference).

## Limits

- Four target languages (German, Spanish, Chinese, Arabic) are compared with English. The authors describe the pairs as "ranging from easy (es-en)" to harder ones, a ranking they take from earlier work.[^x9] Other language pairs and scripts are not covered.
- The questions are generated by GPT-4o and the Q&A pairs are human-translated, so the translation of questions is a confound the design does not remove; see [[translation-confounded-language-gap]].
- Five LLMs are evaluated. The results are a snapshot of those models at one date and do not establish a trend.
- Accuracy is scored by a judge panel, and the authors check it against human judges. The check is for the panel's decisions as a whole, not for each component in isolation.
- The English baseline is the same questions asked in English, so the comparison isolates the language of the question and response, not the difficulty of the content (the paper's design; I have not checked it beyond the text read).
