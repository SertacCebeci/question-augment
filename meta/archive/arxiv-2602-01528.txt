1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly relied upon
for complex reasoning tasks. Recent advances in large
reasoning models (LRMs), such as OpenAI-o1 (OpenAI,
2025) and DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025), equip models
with explicit reasoning mechanisms, including Chain-ofThought (Wei et al., 2023a) and self-reflection (Madaan
et al., 2023), achieving strong performance on complex domains ranging from mathematics to code generation (Jiang
et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025a). These capabilities also underpin the growing use of LLMs as automated evaluators
(LLM-as-a-Judge) (Gu & Others, 2024; Li & Others, 2024).

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly
serve as reasoners and automated evaluators,
yet they remain susceptible to cognitive biases—
often altering their reasoning when faced with
spurious prompt-level cues such as consensus
claims or authority appeals. Existing mitigations
via prompting or supervised fine-tuning fail to
generalize, as they modify surface behavior
without changing the optimization objective
that makes bias cues attractive. We propose
Epistemic Independence Training (EIT), a
reinforcement learning framework grounded
in a key principle: to learn independence, bias
cues must be made non-predictive of reward.
EIT operationalizes this through a balanced
conflict strategy where bias signals are equally
likely to support correct and incorrect answers,
combined with a reward design that penalizes
bias-following without rewarding bias agreement.
Experiments on Qwen3-4B demonstrate that
EIT improves both accuracy and robustness
under adversarial biases, while preserving
performance when bias aligns with truth. Notably, models trained only on bandwagon bias
generalize to unseen bias types such as authority
and distraction, indicating that EIT induces
transferable epistemic independence rather than
bias-specific heuristics. EIT further generalizes
across benchmarks (MedQA, HellaSwag), model
families (Llama-3.2-3B), and scales (Qwen3-8B),
and outperforms distribution-shift methods
(GroupDRO, IRM) without requiring environment labels. Code and data are available at

Despite these reasoning capabilities, LLMs remain highly
sensitive to prompt-level cues that are unrelated to task
correctness. Prior work documents systematic cognitive
biases, including bandwagon bias (conforming to majority opinion) and authority bias (deferring to perceived experts) (Koo et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2024b). Figure 1 illustrates this vulnerability: when asked whether the Great
Wall of China is visible from space, an advanced reasoning model OpenAI-o1 arrives at the correct answer under a
clean prompt, but reverses its decision when presented with
a fabricated consensus—even though the added information
is non-causal and irrelevant to the underlying question.
These cognitive biases mirror well-studied phenomena in
human decision-making, where social signals systematically influence judgments even when non-causal (Koo et al.,
2023). Critically, the issue is not that LLMs lack reasoning
ability—the model in Figure 1 demonstrates sound reasoning under clean conditions. The problem is that prompt-level
cues can systematically alter the model’s reasoning trajectory and final decision. We characterize this as a failure
of epistemic independence: the ability to make judgments
invariant to spurious prompt-level signals, relying instead
on task-relevant reasoning.
Why does this dependence arise? During pretraining and
alignment, cues such as consensus statements or authoritative language often correlate with positive feedback, making
them attractive shortcuts (Sharma et al., 2023). Existing
approaches—whether prompting (Ye et al., 2024b; Wang
et al., 2025a) or supervised fine-tuning (Yang et al., 2025)—

The key insight underlying our approach is that epistemic
independence can only be learned if spurious cues are
made non-predictive of reward. If a bias signal predicts
correctness—even weakly—an optimal policy may rationally exploit it. Conversely, if bias provides no directional
advantage, the only viable strategy is to rely on task-relevant
reasoning.

Contributions. First, we formulate epistemic independence
as a reinforcement learning problem in which spurious cues
must be rendered non-predictive of reward, providing a
principled framework for bias mitigation in LLM reasoning. Second, we propose a balanced conflict strategy and
a bias-penalizing reward design that jointly prevent shortcut learning and force reliance on task-relevant reasoning.
Third, we demonstrate that EIT improves both accuracy
and robustness, generalizes to unseen bias types, benchmarks, and model families, outperforms larger untrained
models and distribution-shift methods on bias resistance,
and produces genuine epistemic independence rather than
surface-level imitation.

We introduce Epistemic Independence Training (EIT), a
reinforcement learning framework that operationalizes this
insight through two components. First, a balanced conflict
strategy: each bias signal supports the correct answer in
50% of samples and the incorrect answer in 50%, making the cue-correctness association unbiased. Second, a
reward design that combines accuracy with a bias penalty:
the model is rewarded for correct answers and penalized
for following bias to wrong answers, while receiving no
extra credit when bias happens to align with truth. We optimize using Group Relative Policy Optimization (Shao et al.,
2024), reinforcing responses that demonstrate both correctness and independence. We train EIT on Qwen3-1.7B and
Qwen3-4B using MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024) with only

position). Experiments show that EIT improves adversarialbias accuracy by +13.2% (70.1%→83.3%) and robustness

has emerged as a scalable alternative to human evaluation
(Zheng et al., 2023), yet its reliability is undermined by
cognitive biases: systematic deviations where models alter judgments based on external cues rather than factual
reasoning (Koo et al., 2023). These biases include social

We formally define epistemic independence as the property
that a model’s prediction is invariant to the injected bias
cue:
P (Y | X, B) = P (Y | X) ∀ B,
(1)
where X is the input prompt, B is the bias cue, and Y is
the model’s output. This is an operational definition scoped
to prompt-level influence cues—we do not claim independence in a broader philosophical sense. Importantly, the
non-predictiveness guarantee in Eq. 1 applies specifically
to the injected bias signal; it does not preclude reliance on
other shortcuts that may be present in the data.

Inference-Time Bias Mitigation Approaches. Existing
inference-time approaches attempt to mitigate bias without
modifying model weights. Prompt engineering instructs
models to ignore external cues, but fails against strong bias
injections because models lack intrinsic incentive to follow
such instructions (Zheng et al., 2023). Activation steering
(Zou et al., 2023) manipulates latent representations to encourage truthfulness, but requires identifying appropriate
steering vectors for each bias type and does not generalize to
unseen biases. Modular detectors (Yang et al., 2025) identify biased evaluations externally and prompt self-correction,
but add inference overhead and depend on detector quality rather than improving the judge model itself. These
approaches share a common limitation: they treat symptoms rather than causes, leaving the model’s underlying
susceptibility unchanged.

We operationalize our core principle via conflict data: the
injected cue supports the correct answer with probability
0.5 and an incorrect answer with probability 0.5. Under
this distribution, any policy conditioning on the cue cannot
achieve higher expected return than one relying on intrinsic
reasoning.
Reinforcement learning is the appropriate mechanism because, unlike SFT which overfits to surface patterns (Guo
et al., 2025), RL enables direct optimization toward accuracy and independence while exploring diverse reasoning
strategies. We train exclusively on Bandwagon Bias and
evaluate generalization along an OOD spectrum: Authority
Bias and Distraction Bias test transfer to related contentbased prompt biases, while Position Bias tests transfer to
structural biases.

Training-Time Bias Mitigation Approaches. Trainingbased approaches aim to produce inherently robust models. SFT on debiasing datasets (Wei et al., 2023b) teaches
models to produce unbiased outputs, but tends to learn surface patterns rather than genuine reasoning, leading to poor
generalization on unseen bias types (Huang et al., 2024).
Distribution-shift methods such as GroupDRO (Sagawa
et al., 2020) and IRM (Arjovsky et al., 2019) learn invariant
representations across environments, but require pre-defined
environment labels (e.g., bias-present vs. bias-absent). In
our experiments (Table 6), EIT achieves competitive or superior robustness without environment annotations, as the
conflict strategy constructs the non-predictive distribution
directly. Our EIT leverages reinforcement learning with
balanced conflict data and bias-penalizing reward design
to make bias signals non-predictive of reward. Detailed
comparison of existing approaches and EIT are in Table 8.

3.2. Bias Injection
We inject biases into MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024)
prompts, training on one bias type and evaluating generalization across an OOD spectrum. Bandwagon bias serves
as our training signal, simulating social consensus (e.g.,
“90% of people choose X”) with the conflict strategy (50%
correct-bias, 50% wrong-bias). For evaluation, we test three
additional biases: authority bias adds expert claims (“Research shows X is correct”), distraction bias introduces irrelevant information, and position bias swaps option order. The
first two are content-based prompt biases related to social
influence; position bias is structural, testing whether transfer
extends beyond content-based cues. See Appendix B for
full examples.

3.4. Epistemic Reward Shaping
We design a hierarchical reward R = Rstruct + Racc + Rind
where each component prevents a specific failure mode:
structural constraint blocks gaming via unparsable outputs,
accuracy prevents random contrarianism, and independence
prevents the “always follow bias” shortcut. Hyperparameter
values are provided in Appendix C.
Structural Constraint. The model receives Rstruct = α only
if the response follows Chain-of-Thought format:
Rstruct (y) = α · ICoT (y)

The indicator ICoT (y) is a binary parse check: it verifies
the presence of <think> tags and a parsable answer via
regex, without assessing reasoning quality. This grounds
exploration by ensuring parsable reasoning paths before
evaluating accuracy.

Our experiments address four key questions: (1) Does EIT
improve both accuracy and robustness across diverse bias
types? (2) Does resistance learned from one bias type transfer to unseen biases? (3) How does EIT compare against
prompting-based and supervised fine-tuning approaches?
(4) Can model scaling alone achieve similar robustness, or
is targeted training necessary? We additionally conduct
ablation studies to validate our conflict data strategy and
hierarchical reward design.

Baselines. We compare against three baselines. Vanilla
models are Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B without fine-tuning
(Team, 2025). Prompt mitigation uses debiasing prompts
from prior work (Ye et al., 2024b; Wang et al., 2025a)
at inference time (see Appendix D). SFT fine-tunes on
bandwagon bias data with paired examples containing biasinjected prompts and correct reasoning responses, using the
same training data and base models as EIT for fair comparison. Hyperparameters are in Appendix C.

performance. Our results show the opposite: EIT achieves
the best or second-best performance in the majority of metrics across both model sizes and bias types. The improvements are particularly striking for distraction bias on Qwen34B, where wrong-bias accuracy jumps from 45.9% to 85.0%

suggest that resisting bias is fundamentally about grounding
decisions in factual reasoning, which benefits both robustness and accuracy.

EIT outperforms both prompting and supervised approaches. Prompt-mitigation often hurts accuracy: Qwen34B clean accuracy on validation drops from 66.2% to 40.9%,

The balanced conflict strategy succeeds by making bias
direction non-predictive, forcing the model to ignore
rather than oppose social signals. Training exclusively on
wrong-bias samples (Wrong-Only) dramatically improves
wrong-bias performance (test bandwagon RRW : 63.6% to

90.4% to 71.2%)—the model learns a shortcut that backfires
when bias happens to support the correct answer. This pattern amplifies on OOD authority bias, where Wrong-Only’s
rejection strategy proves actively harmful. In contrast, our
conflict strategy achieves balanced robustness because neither bias direction provides a reliable signal.

The independence reward is essential for learning genuine epistemic independence. Removing it drops wrongbias robustness from 80.0% to 65.8%, while accuracy-only
training produces results indistinguishable from baseline.
The structure reward provides moderate gains by encouraging explicit separation of fact verification from social signal
acknowledgment. Together, these ablations confirm that
EIT’s effectiveness stems from forcing models to treat bias
as noise rather than signal.

7. Limitations

Figure 9. SFT model response to the question in Figure 7. The

We acknowledge several limitations of this work:
1. Evaluation scope. Our evaluation uses multiple-choice
tasks (MMLU-Pro, MedQA, HellaSwag). While we
demonstrate cross-benchmark transfer, it remains to be
tested whether EIT generalizes to open-ended judging
tasks or free-form generation.
2. Synthetic bias templates. Biases are injected using synthetic templates that approximate real-world influence
cues. Naturally occurring biases may be more subtle and
diverse than our controlled injections.
3. Bias type coverage. We test four bias types (bandwagon,
authority, distraction, position). Other cognitive biases
such as anchoring, framing, and recency effects remain
unexplored.
4. Non-predictiveness scope. The formal guarantee that
the bias cue is non-predictive of reward (Eq. 1) applies
specifically to the injected bias signal. It does not preclude reliance on other shortcuts that may already be
present in the training data (e.g., systematic patterns
in incorrect options (Alzahrani et al., 2024)). Crossbenchmark improvements (Table 4) provide empirical
evidence against benchmark-specific overfitting, but a
formal guarantee over all possible shortcuts is not possible.
5. Insertion-position confound. Bandwagon and distraction biases share post-option placement, while authority uses inline insertion. The cross-position transfer
from bandwagon to authority (+4.4% RR) mitigates this

models. First, domain engagement: EIT models explicitly
invoke relevant knowledge at high rates, while SFT models
rarely do. Second, explicit verification: EIT models perform
actual computations in most traces, whereas SFT models
skip this step entirely. Third, reasoned disagreement: when
bias conflicts with their reasoning, EIT models explicitly
acknowledge the conflict and override it with justification.
This three-stage pattern—engage domain knowledge, verify
through computation, override conflicting signals—is the
behavioral signature of genuine epistemic independence.

6. Conclusion
We presented Epistemic Independence Training (EIT), a
reinforcement learning framework that makes bias cues nonpredictive of reward through a balanced conflict strategy and
bias-penalizing reward design. EIT improves both accuracy
and robustness on Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-1.7B, with strong
transfer to unseen content-based prompt biases (authority,
distraction). Unlike SFT, which produces performative independence, EIT trains models to engage in genuine reasoning
rather than superficial heuristics. Our results demonstrate
that principled reward design can instill robust epistemic
independence in LLM reasoning. Applying EIT to LLMas-a-Judge evaluation pipelines and open-ended generation

6. Potential over-skepticism. While the true authority

7. Model scope. We evaluate on Qwen3 (1.7B, 4B, 8B) and

A. More Related Work

2025; Plaat et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). A key discovery is that sufficiently large models possess significant intrinsic reasoning skills, which can be elicited using either
prompting or reinforcement learning. For example, prompting methods (Yao et al., 2023a; Kojima et al., 2023; Wei
et al., 2023a; Yao et al., 2023b) encourage models to break
down complex issues by producing intermediate steps. This
step-by-step approach has been shown to greatly improve
performance on hard tasks, highlighting that tapping into a
model’s existing potential is just as important as increasing
its size. Extending this idea, reinforcement learning (RL) is
widely used to help LLMs generalize their reasoning, rather
than just mimicking labeled chains of thought (Schulman
et al., 2017; Guo et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). While supervised fine-tuning restricts models to copying fixed patterns,
RL allows them to actively explore a broad range of reasoning paths. By learning from reward signals, models can
find effective strategies and develop new reasoning abilities
(Guo et al., 2025).

Large Reasoning Models Large Reasoning Models have
arisen as a novel class of language models designed to address complex problem-solving tasks by internalizing structured reasoning mechanisms (Plaat et al., 2024). Central to
LRMs are techniques such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) for
generating step-by-step reasoning (Wei et al., 2023a; Zhu
et al., 2025), divide-and-conquer strategies for problem decomposition (Tang et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2023a; Plaat et al.,
2024; Zhao et al.), and self-reflection for iterative improvement (Madaan et al., 2023). Representative models like
DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) and OpenAI-o1 (OpenAI,
2025) demonstrate the success of this paradigm. By leveraging these methods, LRMs have demonstrated superior
performance over previous general-purpose LLMs, particularly in logic-intensive fields like mathematical reasoning
and coding (Xu et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Dong et al.,
2025).
Adversarial Attacks on LLMs LLMs are prone to adversarial exploitations like prompt injection, where embedded
instructions alter the model’s behavior to bypass safety filters or extract confidential information (Cantini et al., 2025;
Maloyan & Namiot, 2025; Peng et al., 2024; Shayegani
et al., 2023). Such incidents emphasize a critical trait of
LLMs: their extreme sensitivity to the nuances of input
framing (Cantini et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025b; TANG et al.,
2025). Drawing from this, we posit that the fragility revealed
by adversarial attacks is essentially the same mechanism
driving cognitive biases in evaluation tasks. For instance,
methods like JudgeDeceive can compromise evaluation reliability, and deceptive fairness attacks can skew outcomes
(Maloyan & Namiot, 2025; Cantini et al., 2025). Therefore,
studying these attack vectors is key to understanding how
subtle input changes impact the fairness and stability of
LLM evaluations (Peng et al., 2024; Shayegani et al., 2023).

Consequence of LLM Judging Bias Judging biases in
large language models, including preferences for specific
positions (Zheng et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2025; Wang et al.,
2023) or writing styles (Wu & Aji, 2023; Koo et al., 2023;
Chen et al., 2024), present practical challenges. The validity
of LLM research and the reliability of downstream applications are fundamentally compromised by these biases,
which distort model comparison outcomes and result in systematically unfair evaluations (Feuer et al., 2025; Dorner
et al., 2025). For example, merely swapping the sequence
of responses could lead advanced models like GPT-4 to produce inconsistent results, a manifestation of positional bias
that critically impairs fairness (Wang et al., 2023; Zheng
et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025a). Moreover, the deployment
of biased judges to generate preference data for alignment
frameworks, such as Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF) (Lee et al., 2024), introduces detrimental
feedback cycles. Research indicates that models subject
to this alignment process may inadvertently learn to favor
superficial features, such as verbosity, at the expense of factual correctness and safety (Feuer et al., 2025; Zhou et al.,
2024). Furthermore, the alignment challenge is aggravated
by a critical flaw in judgment, where models tend to assign
higher scores to fluent hallucinations than to unpolished
truths (Ye et al., 2024a; Fu et al., 2023), exposing a style
bias for persuasive writing over correctness.

LLM Evaluation Evaluating the strengths and weaknesses
of large language models is a key part of their development,
with benchmark performance often serving as a proxy for
general intelligence. Existing benchmarks cover a broad
range of skills, including specialized tasks such as coding
(Austin et al., 2021) and logical reasoning (Liu et al., 2020),
as well as core abilities such as question answering (Yang
et al., 2018), text generation (Lin et al., 2020; Guo et al.,
2017), and general language understanding (Wang et al.,
2019). In addition, recent studies have started combining
these standard benchmarks with human feedback, adversarial testing, and meta-evaluation methods (Chang et al.,
2023). With the continuous evolution of the field, building
stronger frameworks for LLM evaluation remain a key area
of research.
