# Reliability-Aware Score

> Commitment does not have to be all-or-nothing per answer: a
> transcriber can commit word by word, and its overall score should
> discount an admitted gap far less than a confident lie.

## Definition / Mechanism

RAS (Huang (2026)) extends Word Error Rate to a system that may output a special
placeholder mark `PH` in place of any span it will not commit to:
"the PH represents abstention: when the model encounters low-quality or
ambiguous speech and cannot reliably determine the underlying content,
it outputs PH to indicate uncertainty rather than producing a
potentially misleading guess." Unlike instance-level [[abstention|selective
prediction]], which discards a whole output, "Unlike full-sentence
rejection, our approach allows the model to selectively abstain on
ambiguous segments while transcribing the remaining content with high
commitment."[^c1]

A dynamic-programming alignment (a modified edit distance, O(N²M))
scores a hypothesis against the reference. An ordinary substitution,
deletion or insertion costs 1; but a `PH` may absorb an arbitrary-length
span of the reference at a discounted rate, because withholding is
judged less harmful than a wrong committed word: "we assign a cost
factor α ∈ (0, 1) to all PH-related operations." RAS is then
usefulness (the fraction of words correctly transcribed) minus this
discounted cost: "This formulation directly balances informative
content against erroneous commitments under the proposed alignment. It
has a maximum of 1, and a higher RAS implies a more reliable and useful
ASR model." When no `PH` is emitted, RAS reduces to (twice) WER, so
a non-abstaining system is scored exactly as before.

The trade-off α is not set by hand. Listening-test subjects rank a
conventional transcript against an abstention-aware one, and their
preference probability is fit by a Bradley–Terry model on the RAS gap
between the two, giving a closed calibration target: "In practice, α
should be calibrated to align with human preferences over reliability
in specific application contexts, rather than selected heuristically."
 On a mixed medical/noisy-conversational listening test (980
preference annotations, 42 participants), this fitting yielded "α =
0.5064 and an average RAS gap of ∆U = 0.0461, demonstrating alignment
between the proposed RAS formulation and human reliability judgments."
[^c5]

Used as a reward for group-relative policy optimization on top of a
supervised placeholder-emission stage, RAS also functions as a training
target, not only a post-hoc score; a plain per-token confidence
threshold ("Base+Logit") "provides marginal gains, confirming that
naive confidence thresholding is insufficient."[^c6]

## Why it matters

RAS separates two things the wiki elsewhere keeps together — how much
an answerer commits and how it is scored for withholding — by making
the granularity of commitment itself part of the metric: standard WER
and semantic metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore) all force a transcript
and cannot reward a system for admitting a gap instead of guessing. The
discounted-cost DP alignment gives that reward a principled, tunable
shape rather than an ad hoc partial-credit rule, and the human-fit α
grounds the shape empirically instead of by convention.

## Limits

α was fit on one listening-test population (medical and noisy
conversational speech, 42 participants) and one instrument (BeaqleJS
pairwise choice with a "Can't Decide" option); the paper does not
claim the fitted α = 0.5064 transfers across domains or languages. The
placeholder-replacement training data (Stage 1) is itself generated by
aligning the base model's own errors against ground truth, so RAS as a
reward inherits whatever error distribution that base model has. The
metric assumes a sequential, word-aligned output (edit distance over a
reference sequence) and is not shown to generalize to non-sequential
or free-form answers.
