# Offline-augmented characteristic time

In fixed-confidence best-arm identification, the lower bound on online samples comes from a small optimization. Choose per-arm sample counts so that, for every wrong arm, a KL-type divergence between the best arm and that arm, evaluated at the pooled counts, clears log(1/δ); then minimize the total count. The offline-online version changes one thing: each arm's count is the sum of the offline samples it already has and the online samples still to be drawn. The offline count is defined as "the total number of samples from arm a in the offline data be denoted by Nao", and the online count is the rest.[^c1] The optimization then runs over online counts only, with the offline counts sitting inside each constraint as pulls already made.

The consequence is that offline data cannot raise the bound and can lower it. The paper states the monotone form directly: "offline samples always reduce the lower bound".[^c2] The saving is governed by a characteristic time that shrinks as the offline fraction grows, so the purely online value is the upper end of the range. The offline data need not come from a good policy. In the experiments, data from a policy that never pulls the best arm still "helps reduce the number of online rounds".[^c3] The stopping statistic of the algorithm uses the same augmentation, with "number of offline samples from each arm, and the empirical estimates of the means of each arm" in place of the true quantities.[^c4] The sampling rule is "a track-and-stop algorithm that pulls arms in proportions suggested by the" bound.[^c5]

The allocation is where the offline case departs from the purely online one. The proportions the bound suggests depend on how much offline data each arm holds, and they need not be positive: "these are guaranteed to be strictly positive, it is no longer the case in our setting."[^c6] The paper calls this the main technical difficulty, since the proportions "can possibly converge to 0 in the limit of small δ", and the analysis treats that case separately.[^c7] An arm with ample offline data can therefore receive almost no online pulls in the limit. A purely online intuition, in which every arm needs some sampling, would miss this. That reading is my own inference from the proportion result, not a claim the paper makes.

## Limits

The result is a lower bound together with an asymptotic match, not a finite-sample guarantee. The paper's upper bound matches the lower bound as δ goes to zero, and it notes that "such asymptotic guarantees are standard in BAI literature".[^c8] The setting assumes that offline and online data come from one bandit instance: the offline data are generated by a policy that "generates samples from the same bandit instance as the one in the online phase".[^c9] The arm distributions form a known single-parameter exponential family, and the best arm is unique; with ties, the algorithm "will need to statistically separate the tied arms", a harder problem the paper does not solve here.[^c10] The amount of offline data is allowed to depend on δ, which the analysis needs.[^c11] The experiments are narrow: Gaussian rewards with variance 1, δ = 10^-3, two offline policies, averaged over 50 trials.[^c12]

The text does not establish how the bound behaves when offline samples come from another instance, when their counts are noisy, or what the saving is in a finite-sample regime. It shows only that more offline data lowers the bound, not an exchange rate between offline and online samples; reading one offline sample as worth one online sample would be my extrapolation. The guarantee used here is the joint one, under which offline samples are random. Under a guarantee that must hold on every offline path, the offline data stop helping; see [[joint-versus-conditional-delta-guarantee]].
