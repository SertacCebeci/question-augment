# Default tone skew

Asked for a neutral reply, a language model does not necessarily return a neutral tone. Bodara, Mushfiq and Siddiqui build two synthetic sets of assistant dialogues with several LLMs: one from tone-neutral prompts, one from prompts that ask for a positive or a negative tone. Each assistant reply is then given a tone label by a DistilBERT model fine-tuned on SST-2, used as weak supervision.[^c3] The finding they call surprising is that the neutral set is not neutral: "even the neutral set showed consistent tonal skew",[^c2] and tone-neutral prompts "yield consistently polite or positive framings".[^c1]

The paper does not identify a mechanism. It offers one hypothesis: the lean comes from "the model's underlying conversational style" rather than from the user's input, since the same prompt without a tone instruction still produces the lean. What the paper does concretely is a measurement pipeline. Label the replies, train classifiers (TF-IDF or Word2Vec features with Naive Bayes, logistic regression, linear SVM, and an ensemble) to recover those labels, and read their performance as evidence that the tone is systematic. The ensemble reaches macro-F1 values "between 0.84 and 0.92 across both datasets" at the stricter threshold, and "dropped to the 0.66-0.80 range" at the looser one.[^c4]

The threshold matters for what the skew means. A looser confidence threshold admits borderline replies and lowers macro-F1; the authors read the stricter threshold's gain as tone bias becoming "more apparent when ambiguous samples are excluded".[^c5] So the size of the lean depends on which replies are counted as carrying a tone at all.

The lean also shows up in answers to real questions, and a human baseline makes it visible. One study takes 2000 Stack Overflow questions with "answers from ChatGPT and human experts to a randomly selected set"[^c11] of them, and reads the answers with a multi-label emotion classifier. The ChatGPT answers come out more optimistic and joyful, and the human answers more often carry anger and disgust.[^c12] The sections read give no instruction behind the ChatGPT answers, so on my reading the positive lean there is a default rather than an instructed tone; that reading is my inference, not the paper's claim.

The hard cases show what the detector misses. The most error-prone replies were "subtly framed replies where lexical positivity contrasted with negative implications", such as polite disagreement or cautionary advice.[^c6] Sarcastic or hedged replies challenged the linear models, "as these cues are often expressed through punctuation or rhythm rather than explicit word choice."[^c7]

For asking and answering, the consequence is about baselines. Tone is treated in this literature as something that affects trust, empathy and fairness: "Tone affects how users perceive trust, empathy, and fairness in their interactions".[^c8] If a model's neutral answer already leans polite or positive, an answer's warmth cannot be read as a response to the question without a neutral baseline from the same model. This is my inference from the paper's finding, not a result the paper tests. A deliberately prompted style is a different case, see [[assistant-personality-style]], where the lean is an instructed condition rather than a default. Agreement aimed at the asker, as in [[social-sycophancy]], also differs: a default tone needs no cue from the asker.

## Limits

- The labels are weak. The authors say so and plan to "replace weak supervision with an expertannotated bias rubric and a calibrated gold-standard dataset".[^c9] No human rater's judgement of any reply appears in the sections read.
- The metric measures agreement with a sentiment model's labels, not the size of the skew. A macro-F1 of 0.92 says a classifier can learn the DistilBERT labels from the text; it does not say how far the neutral set leans from neutral (my inference). The size of the neutral-set lean is not reported in the sections read.
- SST-2 is a movie-review sentiment dataset. Its notion of positive and negative is the labeller's, not a reader's; whether "polite" and "positive" in the SST-2 sense are the tone that matters for trust is not tested (my inference).
- No user perception is measured, although trust and fairness are the stated motivation.
- The data are synthetic, generated by ChatGPT, Gemma and Hugging Face variants. The authors plan to "evaluate the framework on real personalassistant interactions",[^c10] which has not been done. Results are not broken out by generating model in the sections read.
- The paper gives no account of what a non-skewed neutral reply would look like, so "bias" here means departure from the labeller's neutral class, not from a reference neutrality.
- The Stack Overflow measurement uses a classifier built for another text type. Its label set was chosen as "encompassing 11 emotions commonly expressed in tweets: anger,"[^c13] and the paper's dominant-emotion result does not separate the groups: "dominant emotion for both the question and the answers from humans and ChatGPT."[^c14] The lean is therefore read from mean scores, not from a categorical label, and only in software engineering, which the authors chose as a domain "less inclined toward emotional interactions, as it is primarily about"[^c16]. Whether the lean holds where emotion is central is not tested.
- The fingerprint use the paper proposes, that emotion could detect ChatGPT text, is a suggestion in its discussion: the differences "serve as unique fingerprints, enabling the detection of text generated"[^c15]. No detection test is run in the sections read.
