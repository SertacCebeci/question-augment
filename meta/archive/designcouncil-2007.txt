the Double Diamond A universally accepted depiction of the design process. “How do we describe
design process?”&nbsp; In 2003,&nbsp;Design Council was promoting the positive impact of adopting a
strategic approach to design and the value of ‘design management’ as a practice. However, they had
no standard way of describing the supporting process. Richard&nbsp;Eisermann,&nbsp;Design Council’s
then&nbsp;Director of Design and Innovation,&nbsp;thought that this was incompatible with their
broader message, so he asked&nbsp;his&nbsp;team, “How do we describe design process?”.&nbsp; Of
course, kite-shaped&nbsp;process&nbsp;models have been referenced as far back as the 60s, but models
of design process&nbsp;were&nbsp;not widely&nbsp;shared&nbsp;at this point. Part of Design Council’s
reason for creating the Double Diamond was to address this lack of visibility. Today the Double
Diamond is a universally accessible description of the design process and has become an accepted
part of design language,&nbsp;it is used and referenced worldwide.&nbsp; At the time,
Richard&nbsp;Eisermann&nbsp;was&nbsp;new to his role at&nbsp;Design Council. In his words, he and
his&nbsp;new&nbsp;team “took big steps to change the landscape for design in the UK.”&nbsp;&nbsp;
Moving from Whirlpool Europe to Design Council in 2003, Richard had inherited a multi-disciplinary
Design and Innovation team from predecessor&nbsp;Clive&nbsp;Grinyer. The team comprised a mix of
Design Council and independent design strategists, with a varied combination of backgrounds,
expertise and experience. The group included Anna White, Chris Vanstone, Gill Wildman, Jennie
Winhall and Jonathan Ball, all of whom were embedded in challenge-oriented project
teams.&nbsp;&nbsp; Clive&nbsp;Grinyer&nbsp;had routinely brought the group together to share
experiences from their developing practice. Richard continued these regular meetings when he took
over the reins.&nbsp; One of his first steps was a study of Design Council methods and practices.
The aim was to&nbsp;categorise&nbsp;and document the design methods used across
the&nbsp;organisation, and&nbsp;determine a flexible framework in which these methods could be used.
This&nbsp;endeavour&nbsp;led to the creation of both the Double Diamond and another Design Council
resource called the Methods Bank.&nbsp; Although project teams across Design Council talked about
design process, they didn’t have a standard way of presenting this process, or a consistent way of
managing design projects. They also had widely varying points of reference and experience.&nbsp;
Jonathan Ball, an independent strategist on the Design and Innovation team, recalls how things got
started: “As Richard got his feet under the table, he was excited to see the new work at Design
Council and the breadth of challenges that were being addressed. He&nbsp;realised&nbsp;that Design
Council talked about process&nbsp;–&nbsp;&nbsp;the&nbsp;design process&nbsp;–&nbsp;but wasn't
explicit about how this process was defined.”&nbsp; Richard believed Design Council needed a
consistent way of telling a process story.&nbsp;So, at one of those regular team meetings, Richard
expressed his curiosity as to how Design Council might define design process and the methods that
could be used at different stages of that process. He then challenged his team to answer the
question.&nbsp; Deconstructing a common process “We assumed from what we'd done that there might be
a common process. What we went through was a way of finding out if we were right, and what that
process might be.”&nbsp; Jonathan Ball Responding to the challenge, the Design and Innovation team
set to work beginning a series of reviews of recent projects shaped and delivered through Design
Council&nbsp;programmes. At the time, the&nbsp;programmes&nbsp;were responding to business, science
and technology, and social challenges, with the approaches heavily influenced by the newly
developing practice of service design.&nbsp; The ambition was to create something that would be
applicable in any field; to create a model that&nbsp;Design Council could use with anyone&nbsp;they
worked with.&nbsp;&nbsp; There were (and are) many ways to describe design process,&nbsp;and
diamond-shaped models were not unfamiliar to Richard. He remembers the first time he was exposed to
the concept, “Dave&nbsp;Duncanson, an engineer at IDEO, talked to me about the product development
process as being like the classic diamond-shaped kite, with a tail composed of progressively smaller
diamonds.&nbsp;&nbsp;So&nbsp;the double diamond shape was definitely already present at IDEO in the
late 90s, although it may not have been called such”.&nbsp; Richard’s next encounter with this
diamond shape was at Whirlpool. “We did an extensive
innovation&nbsp;programme&nbsp;with&nbsp;business guru&nbsp;Gary Hamel.&nbsp;Gary used the diamond
as a way of framing innovation. He even called it the Double Diamond. But the names of the four
steps were different.”&nbsp; So&nbsp;the seeds of a diamond shape were already planted in Richard’s
mind and he conveyed the idea to the team as part of their review and development process. Richard
said, "The team put in the work trying to define design, process, methods, etc. What we did with the
Double Diamond was codify it, rename the steps and popularise it. It was important work, but we were
certainly standing on the shoulders of giants." These&nbsp;were the likes of Herbert Simon, Thomas
Marcus, Thomas W.&nbsp;Maver, Bela H.&nbsp;Banathy,&nbsp;Barry Boehm, Paul Souza and Nigel Cross.
From the 60s through to the 90s, their research had resulted in proposed models containing elements
of divergence and convergence, cycles and iterative structures.&nbsp; Chris Vanstone and Anna White
were tasked with setting up the reviews, which included research with each of the Design Council
teams, as well as looking further afield.&nbsp;&nbsp; Anna recounts the extent of the task: “We
mapped out the processes for each team, comparing, contrasting&nbsp;and looking for similarities. We
also looked outside out&nbsp;Design Council too, mapping methodologies and reviewing publications on
design process and design methods at the time, which were also incorporated.”&nbsp; The group
individually assessed the methods they had used in their projects. They considered the criteria for
defining what ‘good’ looked like in terms of project output. They explored the place of concepts
such as divergent and convergent thinking and iteration&nbsp;–&nbsp;the repeatable characteristics
that come into play throughout the design process. They also critiqued the impact of the systematic
use of design methods including user research, prototyping and testing.&nbsp; By deconstructing the
process around the methods used in different projects, they started to see similarities and
patterns. This enabled them to then work together to map a potential common design
process.&nbsp;&nbsp; Over the course of several sessions, the group came up with a simplified way to
describe any design and innovation process. It is based on four distinct phases that the team,
deliberately seeking a memorable device, named Discover, Define, Develop and Deliver:&nbsp; Discover
The process&nbsp;starts&nbsp;by questioning the challenge and quickly leads to research to identify
user needs.&nbsp; Define The second phase is to make sense of the findings, understanding how user
needs and the problem align. The result is to create a design brief which clearly defines the
challenge based on these insights.&nbsp; Develop The third&nbsp;phase&nbsp;concentrates on
developing, testing and refining multiple potential solutions.&nbsp; Deliver The
final&nbsp;phase&nbsp;involves selecting a single solution that works&nbsp;and preparing it for
launch.&nbsp; Chris and Anna worked with graphic design agency Cartlidge&nbsp;Levene&nbsp;to develop
the graphic design and print representation of the Double Diamond and Methods Bank. Visual design
company Matt &amp; George then worked on a digital animation.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; This representation
of the Double Diamond remains unchanged today. Two diamond shapes starting with the initial
challenge or problem statement to the left, moving through a definition of the problem to be
addressed in the&nbsp;centre&nbsp;and ending with the solution to the right. The diamond shapes
represent how divergent and convergent thinking fit within each stage. Symbols in each phase
represent points of iteration for research, learning, prototyping and testing.&nbsp;&nbsp; From the
outset the team&nbsp;recognised&nbsp;that, although a graphic convenience, the simplicity in the
symmetry of the diagram helped the introduction of the concept and its subsequent
application.&nbsp;&nbsp; Working with the process “For me the first part of the diamond is about
questioning the brief and defining the problem statement. I explain it as ‘designing the right
thing’. The second part of the diamond is about exploring possibility, iteration, testing and
developing, so ‘designing the thing right’.”&nbsp; Anna White In 2004, Design Council started to
share the Double Diamond at conferences and in presentations. The Design and Innovation team also
started to use it and reference it in their work with clients on the
Council’s&nbsp;programmes.&nbsp;&nbsp; While Jonathan remembers that the team was pleased to have
succeeded in meeting Richard’s challenge, he recollects that the process of doing so was, at times,
a struggle. In the beginning, Jonathan also couldn’t see how he would use it in his own work: "As a
model for the design process, I thought it was a neat representation, but I have to say that at the
time, apart from presenting it in an almost academic way, I couldn't get my head around using it in
practice. I found I had to rehearse the way I told the Double Diamond story. Once I was comfortable
with it, it became the starting point for any design-orientated conversation, for positioning design
in a broader context, discussing a specific design project, or as the process itself." The
versatility of the Double Diamond is evident in the variety of different ways that it can be used
today, including:&nbsp; For&nbsp;an initial assessment of project type and the approach needed to
address a specific challenge&nbsp; To&nbsp;facilitate&nbsp;a conversation around&nbsp;a number
of&nbsp;projects, to help decide on their priority in terms of relationship, stage or
approach&nbsp;&nbsp; As a tool to help focus teams at the start of a project&nbsp;&nbsp; As a method
for starting to shape the strategy and management of a project&nbsp; To&nbsp;check&nbsp;in on a
project in terms of ‘where we are in the process’&nbsp;&nbsp; To help people get comfortable with
‘going broad and unfocused’ in&nbsp;both of the divergent&nbsp;Discover and Develop phases&nbsp; For
shaping research&nbsp;(for example Design Council’s 2007 research report “11 Lessons on Managing
Design” was structured around the Double Diamond).&nbsp; The people behind the Double Diamond The
Design and Innovation team were perfectly placed to take on this challenge. Originally handpicked by
Clive&nbsp;Grinyer, they were smart, enthusiastic and had permission to experiment. The team’s
diversity of interests, skills and experience proved to be invaluable.&nbsp;The team included, along
with&nbsp;others:&nbsp; Anna White, an ergonomist and product designer, had come from Ford Motor
Company and subsequently went on to work with IDEO&nbsp;and LUMA. Anna is now&nbsp;Design Thinking
Lead for Zeiss Group.&nbsp; Chris Vanstone,&nbsp;a recently graduated product designer,&nbsp;worked
as an independent strategist. He was one of the first people to&nbsp;realise&nbsp;the place and
application of design in public sector services. Chris later joined Design Council’s pioneering Red
Team, which used transformation design to influence policy. He now works with the Australian Centre
for Social Innovation.&nbsp; Gill Wildman’s background was in design strategy and innovation. First
a design manager and then design strategist for Design Council, at the time she was involved with
their science and technology
accelerator&nbsp;programme&nbsp;–&nbsp;Humanising&nbsp;Technology&nbsp;–&nbsp;and exploring service
design as a specialism. Gill has gone on to&nbsp;found&nbsp;her own business,&nbsp;Plot, to hold the
position of joint design chair at Carnegie Mellon University and to work with companies such as
Hyper Island and&nbsp;Upstarter.&nbsp; Jennie Winhall,&nbsp;a Design Council strategist,&nbsp;was
focusing on the place of design in social enterprise. She later joined the Red Team with Chris
Vanstone. Jennie now works as a social innovator and service design consultant,
advising&nbsp;organisations&nbsp;such as The Banff Centre, Rockwool&nbsp;Fonden, Innovation Unit and
The Point People.&nbsp; Jonathan Ball&nbsp;was a product designer by training who had worked across
sectors. He has contributed to the development and delivery of many government-funded
support&nbsp;programmes&nbsp;in the UK and overseas.&nbsp;Jonathan continues to work closely with
Design Council, is an instructor for LUMA Institute, an associate of What Could Be and works with
many other businesses and public sector&nbsp;organisations.&nbsp; Keeping it simple "I think you can
use the Double Diamond to tell all sorts of design stories in really helpful ways. Part of its
beauty is its simplicity." Jonathan Ball The Double Diamond&nbsp;originated from humble beginnings
as the answer to a simple question, but its impact has been far reaching.&nbsp; As Anna White
recalls, it initially formed part of “a drive to demystify design process and make it accessible to
a non-design audience of policy makers, manufacturing CEOs, technologists and educators. What was
nice,&nbsp;though, is that the design world embraced the model as well.”&nbsp; The Double Diamond
today The Double Diamond&nbsp;remains at the heart of Design Council's work today; not just where it
all started as a&nbsp;visualisation&nbsp;of design and innovation process and as a method for
understanding the type and shape of project that is being undertaken, but also at the heart of
the&nbsp; Framework for Innovation ,&nbsp;Design Councils' framework for setting design thinking in
a strategic context. Eleven lessons: Managing design in eleven global brands How do leading
