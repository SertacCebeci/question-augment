Abstract
Can a multimodal LLM predict which version of a web page will win a real A/B test from
screenshots alone? We report the most complete answer we are aware of, from six weeks of
pre-registered experiments on real conversion tests: mostly no — and the exceptions are
precisely identifiable in advance.
Three findings structure the paper. (1) Unconditional winner prediction does not clear
the honesty bar. On 330 real A/B tests, a Gemini 3 Flash judge attains Cohen’s κ = 0.141
[0.034, 0.248] — detectably above chance, but on the trustworthy (statistically significant) half
of the labels the evidence is inconclusive (κ = 0.108 [−0.049, 0.264]). We further show that 44%
of the “ground-truth” labels in our primary corpus — the curated case library of a
leading CRO agency — come from non-significant tests, and that the judge agrees more
with those unreliable labels than with reliable ones — the signature of a shared prior between
label curator and model, not of prediction. Every standard improvement lever fails to move this: a
2.8× more expensive frontier model is statistically equivalent to Flash as the judge (∆κ = −0.037
[−0.152, +0.076], within a ±0.20 TOST bound, n=159 paired); prompt-mechanism redesign,
stimulus fidelity, and change-type priors all fail their pre-registered gates. (2) The judge’s
confident calls are different. Gating predictions on internal panel agreement concentrates
real signal: at a vote-margin ≥ 0.6 gate the judge calls 49% of tests and abstains on the rest,
and on significant-only labels the called subset reaches κ = 0.311 [0.025, 0.555] — the only
operating point that survives our own label-validity audit (exploratory tier; a pre-registered
held-out confirmation at the stricter unanimity point returned κ = −0.100 [−0.200, 0.000] on
an underpowered 33-call cohort, which by our locked decision rule upgrades nothing and kills
nothing; a powered confirmation on partner data is pre-registered and in motion). The abstention
behavior itself is robust: coverage replicates out-of-sample (27.8% → 30.3%), and the gated
calls are perfectly reproducible across independent runs (31/31) — which we show is exactly
why reproducibility must never be mistaken for validity. The mechanism is measurable: judges
differing in model or prompt agree with each other at κ = 0.74–0.88 while each agrees with
outcomes at only ∼ 0.2, and a 16-vote panel carries ≈ 2 effective independent votes. (3) The
economics work. Vote-subsampling shows a 9-vote panel preserves the gated operating point
(κ = 0.264–0.271 at 46% coverage) at roughly half the inference cost of the full panel, and the
proxy tier runs at ∼ $0.02 per evaluation versus ∼ $1.25 for a browser-agent tier.
The deliverable this evidence supports is not a win-rate oracle. It is a calibrated screening
instrument: confident-call-or-abstain winner reads, a grounded mobile-UX audit whose findings

Practical implications for experimentation teams
• Raw accuracy quoted against curated A/B catalogs is a base-rate artifact: the rule always pick the
variant scores ∼75–85% on such catalogs while contributing zero decision value. Evaluations of this
task should report chance-corrected metrics (κ), stratified by statistically significant labels.
• Expert humans are not the bar one might assume: in the largest public test of practitioners (200+
professionals, 8 real A/B tests, prize money on the line), the average was chance. A judge that
knows when it knows is the relevant comparison point for screening layers.
• What this evidence supports deploying: (a) confident-call-or-abstain winner reads — the judge
panel calls a winner only when it clears its confidence gate, and abstains otherwise; (b) screenshotgrounded UX audits whose findings pass an adversarial verification pass before being surfaced; (c)
a tiered claims ledger in which every externally quoted number carries its evidence tier, including
failed experiments.
• What it does not support claiming: predicted conversion-lift percentages, “mobile-validated” accuracy (no mobile-rendered labeled corpus exists, ours or anyone’s), or any unconditional accuracy
claim. The ledger in Appendix A makes this binding.

Introduction

LLM judges are being sold as pre-test oracles for conversion optimization: show the model two page
designs, get tomorrow’s A/B winner today. The pitch is seductive because the evaluation is easy
to game — often unintentionally. Curated A/B-test catalogs are heavily skewed toward variant
wins (teams build variants they expect to win, and curators showcase wins), so a judge that simply
prefers the changed page scores ∼75–85% “accuracy” while contributing zero decision value. The
failure mode of this product category is not weak models; it is unfalsifiable marketing built on
base-rate exploitation.
This paper is our attempt to hold ourselves to a falsifiable standard, in public. Over six weeks
we ran a pre-registered experimental program against real A/B outcomes — locked hypotheses,
locked decision gates, locked prediction files, adversarial verification of our own headline results —
and we report everything: the negative results (most of the program), the one operating point that
survives, its held-out test that came back inconclusive, and the powered confirmation now in motion
on partner data. Three properties distinguish this from a standard vendor whitepaper:
1. Every number carries an evidence tier (§1.1; ledger in Appendix A). “Confirmed” means
pre-registered, powered, and gate-passed. Nothing exploratory is dressed up as confirmed —
including our own headline.
2. The machinery caught our own best results. Our label-validity audit partially invalidated
our strongest unconditional benchmark; our held-out confirmation declined to promote our
strongest gated estimate. Both events are reported here as findings, because a measurement
system that cannot kill its owner’s claims is advertising, not measurement.
2

3. The product claim is calibrated, not maximal. The instrument we ship abstains by design
on ∼half of inputs. We argue — with a frontier of operating points, not a single cherry-picked
threshold — that a small number of high-precision calls plus honest abstention is worth more to
an experimentation program than a confident answer to every question.
1.1

The claims discipline

We tag every quantitative claim with one of six tiers, defined once and used everywhere (including
in our sales materials — the tags are the contract):
• CONFIRMED — pre-registered hypothesis, adequately powered, locked gate passed.
• REPLICATED-DIRECTIONAL — same direction in ≥ 2 independent runs; no single
powered held-out confirmation yet.
• EXPLORATORY — post-hoc, in-sample, or threshold-selected; selection effects not fully
modeled.
• INCONCLUSIVE — a pre-registered held-out test that returned an underpowered null:
upgrades nothing, refutes nothing.
• REFUTED — tested and failed: CI includes zero on trustworthy data, or contradicted by a
stronger design.
• DEMONSTRATED — engineered pipeline behavior, verified end-to-end; not a statistical-skill
claim, carries no CI.

2

Related work

LLM-as-judge and its failure modes. Using LLMs to evaluate artifacts at scale was popularized
by MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena [1] and framing-based evaluators such as G-Eval [2]. The judge
paradigm’s known pathologies — position bias, verbosity bias, self-preference — are documented by
Wang et al. [3] among others; our orientation-swap control (§3) is the standard mitigation, and our
panel design follows the multi-judge direction of Verga et al. [4], though our results add a caution
that panel agreement can be deterministic prior-expression rather than independent evidence (§6.2).
Our task differs from typical judge applications in one decisive way: the ground truth is an external
causal outcome (a randomized experiment’s winner), not a human preference label, so judge–label
agreement cannot be improved by aligning the judge to the labeler’s taste — and shared priors
between judge and labeler become a confound rather than a convenience (§4).
Selective prediction and calibration. Prediction with a reject option dates to Chow [5],
with the modern risk–coverage formulation developed by El-Yaniv and Wiener [6] and brought to
deep networks by Geifman and El-Yaniv [7]. Kamath et al. [8] apply selective prediction to QA
under domain shift; Guo et al. [9] establish that modern networks are systematically miscalibrated.
Our contribution to this line is empirical rather than algorithmic: in a domain where unconditional
skill is statistically unprovable on trustworthy labels, a vote-margin gate over a counterbalanced
panel isolates the subset of inputs where measurable skill exists — and a pre-registered held-out
test of that gate illustrates how easily selective-prediction gains can fail to transfer (§6.2).
Online controlled experiments. The methodology and ground truth of our corpus come
from the A/B-testing literature: Kohavi et al. [10, 11] codify trustworthy experimentation practice,
and Kohavi and Thomke [12] report that even at mature experimentation organizations only
10–20% of expert-designed treatments win — the base rate that makes screenshot-level winner
prediction information-poor. The practitioner-prediction benchmark we use as a human anchor (200+
3

professionals, 8 real tests, mean 2.3/8) is reported in Kohavi’s practitioner materials accompanying
[11].
LLM user simulation. A complementary line uses LLM agents to simulate users rather
than judge outcomes: generative agents [13] and, closest to our setting, UXAgent’s LLM-agent
usability testing for web design [14]. Our production system runs synthetic shoppers in real browsers
in exactly this spirit; this paper isolates the judging question — what the simulation layer can
honestly claim about real experimental outcomes — and supplies the calibration discipline that any
user-simulation product ultimately needs.
Statistical instrumentation. Cohen’s κ [15], percentile bootstrap [16], TOST equivalence
testing [17], and pre-registration [18] are each standard; our assembly of them — locked gates
committed before data, equivalence bounds declared in advance, gate-on-resample intervals for
selective metrics — is, to our knowledge, not yet standard in commercial LLM-evaluation practice,
and Appendix C specifies it precisely so it can be.

3

The Reliability Stack

All results below run through one statistical harness, reused unchanged across experiments:
• Chance-corrected gating. Cohen’s κ [15] is the gate metric everywhere; raw accuracy is
reported but never gates (§4.3 shows why with a worked example where the judge’s most-confident
slice scores 75.8% accuracy and still has negative κ).
• Percentile bootstrap CIs [16], B = 10,000, fixed seed 42; paired joint resampling for any
two-arm contrast on shared test IDs (both arms resampled together per replicate).
• Gate-on-resample for gated metrics. When a confidence gate selects the scored subset, we
resample the full test set and re-apply the gate inside every bootstrap replicate, propagating
coverage uncertainty into the κ CI. Conditioning on the realized called subset — the natural
shortcut — understates the interval.
• Pre-registration with locked gates [18]. Hypotheses, decision rules, power analyses, and
verdict colors (GREEN/YELLOW/RED/INCONCLUSIVE) are committed to the repository
before predictions are collected; prediction files and analysis outputs are locked after.
• Position counterbalancing. Judge panels see both image orders; an orientation-swap control
exposed a content-independent second-image position bias early in the program.
• Trivial baselines as floors. Always-predict-B, pixel-difference counters, and DOM-feature
heuristics run alongside every benchmark.
• Adversarial verification of our own results. Headline findings get an explicit falsification
pass (independent re-derivation from raw votes, per-test diffs against file collisions, label-validity
stratification) before they are reported — this pass is what produced §4 and §6.2.

On trustworthy labels, the evidence for unconditional judge skill is statistically
inconclusive. The direction is the diagnostic part: if non-significant labels were merely noisy, κ
against them would be lower, not higher. A judge that agrees more with unreliable labels than
reliable ones is exhibiting a shared-prior artifact: on marginal tests the provider’s directional call

Implication beyond this paper: any LLM-judge accuracy number computed against a
curated A/B catalog — ours or any vendor’s — inherits this artifact unless it stratifies by label
significance. We recommend the stratified report become standard practice.
4.3

Why κ, with a worked example

On the held-out cohort of §6.2, the judge’s most-confident (unanimous) slice is 93.9% B-truth.
A do-nothing always-B rule scores 93.9% accuracy there; the judge scores 75.8% — and its κ is
−0.100, because it called A six times (all wrong) and missed both real A-winners. High accuracy,
negative skill, on the slice the judge was most confident about. Raw accuracy cannot see this; κ is
built to. Every gate in this program is therefore a κ gate, and no accuracy figure in this paper is
independently significant evidence of anything.

The closed levers: powered negative results

Each “obvious fix” below was tested under a locked pre-registration and failed its gate. We consider
these results load-bearing: they are why the calibrated-abstention operating point (§6) is the product,
rather than a bigger model or a cleverer prompt.
5.1

Model capacity: a frontier model is equivalent, not better (CONFIRMED)

Head-to-head as the judge on the same 159 tests (paired joint bootstrap): Gemini 3 Flash κ =
0.214 [0.054, 0.365] vs. Gemini 3.1 Pro κ = 0.178 [0.024, 0.327];
∆κ(Pro − Flash) = −0.037 [−0.152, +0.076].
The CI includes zero and lies entirely inside the pre-declared ±0.20 TOST-style equivalence bound
[17]: a powered equivalence claim, not an absence-of-evidence shrug. Pro also over-calls A more
(calibration gap 11.3pp vs. 8.8pp) at ∼2.8× the latency and cost. Model capacity is not the
bottleneck on this task.
5.2

Prompt mechanism, stimulus fidelity, and priors (REFUTED)

• Prompt redesign: a calibration-targeted mechanism rewrite (M3) shrank the A-over-prediction
gap (8.8% → 7.0%) but failed κ non-inferiority against the incumbent (0.173 vs. 0.214; paired
∆ = −0.029 [−0.10, +0.04]). Four earlier prompt generations and two GEPA-style automated
optimization chains either failed their holdout gates or won their gate while leaving corpus-level κ
at zero (full ledger in Appendix B, including a cautionary finding: a reflection-LM data leak that
caused candidate prompts to memorize benchmark pattern IDs as lookup tables, caught by a
forensic audit and patched with a validator).
• Stimulus fidelity: judging real captured screenshots vs. reconstructed pages produced parity at
the κ ≈ 0 ceiling on matched tests (∆κ = −0.07, CI includes 0) — fidelity was not the constraint.
• Change-type priors: leave-one-test-out pattern-level priors (“this category of change usually wins”) predict held-out outcomes at κ = 0.015 [−0.098, 0.131], versus the visual judge’s
0.140 [0.021, 0.259] on the same 279 tests. Within-pattern outcome consistency is poor; context
dominates change type. The folk theory underlying most “best-practices” tools fails its own
catalog.
• Mobile: on mobile-segment tests all judge arms sit at κ ≈ 0.00–0.02 (CIs ≈ [−0.20, 0.27]), and no
mobile-rendered, outcome-labeled corpus exists anywhere we could find to do better. We therefore
make no mobile-accuracy claim, and flag any vendor who does. (The mobile capture-and-audit
pipeline is production-validated; the accuracy claim is what’s unavailable.)

The in-sample κ ≈ 0.35 did not directionally replicate on this cohort; the cohort is also far too
small to refute it (power ≈ 0.36–0.59 against a true κ = 0.30). Per the locked rule we report it as
exactly that. Two genuinely new findings came out of the error anatomy:
• Reliability is not validity. The held-out run’s unanimous calls agreed with an independent
earlier run’s calls on 31/31 overlapping tests (different panel size, different context framing) —
perfect test–retest reliability — while scoring κ = −0.100 against truth. Unanimity in an LLM
panel is one deterministic prior expressed many times, not independent evidence accumulating.
We offer this as a general caution for LLM-judge ensembles: agreement metrics cannot certify
skill.
• Confident errors cluster by folk wisdom. The six wrong unanimous calls collapse onto four
change-pattern families, and the flagship miss backed an interactive “gradual engagement” flow
(36/36 votes across two panels) over the plain signup that actually won, significantly. The gate’s
failure mode is exactly the shared-prior artifact of §4, observed item-by-item.
What would settle it: ∼140–180 unanimous calls on real-outcome data the judge has never
seen, from a corpus that does not carry the curated B-prior. A pilot protocol on a commerce
A/B-testing platform partner’s ∼300-test historical dataset is pre-registered and locked as of June 10;
its verdict space and power ceiling are stated in the pre-registration before data receipt.

Two judges that differ in model or in prompt agree with each other three to four times more
strongly than either agrees with real outcomes. The shared-prior account stops being an inference
here and becomes a measurement. Three corollaries:
• A panel vote is not a sample of independent evidence. One-way ICC on the binary votes
grouped by test gives ρ = 0.34–0.51 across the three judges: a 16-vote panel carries neff ≈ 1.9–2.6
effective independent votes. This single number jointly explains why unconditional κ is flat in
panel size, why gated calls reproduce 31/31 across runs (§6.2), and why unanimity must not be
read as accumulating evidence.
• Diversity does not rescue the task. Mixed 8+8 panels (model-diverse or prompt-diverse,
votes drawn hypergeometrically from the recorded tallies) perform within noise of pure panels at
the unanimity gate (e.g., model-diverse κ = 0.337 [0.235, 0.432] at 26% coverage vs. pure-Flash
0.310 at 25%). Juries of correlated jurors are still one juror [4].
• A modest, honest ensemble gain exists. Requiring both judges’ panels to be unanimous lifts
gated κ to 0.39 (model pair) at 21% coverage — diversity buys a little extra precision at the gate,
consistent with mostly-shared-but-not-identical priors. We report it as exploratory context, not a
product claim.
For builders of LLM judge panels and multi-agent simulations, we believe this is the paper’s
most transferable warning: measure inter-judge κ against your judges’ agreement with
ground truth before attributing meaning to consensus. Consensus among correlated judges
is reproducible, persuasive, and — on this task, out of sample — not evidence.
6.4

Abstention behavior itself is stable (DEMONSTRATED)

Across calibration and held-out runs the gate’s coverage replicated (27.8% → 30.3% at unanimity;
49% at margin-0.6), abstentions are explicit (never silent low-confidence answers), and the decision
rule is nine lines of arithmetic over panel votes — auditable by a customer in one sitting.

The human ceiling

κ = 0.31 at half coverage sounds modest until you ask what the human number is. The best public
anchor [11, 12]: Kohavi’s survey of 200+ experimentation practitioners on 8 real A/B
tests, with a prize for 6/8 correct: nobody won, and the average was 2.3/8 (≈29%) —
chance. At Google and Bing only 10–20% of expert-designed experiments win their primary metric;
Microsoft’s published rule of thumb is one-third win, one-third flat, one-third hurt. Screenshot-based
winner prediction appears to be near-impossible for humans too — which reframes a calibrated
judge from “weak oracle” to “a screening instrument that knows which minority of cases are callable
at all.”
The missing number is a human baseline on our corpus, and we now have it. We built
and froze a 50-item instrument (25 A-wins / 25 B-wins, all significant-only labels, presentation
sides counterbalanced, private answer key, “recognized-this-test” exclusions) for source-naı̈ve CRO
practitioners — anyone who has studied the provider’s published case library is excluded, since their
answers would be recall, not prediction; scoring uses the identical κ harness as the judge. Fifteen
qualified practitioners (≥ 2 years running A/B tests) each scored all 50 items (740 answered
after recognized-exclusions).
The result reproduces this paper’s central artifact — in humans. Against the real outcomes
the experts are at chance: pooled κ = −0.03 [−0.24, 0.17] (48.5% accuracy), and a 15-expert
majority vote does not rescue it (κ = −0.04, 24/50 correct — pooling experts moves nothing,
because they share the error). Even the high-confidence calls score κ = −0.01 (49.3%): human
confidence, like the ungated judge’s, does not track truth. Yet the experts agree strongly with
one another: mean pairwise inter-rater κ = 0.53, and a one-way ICC of ρ = 0.54 implies neff ≈ 1.8
effective independent experts out of 15 — the same collapse we measured for LLM panels in
§6.3 (neff ≈ 2 of 16 votes). Fifteen experts sharing CRO folk wisdom are, statistically, fewer than
two independent judgments that happen to be wrong together: consensus without validity, exactly
as in §6.3. (Tier: EXPLORATORY — a first-party measurement; the vs-truth CI is wide at
n=50 items but centered on zero, and the inter-rater collapse is the robust part; harness and raw
responses committed, §11.)
Two consequences. First, the practical human ceiling on this task is chance, now confirmed on
our corpus — so the gated judge’s 0.311 [0.025, 0.555] sits at or above it while abstaining honestly
on the rest. Second, and more general: the shared-prior artifact is not an LLM pathology.
Experts and models encode the same folk theory of what should win, agree with each other, and
are uncorrelated with what did win — the appearance of a page underdetermines its experimental
9

outcome for humans and machines alike. It is the paper’s thesis in its strongest form: on this task,
consensus — human or model — is reproducible, persuasive, and not evidence.

Deployment implications

1. Confident-call-or-abstain winner reads (EXPLORATORY, pending the powered partnerdata confirmation; sold as exactly that). The gate is opt-in, default-off in production until the
confirmation passes; demos run it explicitly flagged.
2. The verified mobile-UX audit (DEMONSTRATED). A 375px-capture pipeline feeds a
structured heuristic audit; every finding then faces an adversarial grounding pass that must
locate it in the pixels before it reaches the customer. In the reference run: 13 findings → 12
verified, 1 auto-rejected as a hallucination. The deliverable is concrete problems with screenshot
evidence — not win probabilities.
3. Synthetic-shopper simulation with honest statistics (DEMONSTRATED as pipeline;
effect claims gated separately). Browser-level agents walk both arms; results report κ-gated

Limitations and threats to validity

• Corpus B-prior. The provider’s catalog is curated ∼76% B; positive κ there is partly corpusstructural. Only real-outcome data (∼50/50 decisive, ∼12% true A-win rate in industry telemetry)
controls this; that confirmation is pre-registered and pending.
• Threshold selection. The margin-0.6 operating point is one of four examined post-hoc; the preregistered held-out test ran at unanimity (locked before the audit) and returned an underpowered
null. We hold the gated claim at EXPLORATORY until the powered run.
• Label overlap. ∼103/109 held-out tests had been scored once by an earlier arm under a different
configuration; the held-out run controls threshold and sampling degrees of freedom, not full label
freshness (disclosed in the pre-registration; quantified at 81.6% overall cross-run agreement).
• Desktop-rendered stimulus. Catalog screenshots are desktop-rendered; mobile-stimulus
accuracy is unmeasurable industry-wide until a mobile-rendered labeled corpus exists.
• Single corpus family. All confirmatory statistics to date derive from one catalog’s distribution;
the partner-data program (§6.2) is the designed escape from that family.
• Conflict of interest. This is a company research paper about the company’s product. The
mitigations are structural: pre-registrations and locked gates committed before data, negative
results published, every claim tiered, and the strongest claims explicitly marked unconfirmed.

Reproducibility and data availability

All statistics use fixed seeds (42 throughout), B = 10,000 percentile bootstraps, and committed
prediction/analysis JSON artifacts; pre-registrations are locked in-repo before data collection, and
every experiment in Appendix B cites its close-out document. The human-baseline raw responses
(responses-2026-07-02.csv), participant metadata, and scoring script are committed; the private
10

answer key is held out (raters must never see it) and is consistent with the anonymized kit
images by construction — the kit builder emits the images and the key in one run from the same
counterbalancing seed. The corpus is archived with per-file content-hash provenance against the
consumption-path URLs (a lesson learned: an earlier archive pass had silently stored thumbnail
artwork for 98.9% of files; the repair and its audit are themselves committed). Data availability: all
third-party test data appears under written permission. The catalog provider requested anonymity
and is identified throughout only as a leading CRO agency; under our agreement the underlying
tests, screenshots, and per-test outcomes cannot be publicly redistributed. We therefore release
the statistical harness, analysis code, pre-registrations, and aggregate artifacts, and will support
verification requests from reviewers under terms consistent with the provider’s agreement. Track-B
partner data likewise appears anonymized absent the partner’s written approval.

Formal evaluation protocol

For exact reproducibility, the estimators and decision rules used throughout:
Agreement metric. For called predictions {(ŷi , yi )}ni=1 with ŷi , yi ∈ {A, B}, Cohen’s κ =
(po − pe )/(1 − pe ) where po is observed agreement and pe = p̂A pA + p̂B pB is chance agreement from
the empirical marginals [15]. κ is invariant to the base rate that inflates raw accuracy on B-heavy
corpora.
Panel and gate. Each test receives m panel votes (m = 16–20) with image order counterbalanced across votes. With c votes for A (control) and v for B (variant), the vote margin is
|c − v|/(c + v). A gate at threshold τ calls the majority arm iff margin ≥ τ and abstains otherwise;
τ = 1.0 is unanimity. Coverage is the called fraction. The deployed decision rule is this gate
verbatim; no other signal enters.
Gate-on-resample confidence intervals. For gated metrics we resample the entire held-out
set with replacement B = 10,000 times (fixed seed 42), re-apply the gate within each replicate, and
compute κ on whatever is called; the 2.5th/97.5th percentiles of the resulting distribution form the
CI [16]. This propagates coverage uncertainty into the interval; conditioning on the realized called
subset (the naive scheme) understates it. Two-arm contrasts on shared test IDs use paired joint
13

resampling (both arms resampled together per replicate).
Equivalence testing. The model-capacity claim uses a TOST-style rule [17]: equivalence
within ±δ (δ = 0.20 κ, declared before scoring) is concluded iff the full 95% CI of ∆κ lies inside
(−δ, +δ). Observed: ∆κ = −0.037, CI [−0.152, +0.076] ⊂ (−0.20, +0.20).
Power and verdict space for the held-out confirmation. The pre-registration committed,
before data collection, to: simulation-based power at three true-κ assumptions (0.25/0.30/0.35)
under the same gate-on-resample scheme used for adjudication; a ∼140-unanimous-call floor for
any “confirmed” verdict (≥ 80% power at κ = 0.30); and an asymmetric verdict space in which
an underpowered cohort can return at most a directional (never confirmatory) positive, and an
underpowered negative is INCONCLUSIVE rather than refuting [18]. The realized cohort (33 calls)
sat below the floor by design feasibility, and the verdict followed the locked rule.
Vote-subsampling for panel economics. Small-panel performance (§7) draws n ∈ {3, 5, 9}
votes per test hypergeometrically (without replacement) from the recorded tallies, valid under the
exchangeable-votes model; 2,000 seeded replicates; bands are percentiles across replicates and reflect
vote-sampling uncertainty only.
