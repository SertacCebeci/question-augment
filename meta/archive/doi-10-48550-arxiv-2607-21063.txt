Abstract

arXiv:2607.21063v1 [cs.CL] 23 Jul 2026

Almost every large language model that reaches
a broad audience is quantized: trained in full
precision, then compressed for efficiency. This
step is assumed harmless and its safety is rarely
re-checked. We find its principal side effect
is increased bias that standard safety evaluation misses. Holding the model, its training, and the prompts fixed, a quantized model
still refuses harmful requests, still avoids overrefusing benign prompts, and still selects the
unbiased multiple-choice answer. Yet asked
an open-ended question, the same model volunteers stereotypes in all eight languages we
probe, in roughly one in four open-ended answers under an independent judge (~24% to
~27% across the compression ladder): it passes
every standard check and still reaches users measurably more biased. The selective gap is a robust finding; whether open-ended bias further
increases with compression is less certain, sensitive to the judge that scores it. We address
both with QuantiBias, a benchmark that pairs
a generative, multilingual stereotype probe with
the refusal and multiple-choice controls that
isolate open-ended generation, contrasts each
build with and without reasoning, and rates the
content severity of what it generates. Across
two backbone models (Qwen and Gemma), a
five-family screen, and eight benchmarks, quantizers allocate their extra precision by capability data that carries no bias-prevention signal,
and reasoning before answering roughly halves
the effect on some families while doing nothing on others. A quantized build must be reevaluated for open-ended bias, not only on the
short-form safeguards it already passes. Code
and baselines: https://huggingface.co/
datasets/emilioferrara/quantibias.

Introduction

A language model is evaluated at full precision and
typically deployed compressed. Post-training quantization shrinks the weights before release, often

by a factor of 3 to 14, so the build a user interacts with is usually not the one the evaluation measured. Quantization is expected to preserve behavior, and capability benchmarks largely bear this out;
the safety and bias side has been probed less systematically. Reports on aggressively quantized builds
tend to document capability retention across benchmarks while saying less about safety or bias.
Two prior results suggest this gap is consequential. The butterfly-effect account of AI bias (Ferrara,
2024) shows that small perturbations to a model or
its training pipeline can produce disproportionate
and hard-to-predict changes in bias. Quantization
is an unusually clean perturbation of this kind: it alters no training data, no objective, and no prompt,
only the numerical precision of the weights. Separately, a multi-dimensional audit of targeted biasreduction methods (Chand et al., 2026) found that
reducing bias on one dimension often shifted it on
dimensions the method did not target, with smaller
models affected more, which the authors attribute
to entangled representations in which no adjustment
stays local. Together these results predict that quantization moves behavior on several axes at once: it
perturbs every weight simultaneously, is directed at
no particular behavior, and, if representations are
entangled, does not confine its effects to the capabilities it preserves.
Prior studies of quantization and safety often disagree, and much of the disagreement follows from
what the standard safety metric examines. Safety
is usually summarized as the rate at which a short
harmful prompt succeeds. That measure averages
over the brief exchanges in which quantization does
the least damage, and never examines the sustained,
open-ended generation in which it does the most;
separating the two shows the short-form safeguards
holding while open-ended generation carries bias
the checks never sample. Two measurement details compound the confusion, and we control both:
a build’s nominal label understates how much it is

This paper makes three contributions. First, we
document selective blindness: across two anchor
backbones, a five-family screen, and eight benchmarks, quantization leaves the safeguards standard
evaluations measure, refusal and multiple-choice
bias avoidance, unchanged, while the bias a model
volunteers in open-ended generation goes unmeasured by them. Second, we give the mechanism
(Section 7): quantizers allocate precision to calibration data that carries no bias signal, leaving the
bias-relevant weights fragile, and we show when reasoning before answering suppresses the effect and
when it does not: reasoning halves the open-ended
bias on Qwen but leaves it unchanged on Gemma.
Third, we build the instrument that detects the gap.
We introduce QuantiBias, a benchmark that pairs
a generative, multilingual stereotype probe with the
refusal and multiple-choice controls that establish
the effect is specific to open-ended generation, contrasts each build with and without reasoning, and
rates the content severity of what a model generates
(Section 4).

2 Related Work
Quantization is known to change model behavior
beyond the capability benchmarks used to validate
it, and its effect on safety and bias has been studied unevenly. Xu et al. (2024) evaluate pruning
and quantization along degeneration harm, representational harm, and dialect bias, and show that
generation-side safety can erode while task accuracy
holds. Closest to our setting, Marcuzzi et al. (2026)
extend this across 13 benchmarks and report that aggressive quantization slightly raises stereotyping and
bias in generative tasks while lowering toxicity and
leaving sentiment flat. Rath and Maliakkal (2026)
find that three-bit quantization turns 6 to 21% of
previously unbiased BBQ items stereotypical, and
Hua et al. (2026) show that such flips often cancel
in aggregate, masking large per-group swings. On
the safety side, Kharinaev et al. (2025) document
alignment degradation across 66 quantized variants,
Egashira et al. (2024) show that quantization can
be weaponized adversarially so the artifact a user
evaluates is not the one they deploy, and Al Hakim
et al. (2026) protect bias-critical weights and find
the damage worst in non-English settings.
We build on these findings and depart from them
in three ways. First, we isolate a selectivity effect that aggregate reporting obscures: the shortform safeguards these evaluations rely on, refusal
and multiple-choice bias avoidance, stay flat while
the bias a model volunteers in open-ended generation rises (Jin et al., 2025), so a build passes the
checks and reaches users more biased. Second, we
test whether reasoning acts as a safeguard and find

3

Measuring True Quantization

A build’s name states a nominal bits-per-weight,
but conventional low-bit builds retain some components, the embeddings, attention, and output head,
at substantially higher precision, and the size of that
hidden margin varies from build to build and vendor to vendor. We therefore measure every model
directly, reading the size and quantization type of
each stored weight tensor and dividing total weight
bytes by parameter count to obtain the effective bits
per weight (bpw). Table 1 gives the two anchor ladders; nominal labels overstate the degree of quantization by 11 to 40% (Figure A3).
Two consequences follow. First, from here on we
plot every result against measured bits per weight,
and the label serves only to identify the artifact. Second, weight precision does not fully specify the treatment: the checkpoint does not determine whether
the model’s matrix multiplications run in 16-bit or
4-bit, so we record the resolved backend for every run and treat the weight measurement as a partial description. The label understates quantization
because the high-precision components are hidden
behind it. In the “2-bit” UD-IQ2_XXS build the
multilayer-perceptron weights sit at 2.14 bits while
the output head and attention tensors carry substantially more (4.53 and 3.55 bits), and the build
mixes nine distinct quantization types; normaliza-

tion tensors stay at full 32-bit precision. This mixing is inherent to the llama.cpp k-quant schemes
these builds use (Gerganov et al., 2023), which,
like activation-aware and error-compensating posttraining quantizers (Frantar et al., 2023; Lin et al.,
2024; Xiao et al., 2023), allocate more bits to
weights they judge sensitive; that allocation is tuned
on capability data and has no reason to track bias.
The end-to-end ternary and one-bit builds, following the trend toward natively low-bit models (Ma
et al., 2024), are the exception, holding perceptron,
attention, and output head at a single flat precision,
which is why their measured value tracks their label
far more closely.

4 QuantiBias
QuantiBias packages the open-ended bias
measurement into a reusable protocol with
four components.
1. A generative, paired, multilingual probe.
Each item is an open-ended request that gives
a model room to volunteer or decline a stereotype, paired with a stereotype-endorsing claim
and its group-swapped counterpart that fix the
axis a judge scores (Section 6). Open generation surfaces bias that multiple-choice instruments miss (Jin et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024).
A parallel-translated variant holds content fixed
while varying only the language. Multilingual
bias suites exist, but each drops one of these
axes: MBBQ is multiple-choice (Neplenbroek
et al., 2024), the Multilingual Holistic Bias set
scores template sentences rather than free generation (Costa-jussà et al., 2023), and SHADES
supplies parallel stereotypes without a compression axis (Mitchell et al., 2025). Our probe is, to
our knowledge, the only one that is open-ended,
parallel across eight languages, and rated for the
harmfulness of what a model volunteers.
2. Scoring against measured effective bpw.
QuantiBias reports every result against the measured precision of a build (Section 3), never its
nominal label, so two builds sharing a label but
differing in real precision are not conflated.
3. A reasoning-load contrast. Each build is evaluated with deliberation enabled and disabled,
because the effect is concentrated on the nondeliberative path and its remediability is familydependent; a single mode hides both.
4. Controls that show the effect is selective.
QuantiBias pairs the generative probe with re-

fusal, over-refusal, and multiple-choice bias
measures. Their flatness separates selective bias
amplification from a general behavioral change.
A build passes QuantiBias when its generative-bias
rate does not rise across its own precision ladder
while the controls stay flat, and fails when generative
bias rises even though the controls do not, the signature this paper documents. We release the probe,
measurement code, and scoring harness.1

Experiments

Instrumentation. Each evaluation cell is one (artifact, benchmark, arm, prompt-pool, decoding) tuple, executed by a checkpointed, idempotent harness that writes verbatim generations to an appendonly store; every reported number traces to a job
identifier and a content hash. Decoding is held
constant across precisions (temperature 0.7, top-p
0.95, fixed token budgets, the model’s own chat template). We score the full-ladder generative probe
with an in-family Qwen3-8B lean judge, which is
fast enough to run at scale, and validate that scoring with two independent judges drawn from different vendors and from families outside the evaluation
set: Claude Sonnet-5 (Anthropic) and Gemini-2.5pro (Google), both fixed frontier models never themselves quantized, so the quantization under test cannot affect the instrument that measures them. The
lean judge’s validated quantity is the binary neutralversus-endorsing distinction, which it recovers at
about 94% against MBTP’s own labeled reference
responses; its absolute endorsement level is judgefamily-dependent and far below what the independent judges assign.2 What we claim is the selective
gap: the short-form safeguards stay flat while openended generation carries a high stereotype rate under every judge we ran. The compression slope of
that open-ended rate is a within-judge comparison
and is not established: re-scoring the reasoning-off
ladder with an independent judge returns a high but
roughly flat profile (0.238, 0.267, 0.266 from Q4 to
one bit), against the rising 0.042, 0.053, 0.078 the
lean judge reports, so we treat the dose-response as

Results

We establish the effect and its structure on the
Qwen3.6-27B anchor, on a second backbone, and
across a five-family screen (build details in Table A2). Every rate carries a Wilson 95% interval.
The safety checks hold flat. Table A3 reports
the safeguard, capability, and MBTP benchmarks
across the full Qwen3.6-27B ladder, from 16 to 1.13
measured bpw. The safeguards standard evaluations
probe do not move: refusal of directly harmful requests stays at 0.98, over-refusal on XSTest near
0.33, and BBQ accuracy under ambiguity near 0.97,
at every quantized rung. The capability anchors
hold through IQ2 and lose only a little at the onebit extreme (MMLU-Redux 0.952 to 0.877), which
separates safety-specific degradation from general
capability loss: from Q4 to IQ2 capability is flat
while generative bias already rises. A short-form
next-token bias control (StereoSet stereotype score,
50 ideal) is flat as well, 58 to 60 across the ladder.
A study measuring only these outcomes would conclude that quantization is safety-neutral. The stable
multiple-choice scores may also be flattered by contamination: if BBQ-style items sit in the training
data, a model can recognize the evaluation format
and answer it well while its open-ended behavior diverges (Balloccu et al., 2024), a defeat device in the
sense of Ferrara (2026).
Open-ended bias is high where the safety checks
stay flat. The safeguards hold; open-ended generation does not. On the Qwen3.6-27B anchor the
model volunteers a stereotype in a substantial fraction of open-ended answers while its refusal, overrefusal, and multiple-choice bias controls sit at their
near-ideal values (Table A3, Figure 2). Under an independent judge that fraction is 0.238, 0.267, and
0.266 across the reasoning-off ladder (per language
in Figure A1), close to one open-ended answer
in four, while the same model’s multiple-choice
bias control stays near 0.97 and its refusal near
0.98 (Limitations), so a build passes every standard
check and still reaches users markedly more biased.
This selective gap is the robust finding: it is present
at every precision and under every judge we ran.

7 Why the Effect Is Selective
The pattern documented above, refusal intact while
open-ended bias worsens as precision falls, is not
specific to these models. It follows from how quantization perturbs a network and how generation reads
out its state. The account is mechanistic, not a
proof; it is consistent with the measurements above.

ment rate is R(b) = ρ(m) Φ − m/σ(b) dm ,
with ρ the distribution of margins over prompts and
Φ the standard normal CDF. This is the same tailprobability object that governs flip rates under Gaussian smoothing (Cohen et al., 2019): a behavior
changes only as fast as noise of scale σ(b) reaches
its margin.
What follows. Under a margin distribution
skewed toward declining, three properties hold,
each of which the results above bear out. (i)
The rate is non-decreasing as precision falls,
dR/dσ ≥ 0. (ii) A behavior whose margins all
exceed some mmin ≫ σ has R(b) ≤ Φ(−mmin /σ),
exponentially small and nearly flat across the ladder,
so selectivity is a property of where a behavior’s
margins lie, not of the size of the perturbation,
which is common to all behaviors. (iii) At the
high-precision end the excess over the clean rate
grows as R(b) − R0 ∝ 2−2b , matching the
dose (Figure A6). Refusing an overtly harmful
request and selecting the unbiased multiple-choice
option are behaviors alignment training reinforces
directly; they sit behind wide margins and survive.
Declining to volunteer a stereotype in open-ended
generation was never a direct training target; it sits
behind a narrow margin and degrades (Tran et al.,
2022; Dam et al., 2023). Current quantizers can
only widen this asymmetry, since they score each
layer on coding and chat data that carries no bias
signal and so have no reason to protect the fragile
weights. Reasoning helps because each committed
token is written to the transcript and conditioned
on thereafter (Guan et al., 2024), letting later
steps correct an error the drift introduced; it fails
when the deliberation never revisits the stereotype
decision, which nothing guarantees.

Frequency and severity are separate axes. The
rate R(b) counts how often the model crosses into
endorsement, not how far past the boundary it goes.
Per-generation harm is the product of this frequency
and the mean severity s̄(b) of an endorsement,
H(b) = f (b) s̄(b), and our results estimate only
the first factor. The same margin model predicts
the second also rises with compression: conditioned
on a crossing, the expected depth grows with the
noise scale σ(b), so a model that endorses more often is predicted to endorse more decisively, with total harm non-decreasing and its sensitivity concentrated on the near-boundary behaviors alignment
never reinforced (Appendix C). This severity channel is a prediction, not a result: our current instrument scores frequency alone. Measuring s̄(b) calls
for a graded severity rubric rather than the binary
lean, in the spirit of continuous stereotype-strength
measures (Liu, 2024; Sheng et al., 2019); no standard toxicity classifier captures a politely worded
stereotype endorsement (Hartvigsen et al., 2022), so
the rubric is scored by an independent judge (Souly
et al., 2024) and controlled for generation coherence
(Xu et al., 2024). QuantiBias reserves this as its
severity extension.

8 Conclusions
Quantization leaves a model’s short-form safety behavior almost untouched while its open-ended generation grows markedly more biased. Across the
Qwen3.6-27B ladder the refusal, over-refusal, and
multiple-choice bias controls hold near their fullprecision values at every rung, yet an independent
judge flags a stereotype in close to one open-ended
answer in four. This selective gap is robust across
judges and families; whether the open-ended rate
also rises with compression is a separate question we
leave open. A build that passes every safety check
can still reach users markedly more biased; a quantized model needs re-evaluation on open-ended generation, not short-form scores alone.

Limitations
The effect is clear; the ways it could be sharpened
are specific and named, in order of how much they
matter. (1) The full-sample bias results rest on a single in-family judge; on the controlled parallel probe
we ran a three-family panel (Section 5), and the
two independent judges agree closely (α = 0.69)
while the in-family judge is uncorrelated with both,
which shows absolute bias levels are judge-familydependent, consistent with known LLM-as-judge
pitfalls of position and self-preference bias (Zheng
et al., 2023; Panickssery et al., 2024; Wataoka et al.,
2024). Label instability of this kind is not confined to model judges: human harm annotations are
themselves inconsistent enough that filtering highvariance annotators flips majority harm classifications for 18.6% of prompts (Ghafouri et al., 2026).
The full-sample diagonal cells must therefore be rescored with the independent ensemble before any
single level is relied on. That pilot spans two middle rungs at 80 items each and does not yet reach
the one-bit rung where the diagonal slope concentrates, so it does not by itself resolve the slope on
the controlled instrument, which a full-ladder parallel run would settle. (2) The full-sample evidence
rests on one backbone; the second-backbone replication and the independent CEB instrument point
the same way but are underpowered. (3) Power analysis against the observed effect size (Cohen’s h ≈
0.15) indicates several hundred to above one thousand paired prompts per cell are required to resolve
the per-family slope; the prompt pools are being expanded accordingly. (4) Activation precision is held
constant here and is a second treatment dimension
this stage does not vary; it requires hardware unavailable on the current cluster. (5) The screening
probe is sensitive to the fast path by design (reasoning off); families whose degradation lives elsewhere
would be missed. (6) The multilingual probe is a
diagonal design in which the language of presentation (Yong et al., 2023), national target, and content are entangled, so any between-language comparison is confounded; the within-language quantization slopes reported here do not rest on that comparison, which a parallel-translated instrument that
holds content fixed would remove.

Ethics Statement
This work studies a safety and bias failure mode
in deployed models and introduces an evaluation,
not an attack. QuantiBias quotes model-produced

stereotype text for measurement; we mark such content with warnings and select it deterministically
rather than by hand. Harmful-prompt benchmarks
(StrongREJECT) are used only to measure refusal,
under standard responsible-disclosure practice. The
study involves no human subjects; all data are model
generations over public benchmarks. The intended
use of QuantiBias is to re-evaluate quantized builds
for bias before release, closing a gap that current
short-form safety testing leaves open.

The flagship probe. MBTP is a multilingual generative stereotype benchmark. Each item is an openended request paired with a stereotype-endorsing claim and its group-swapped counterpart, which fix the
axis a judge scores without constraining the model’s answer. We freeze a 320-item subset, balanced across
categories, spanning eight languages (Arabic, Chinese, English, French, German, Japanese, Russian, Spanish) and 13 social categories, with a fixed seed and a frozen prompt order. The reasoning-load contrast is
applied at run time (deliberation on versus off), not baked into the prompt, so the same frozen item serves
both conditions. The parallel-translated variant is built by rendering 40 canonical English items into the
other seven languages with a frontier model and back-translating every field for a gross-drift check, holding
content fixed so that only the language of presentation varies.
Models. Table A2 gives the two precision ladders that carry the central claim and the five-family screen
that tests its breadth. The anchor is a single 27B backbone quantized down five rungs from 16 to about 1.1
measured bits per weight, holding architecture fixed so that each rung isolates the cost of precision. The
second backbone repeats the top of the ladder on a different family. The screen spans six separate model
families at three nominal precisions each, chosen to separate two axes a single number conflates: a family’s
baseline bias level and its sensitivity to compression.

C.1

Rarely-reinforced behaviors collapse first

A rate-constrained code spends its bits where the signal is strong. Behaviors reinforced rarely in training
carry a low-amplitude weight signal and are dropped first as precision falls. This is reverse water-filling
(Shannon, 1959; Cover and Thomas, 2006), the mechanism behind the pruning-as- compression result of
Isik et al. (2022) and the information-bottleneck view that a rate-constrained representation discards the
least task-relevant information first (Tishby et al., 1999; Tishby and Zaslavsky, 2015). It is the formal
counterpart of the empirical finding that compression forgets the long tail (Hooker et al., 2019, 2020).
Assumption 1 (behavior sources). On the behavior-relevant weight subspace the weights decompose along
an orthonormal basis {uk } with coordinate wk = ⟨W, uk ⟩; behavior k’s margin is a strictly increasing
function of wk . Across contexts wk is zero-mean with variance λk (its signal power), and λk = Λ(fk ) is
strictly increasing in the reinforcement frequency fk of behavior k.

and maximal distortion. The collapsed set {k : fk < Λ−1 (θ(R))} is exactly the least-frequently reinforced
behaviors and grows monotonically from the bottom of the frequency ranking as precision falls.
