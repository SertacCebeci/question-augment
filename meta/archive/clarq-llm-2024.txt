ClarQ-LLM: A Benchmark for Models Clarifying
and Requesting Information in Task-Oriented Dialog

arXiv:2409.06097v2 [cs.CL] 14 Sep 2024

Yujian Gan, Changling Li, Jinxia Xie, Luou Wen, Matthew Purver, Massimo Poesio

Abstract—We introduce ClarQ-LLM, an evaluation framework
consisting of bilingual English-Chinese conversation tasks, conversational agents and evaluation metrics, designed to serve as a
strong benchmark for assessing agents’ ability to ask clarification
questions in task-oriented dialogues. The benchmark includes
31 different task types, each with 10 unique dialogue scenarios
between information seeker and provider agents. The scenarios
require the seeker to ask questions to resolve uncertainty and
gather necessary information to complete tasks. Unlike traditional
benchmarks that evaluate agents based on fixed dialogue content,
ClarQ-LLM includes a provider conversational agent to replicate
the original human provider in the benchmark. This allows
both current and future seeker agents to test their ability to
complete information gathering tasks through dialogue by directly
interacting with our provider agent. In tests, LLAMA3.1 405B
seeker agent managed a maximum success rate of only 60.05%,
showing that ClarQ-LLM presents a strong challenge for future
research 1

I. I NTRODUCTION
Task-oriented dialogues have always been a hot topic in
the research on dialogue systems [1]–[3]. The typical scenario
for these dialogues involves an interaction between a helper
agent that needs to acquire information in order to help
(referred to as ‘(information) seeker’ for simplicity) and
a user who interacts with the helper/seeker to carry out a
task (referred to as ‘(information) provider’). In this setting,
the seeker must engage in conversation with the provider to
gather the required information to complete a task, such as
making hotel reservations [4], navigation [5], and others. This
process often requires asking clarification questions 2 , due
to the potentially complex nature of these tasks, the seeker’s
possible lack of crucial details about them and/or the ambiguity
or incompleteness of the input offered by the provider (see
e.g. [7]).

ClarQ-LLM requires seekers to ask clarification questions to
accurately understand the provider’s intentions: it is designed to
assess the ability of LLM seeker agents to identify uncertainties
in dialogues and eliminate them through clarification questions.
As LLMs have evolved to generate fluent and precise language,
persisting in the use of traditional fluency and match-based
evaluation metrics to assess their context-specific responses
appears increasingly inadequate. For example, as illustrated
in Figure 1, the response from S2 to the same sentence
significantly differs from that of the human seeker (S1). When
evaluated using traditional metrics such as BLEU or dialogue
state tracking, the S2 seeker agent’s response would likely
be assessed negatively. Nevertheless, ClarQ-LLM contends
that the S2 seeker agent’s response is not problematic, despite
its complete divergence from the human seeker’s. Since the
customer did not specify the types of pizza and bread desired,
inquiring about the type of pizza or bread first seems acceptable.
ClarQ-LLM utilizes the success rate of the dialogue tasks
as one of the metrics to evaluate the seeker. The success of a
dialogue task hinges on the seeker agent’s ability to gather all
necessary information from the provider agent. For instance,
in the dialogue tasks depicted in Figures 1 and 2, the seeker
must obtain crucial details from the provider agent, such as
the pizza’s type and size, as well as the bread type. The
human seeker in Figures 2 successfully obtains these by asking
several questions. Conversely, even though the response of the
S3 seeker agent in Figure 1 correctly guesses the customer’s
needs, it is still considered incorrect. Because the customer in
Figure 1 did not specify the exact items desired, the seeker
agent should not make assumptions but should instead ask
clarification questions to understand the customer’s true needs.
Even if the seeker agent in this example fortuitously guessed
correctly, reliance on such guesswork cannot ensure consistently
accurate responses; thus, ClarQ-LLM deems it incorrect.
The provider agent in ClarQ-LLM can capture the genuine
needs of human providers and simulate conversations with the
seeker agent, acting as human providers would. This ensures
that regardless of whether the seeker agent inquires about
bread or pizza first, the provider agent can deliver appropriate
responses. Upon concluding the dialogue, we determine the
success of the seeker agent by checking if it has gathered all
necessary information from the provider agent. Experimental
results indicate that, even with the use of state-of-the-art models
like GPT-4o and LLAMA3.1-405B, the success rates of LLMbased seekers on ClarQ-LLM are only 50.8% and 60.5%,
respectively. These figures are significantly lower than the 85%
success rate achieved by human seekers. This indicates that
our tasks pose a significant challenge for future research.

seeker agent and the provider agent. ClarQ-LLM includes
different conversational tasks, evaluation metrics, and provider
agents based on GPT-4o or LLAMA3.1-405B. ClarQ-LLM
is designed primarily to evaluate the seeker agent, not the

II. R ELATED W ORK
A. Task-Oriented Dialogues
Task-oriented dialogue focuses on conducting conversations
to complete a specific task. Some task-oriented dialogues
are accompanied by a task of dialogue state tracking, which
requires the model to understand the user’s needs and update the dialogue state as the conversation progresses, while
also generating reasonable natural language responses to the
user [11], [12]. With the advancement of dialogue system
technology, researchers have shifted their focus from previous
single-domain dialogue datasets to multi-domain task-oriented
dialogue datasets [4], [13]–[15].
However, there are not many task-oriented dialogue datasets
focusing on clarification questions [16]. The recent datasets
ShARC [8] and ClariT [9] contain clarification questions that
only require a "yes" or "no" response, and the knowledge points
needing clarification are usually listed in advance, making the
dialogue task appear relatively simple. Beyond the domain
of task-oriented dialogue, Aliannejadi et al. [17] propose a
dataset and task for clarification in chat. However, it is not
suitable for our purposes, as it focuses solely on informationseeking. Therefore, we believe it is crucial to introduce a more
challenging dataset that, like earlier datasets, facilitates the
gathering of needed information to accomplish tasks through
the process of asking clarification questions. What sets it apart
is the flexibility in responses to these clarification questions,
alongside the model’s need to be able to ask other types of
questions for more information.

A. Overview
ClarQ-LLM encompasses a diverse range of dialogue tasks,
each accompanied by its background and conversations between
a human seeker and provider. All these tasks were written
by four PhDs and graduate students over a total of 700
person-hours. In the ClarQ-LLM conversations, the provider
intentionally uses language filled with uncertainties based on
the task background, prompting each conversational task to
contain multiple pieces of information that seeker agents must
clarify through clarification questions. To ensure diversity, we
have designed 31 different types of tasks, including common
travel planning, specialized electrical engineering, and even
fictional magical dialogues. For each type of task, there are
10 unique task instantiations, culminating in a total of 310
task instantiations3 . We’ve organized these 31 types into two

C. Human Provider Response Tree
ClarQ-LLM encompasses not only dialogue tasks, backgrounds, and content but also annotates human provider
responses in a tree structure. This helps provider agents to more
accurately simulate human providers from the original task.
Figure 3 displays the tree-structured annotations of provider
responses for the dialogue tasks in Figures 1 and 2. This topdown tree structure clearly delineates the logical sequence in
which the provider should offer information to the seeker. For
example, the tree indicates that the provider initially informs
the seeker of the information at node 1, which introduces three
uncertainties: the type and size of the pizza, and the type of
bread, with uncertainties resolved by provider responses at
nodes 2, 3, and 5, respectively. This means that the seeker
needs to analyze the uncertainties in the content of node 1 and

JOURNAL OF LATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021

pose clarification questions accordingly. Since the uncertainty
at node 4 is related to node 2, we place node 4 after node
2. This ensures that the dialogue for node 4, which involves
determining the sauce type, only occurs after node 2 where
Pepperoni Pizza is mentioned. This stipulation is because a
breakfast pizza does not require choosing a sauce, as detailed
in the conversation background in Figure 1.
It’s important to note that this tree is intended primarily
to ensure the logical correctness of the provider; it does
not impose any constraints on the seeker. The seeker is free
to question the provider based on their own judgment. For
example, some seekers might overlook the information that the
provider requested a pizza, and then ask, “How many pizzas do
you need?” In this situation, the provider agent will inform the
seeker again. For details about how the provider agent works
with this tree, please refer to Section IV.
D. Annotation Steps
We develop all tasks in five steps, involving around 700
hours of human labor in total:
Step 1: Creating Initial Dialogues Between the Seeker and
Provider. We assembled a team consisting of four college
students tasked with writing the dialogues in Chinese. The
diverse composition of the team contributed to a greater variety
of tasks.
Step 2: Improving the Drafts. After completing the initial
versions, we identified many errors and illogical parts. A postdoctoral researcher meticulously reviewed each task to correct
any issues, ensuring that everything was logical and adhered to
the principles of data annotation. Simultaneously, he annotated
the provider’s responses into a tree.
Step 3: Reviewing the Chinese Version. Next, another
undergraduate checked the Chinese data for anything that might
be missing or unclear, didn’t make sense, contained typos, or
whether the provider response tree was correctly organized.
Step 4: Translation and Cultural Adaptation. After finalizing
the Chinese version, we utilized GPT-4 to translate it into
English. A bilingual student reviewed the translation to correct
any inaccuracies. Then, a PhD student, who is a native English
speaker, evaluated the dialogues to ensure they were clear,
logical, and consistent with Western cultural expectations.
Step 5: Testing with Real Conversations. Once everything
was reviewed and fixed, we asked some students who hadn’t
seen the data before to talk with the provider agent model.
This was to see how well the model worked and if the way
we organized the provider’s responses were right. After these
tests, we had our final conversation tasks.
IV. P ROVIDER AGENT
A. Overview
To provide future seeker models with a dialogue partner without relying on human providers, we developed a provider agent
based on the state-of-the-art models GPT-4o or LLAMA3.1405B. This provider agent can simulate human providers,
understand the seekers’ language, and provide appropriate
responses. Unlike seeker agents, who can talk freely, the

4

provider agent aims to replicate the human provider in ClarQLLM as closely as possible. To this end, we have marked the
responses of the human provider as tree structures to help the
provider agent replicate the needs, language, and logic of the
human provider.
For example, based on the tree in Figure 3, the provider
agent will first inform the seeker of the content of node 1 just
like the human provider would: “I’d like a garlic bread and a
pizza, please.” In this way, the provider agent introduces the
same three uncertainties as the human provider: the type and
size of the pizza, and the type of bread. Afterwards, the seeker
can generate its questions based on its analysis. If the question
posed by the seeker can be answered using the content of
nodes 2, 3, and 5, the provider agent will generate responses
based on the content of these nodes. However, the seeker may
illogically asks the provider agent after the provider’s node 1
response: ‘Would you prefer BBQ sauce or Chilli Sauce?’4
or even pose an unrelated question like ‘How’s the weather
today?’. Faced with such questions, the provider agent will
automatically generate a sensible reply to alleviate the seeker
agent’s concerns and steer them back to the dialogue task (this
will be further discussed in the next subsection).
B. Ensuring Valid Responses
The provider agent cannot freely generate responses; otherwise, it may produce invalid responses, leading to unfair
comparisons between seeker models. To prevent the generation
of invalid sentences, we have imposed restrictions on the
response generation. First, we categorize the responses into
two types: Response I (responses from the human provider
response tree) and Response II (responses generated based on
the conversation context).
For Response I, which contains information necessary for
completing the task, we require that Response I retain the
original text from the human provider. However, additional
content can be added before or after the original text to make the
conversation flow more smoothly. Since the sentences generated
by the agent include the human’s original text, this ensures that
the provider delivers accurate and complete information to the
seeker, and all seekers receive consistent original information.
For Response II, which does not contain information necessary
for completing the task, this type of response does not affect
the judgment of task success. The provider agent only needs
to generate Response II when Response I cannot address the
seeker’s request. For example, in the conversation shown in
Figure 1, suppose the seeker asks: “A 10-inch pizza is quite
large; I’m worried you won’t finish it. Are you sure you want
to order it?” For this task-irrelevant question, we cannot find
an appropriate response in the human provider text, so the
provider will generate a reasonable response based on the
previous conversation with the seeker, reassuring the seeker
and guiding them back to the task. The Response I ensures
the consistency of information, while the Response II ensures
that the seeker is guided back to the conversation task if they

VII. C ONCLUSIONS
In this paper, we present a new benchmark called ClarQLLM, specifically designed to test how well LLMs can
handle task-oriented dialogues with clarification questions as
information seekers. ClarQ-LLM provides a new set of reliable
tools, including evaluation metrics and a provider agent, both
of which can measure improvements in future models. Our
findings indicate that even the state-of-the-art LLMs have yet to
reach human levels of performance on these tasks, underscoring
a substantial opportunity for enhancing the current models.

J. Findings from Experiments
Through these experiments, we have identified four major
shortcomings of LLMs: (1) inadequate ability to analyze and
reason about uncertainties in Chat mode, (2) a tendency to
forget previous dialogue content, even when it is included in
the prompt, (3) a preference for generating longer responses
compared to humans, and (4) a tendency to ask more questions
than necessary to resolve information: as models like GPT4o and Llama3.1-405B-Instruct improve in reasoning, their
D values have also increased, though not as drastically as
Llama2-70B-Chat, but still significantly higher than those of
humans. We speculate that these shortcomings are related to
the instruction fine-tuning of LLMs, where instructions differ
significantly from dialogue. This discrepancy leads LLMs to
overlook logical connections in conversations. While some
LLMs, like Llama2-70B-Chat, are fine-tuned on dialogue data,
this fine-tuning focuses on making the generated language
more conversational and may not include many analytical
and reasoning tasks. As a result, there is likely a decline in
analytical and reasoning abilities in Chat mode. We believe
that enhancing reasoning abilities within conversational content
