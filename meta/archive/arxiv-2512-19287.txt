Abstract
We introduce Vibe Reasoning, a human-AI collaborative paradigm for solving complex mathematical problems. Our key insight is that frontier AI models already possess the knowledge required to solve challenging problems—they simply do not know how, what, or when
to apply it. Vibe Reasoning transforms AI’s latent potential into manifested capability
through generic meta-prompts, agentic grounding, and model orchestration. We demonstrate this paradigm through IMO 2025 Problem 6, a combinatorial optimization problem
where autonomous AI systems publicly reported failures. Our solution combined GPT-5’s
exploratory capabilities with Gemini 3 Pro’s proof strengths, leveraging agentic workflows
with Python code execution and file-based memory, to derive both the correct answer (2112)
and a rigorous mathematical proof. Through iterative refinement across multiple attempts,
we discovered the necessity of agentic grounding and model orchestration, while human
prompts evolved from problem-specific hints to generic, transferable meta-prompts. We
analyze why capable AI fails autonomously, how each component addresses specific failure modes, and extract principles for effective vibe reasoning. Our findings suggest that
lightweight human guidance can unlock frontier models’ mathematical reasoning potential.
This is ongoing work; we are developing automated frameworks and conducting broader
evaluations to further validate Vibe Reasoning’s generality and effectiveness.

Introduction

1.1

Background: IMO 2025 Problem 6

The International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO) 2025 featured an exceptionally challenging
Problem 6:
IMO 2025 Problem 6
Consider a 2025 × 2025 grid of unit squares. Matilda wishes to place on the grid some
rectangular tiles, possibly of different sizes, such that each side of every tile lies on a grid
line and every unit square is covered by at most one tile.
Determine the minimum number of tiles Matilda needs to place so that each row and each
column of the grid has exactly one unit square that is not covered by any tile.
This problem proved extremely difficult: only 6 out of approximately 600 human contestants
solved it correctly [8], and public reports indicate that all AI systems failed to score points on
this problem [4, 11, 5].
1

IMO 2025 P6 is particularly valuable as an AI capability benchmark because it simultaneously
challenges multiple dimensions where current AI systems struggle:
Computational intractability meets limited data. Natural language reasoning cannot
discover the solution since the search space is too large for mental enumeration and for context
windows [7]. Yet even with code execution, exhaustive search becomes infeasible for rather small
n (e.g., n = 16), leaving only a handful of data points for pattern recognition. Furthermore,
recognizing that perfect squares hold the most promising key requires selective focus that current
AI systems lack.
Construction and proof duality. The problem demands both (1) finding the optimal answer
through creative construction, and (2) proving that no better solution exists. These require
fundamentally different reasoning modes—exploratory pattern-finding versus rigorous logical
deduction. Not many competition problems demonstrate both difficulties simultaneously.
Geometric intuition is essential. The problem has rich visual structure: the “residue block”
pattern in optimal permutations, the geometric interpretation of longest increasing/decreasing
subsequences (LIS/LDS) as coordinate axes in the grid, and the spatial reasoning needed for
Fooling Set construction. Current language models lack native visual/geometric intuition [14].
Deep mathematical knowledge required. The lower bound proof requires connecting the
problem to the Erdős-Szekeres theorem, which lies in the tail of standard mathematical knowledge distributions, as well as the Fooling Set framework from communication complexity that
require broad mathematical training.

1.2

Our Contribution

We introduce Vibe Reasoning, a human-AI collaborative paradigm with a clear goal: minimize
human intervention to generic meta-prompts while AI performs the substantive reasoning. Our
key insight is that frontier AI models already possess all the knowledge required to solve even
the most challenging problems—they simply do not know how, what, or when to apply this
knowledge. Vibe Reasoning can elicit AI’s latent capabilities by providing lightweight human
guidance and transform the reasoning protential into actual problem-solving ability.
The approach combines (1) AI as primary reasoner; (2) Socratic meta-prompts—generic
directives like “verify with code” that prompt AI reflection without revealing solutions; (3)
agentic workflows—Python execution for computation/verification and file-based memory for
context persistence; and (4) model orchestration—deploying different models for exploration
versus proof based on their complementary strengths.
Using this methodology, we successfully solved IMO 2025 P6, obtaining both the correct answer
(2112) and a complete mathematical proof. Through iterative refinement across multiple attempts totaling over 24,000 lines of interaction traces, we discovered the necessity of catching
errors through code verification, maintaining context through file-based memory and matching model strengths to task phases, while simultaneously, our human prompts evolved from
problem-specific hints toward generic, transferable meta-prompts. We document this evolution
and extract key lessons about AI capabilities, human roles, and effective collaboration principles.

The Vibe Reasoning Framework

2.1

Definition

Definition 1 (Vibe Reasoning). A human-AI collaborative problem-solving paradigm where:
• AI performs substantive reasoning: autonomously generating exploration, construction, proof, and verification.
• Humans provide meta-cognitive guidance: based on intuition rather than domain
expertise, sensing when to verify, when to pivot, and when to declare success.
• Generic meta-prompts: human contributions are domain-agnostic and transferable:
“verify with code,” “try small cases,” “this approach isn’t promising”.
The term “vibe” captures the essence of AI-human collaboration and the intuitive, non-expert
nature of human guidance. For example, when a human says “this feels incomplete,” they provide
a meta-cognitive signal, not a specific mathematical fix.

2.2

Framework Components

Vibe Reasoning combines four pillars, illustrated in Figure 1:
(1) AI as primary reasoner: the AI autonomously explores the problem space and generates
hypotheses, rather than being guided step-by-step.
(2) Socratic meta-prompts: humans provide generic directives like “verify with code” that
prompt reflection without revealing solutions. Table 1 categorizes these transferable prompts.
(3) Agentic grounding: Python execution for computation/verification catches hallucinations
by testing conjectures and validating constructions. File-based memory compensates for limited
context windows, enabling coherent multi-session reasoning.
(4) Model orchestration: deploying different models for different subtasks. For instance,
GPT-5 for exploration and construction, Gemini 3 Pro for rigorous proof.

I’ll use the Fooling Set (or Cross-Free Set) method:
Definition: A set S of cells is a Fooling Set if for any two cells (r1 , c1 ), (r2 , c2 ) ∈ S,
the rectangle [r1 , r2 ] × [c1 , c2 ] contains at least one “hole” (uncovered cell).
Key Lemma: If S is a Fooling Set, then any valid tiling requires at least |S| rectangles,
since no rectangle can cover two cells in S.
√
Strategy: Construct a Fooling Set of size n + 2 n − 3.

3.3

Case Study Summary: Vibe Reasoning in Practice

The IMO P6 solution demonstrates how all four pillars of Vibe Reasoning work together:
AI as primary reasoner. GPT-5 discovered the residue block pattern and conjectured the
formula; Gemini 3 Pro identified the Fooling Set framework and constructed the proof. The
human never provided mathematical content.
Socratic meta-prompts. Human guidance addressed what (focus on perfect squares), how
(verify with code, visualize constructions), and when (switch from exploration to proof). All
prompts were generic and transferable.
Agentic grounding. Python execution enabled small-case enumeration, formula validation,
construction visualization, and proof verification. Without code, the AI would have accepted
incorrect conjectures.
Model orchestration. GPT-5’s exploratory strength found the answer; Gemini 3 Pro’s rigorous strength proved the bound. Neither model alone could have achieved the complete solution.
The contrast between autonomous failure (Figure 3) and collaborative success illustrates that
Vibe Reasoning can provide AI’s missing guidance through lightweight, generic meta-prompts.

Discovering the Need for Agentic Grounding

work in automated frameworks.

4.4

Summary: Why Autonomous AI Fails

The methodological improvements above were hard-won through failure. Each lesson corresponds to a specific AI weakness that the problem exposed:
12

The Knowledge-Application Gap. AI models possess extensive mathematical knowledge—
they can state the Erdős-Szekeres theorem and describe Fooling Set constructions. Yet when
solving IMO P6 directly, they fail. The problem is not missing knowledge but missing judgment
about when and how to apply it.
Overconfidence and Verification Blindness. Models present incorrect answers (e.g., M (n) =
2n − 2) with high confidence, lacking self-awareness to recognize when claims need verification.
They rarely spontaneously verify claims with code. Even when verification is possible, they default to reasoning-based confirmation sharing the same blind spots as the original claim. Once
a formula is proposed, they defend rather than question it.
Circular Proof Attempts. In proof phases, AI cycled through variations of the same failed
approaches (guard cells → quadrant coloring → parity augmentation) without recognizing the
need for fundamentally different strategies. They lack meta-cognitive insight to evaluate the
overall proof strategy and pivot when necessary.
Context Loss and Exploration-Rigor Trade-off. Without persistent memory, insights from
failed attempts are lost across sessions. Models excelling at creative exploration may struggle
with formal proofs, while rigorous provers sometimes miss exploratory insights.
The Core Issue: Unreliable Self-Evaluation. All these failure modes share a common root:
AI cannot reliably evaluate its own outputs. The same model expresses identical confidence for
correct and incorrect answers. Without external grounding such as code execution or human
judgment, AI cannot distinguish insight from hallucination. This explains why human guidance,
even minimal and non-technical, proves essential: it provides the independent evaluation that
AI lacks.

Discussion

Section 4.4 identified why autonomous AI fails on complex problems like IMO P6. We now
discuss how each pillar of Vibe Reasoning addresses these challenges, following the framework
structure from Section 2.2.

5.1

Pillar 1: AI as Primary Reasoner

Vibe Reasoning fundamentally depends on sufficiently capable foundation models. The AI must
be the primary reasoner —autonomously exploring, hypothesizing, and constructing—rather
than being guided step-by-step. Several capabilities proved essential:
Pattern Recognition: The ability to observe patterns in small-n enumeration results requires
strong inductive reasoning.
Mathematical Knowledge Association: Connecting the problem to relevant theorems, such
as recognizing that the Erdős-Szekeres theorem provides exactly the bound needed, requires
broad mathematical training.
Code Competence: Writing correct enumeration and verification code is non-trivial; early
traces show that buggy code led to incorrect conclusions.
Weaker or insufficiently trained models cannot support Vibe Reasoning. They lack the baseline
capabilities to generate meaningful hypotheses or execute correct verification. This represents a
prerequisite rather than a contribution of our methodology.

5.2

Pillar 2: Socratic Meta-Prompts and Human Guidance

As Section 4.4 established, AI’s core limitation is unreliable self-evaluation. The human’s role is
to provide the independent judgment that AI lacks—not domain expertise, but meta-cognitive
guidance on how, what, and when to apply AI’s existing knowledge.
5.2.1

How: Out-of-Distribution Guidance

When AI encounters situations outside its training distribution, it defaults to familiar but inappropriate patterns. Humans recognize these moments and redirect:
• Recognizing special structure: When AI fitted linear patterns to all n, the human
prompted “focus on perfect squares since 2025 = 452 .” This out-of-distribution insight was
invisible to the AI but obvious to the human examining the data.
• Choosing representations: The human recognized that LIS and LDS could serve as
natural “coordinate axes” for the Fooling Set construction. The AI persistently tried
row/column indices, requiring correction.
• Breaking fixation: When AI circled through failed proof approaches (“guard cells,”
“quadrant coloring,” “parity augmentation”), the human prompted a fundamentally different direction rather than variations on the same theme.
5.2.2

What: Search Space Pruning and Phase Transitions

AI can explore vast search spaces but struggles to prioritize. The human provides crucial filtering:
• Selecting promising directions: From AI’s enumeration of small cases, the human
identified which patterns merited deeper investigation (residue blocks) versus dead ends
(symmetric constructions).
• Deciding phase transitions: The judgment that “we have the answer, now focus on
proof” requires recognizing when exploration should yield to verification. AI lacks reliable
confidence calibration for such decisions.
• Model selection: Recognizing that GPT-5 excels at exploration while Gemini 3 Pro
excels at rigorous proof, and switching at the right moment are meta-level decisions beyond
current AI capability.
5.2.3

When: Timing of Interventions

Knowing when to intervene is perhaps the subtlest human contribution:
• Letting productive exploration continue: Not every AI tangent should be interrupted. The human must judge when to let AI pursue a direction versus when to redirect.
• Recognizing diminishing returns: After three failed proof attempts using similar techniques, the human recognized the need for a fundamentally different approach.
• Cross-session coherence: Unlike humans who maintain context across days, AI starts
fresh each session. The human ensures insights from failed attempts inform later ones
through structured notes and strategic prompting.
Can these human judgments be automated? The core obstacle, as analyzed in Section 4.4, is that
AI lacks reliable self-evaluation. Beyond this, meta-cognitive oversight requires independence:
using AI to verify AI suffers from correlated errors, as both may share the same blind spots.
The human provides genuinely independent assessment that current AI architectures cannot
replicate. However, some aspects may be automatable in future work through roll-out strategies
14

(autonomous exploration of multiple special cases) or ensemble verification (using diverse models
to cross-check claims).

5.3

Pillar 3: Agentic Grounding through Python Execution

Python code execution was not merely helpful but essential for success. As Section 4.4 showed,
AI reasoning without grounding remains prone to hallucination.
5.3.1

Catching Hallucinations Early

The most important role of code execution was catching confident but incorrect claims:
• Formula Verification: GPT-5 initially claimed M (n) = 2n − 2. Running enumeration
code immediately showed M (4) = 5 ̸= 6, forcing reconsideration.
• Construction Validation: When GPT-5 proposed rectangle coverings, code verified
whether they actually satisfied the constraints (each row/column has exactly one hole).
• Proof Checking: Gemini 3 Pro’s Fooling Set construction was tested on random permutations. Early versions had bugs; code caught violations that pure reasoning missed.
5.3.2

Enabling Pattern Discovery

Beyond verification, code execution enabled discoveries that pure reasoning could not achieve:
• Exhaustive Search: For n ≤ 8, code enumerated all valid configurations to find exact
minima—something impractical by hand.
• Optimal Permutation Analysis: Code identified that the permutation (7, 4, 1, 8, 5, 2, 9, 6, 3)
for n = 9 achieves the minimum. Printing this permutation revealed the residue block
structure.
• Formula Fitting: Given data points (4, 5), (9, 12), (16, 21), (25, 32), code fit the pattern
M (k 2 ) = k 2 + 2k − 3.

Related Work

Our work builds upon several lines of research in AI-assisted mathematical reasoning and humanAI collaboration.
AI for Mathematics. Recent advances in large language models have enabled impressive
mathematical reasoning capabilities. Systems like AlphaProof [3] and AlphaGeometry [14] have
achieved medal-level performance on mathematical olympiad problems. At IMO 2025, multiple
AI systems attempted the competition: DeepMind reported gold-medal-level performance overall [4], while OpenAI [11] and DeepSeek [5] also released their attempts. However, all systems
failed on Problem 6, which required both creative exploration and rigorous proof construction.
Our work demonstrates that human-AI collaboration can succeed where fully autonomous systems fail.
Tool-Augmented Language Models. The integration of external tools with language models
has proven essential for reliable reasoning [12, 10]. Python execution, in particular, provides
crucial grounding for mathematical claims [6, 15]. Our agentic workflow extends this paradigm
by combining code execution with persistent file-based memory, enabling AI to maintain context
across complex, multi-phase reasoning.
Human-AI Collaboration. Prior work has explored various collaboration paradigms, from
AI assistants that help humans [2] to humans verifying AI outputs [16]. Investigations into
human-AI interactive workflow for mathematical research also highlight the importance of human
judgment in guiding AI [9]. Vibe Reasoning introduces a distinct pattern where humans provide
minimal, domain-agnostic guidance while AI performs substantive reasoning.
Model Specialization and Orchestration. Recent work has recognized that different models
excel at different tasks [13]. Our empirical findings on GPT-5 (exploration) versus Gemini
3 Pro (rigor) provide concrete evidence for this specialization and suggest that multi-model
orchestration is essential for complex mathematical reasoning.

7

Conclusion

We have presented Vibe Reasoning, a human-AI collaborative paradigm that transforms AI’s
latent potential into manifested capability. Our key insight is that frontier AI models already
16

possess the knowledge required to solve challenging problems—they simply do not know how,
what, or when to apply it. Through our case study on IMO 2025 P6, we demonstrated how
lightweight human guidance can unlock this potential.
The four pillars of Vibe Reasoning each address specific AI limitations:
AI as primary reasoner: Capable foundation models provide the substantive mathematical
knowledge and computational power, but require external guidance to deploy effectively.
Socratic meta-prompts: Generic, domain-agnostic human guidance supplies the missing judgment on how, what, and when—without revealing solutions or requiring domain expertise.
Agentic grounding: Python execution catches hallucinations and enables pattern discovery;
file-based memory maintains coherence across sessions.
Model orchestration: Matching model strengths to task phases (GPT-5 for exploration, Gemini 3 Pro for proof) overcomes individual model limitations.
This paper represents work in progress. We are actively developing an automated Vibe Reasoning framework with three objectives: reducing human intervention, lowering expertise requirements, and improving reproducibility. By codifying effective meta-prompt patterns into
reusable templates and decision rules, we aim to make Vibe Reasoning accessible to collaborators
without high-level mathematical training, and to reducing human workload while maintaining
solution quality. Also, replacing ad-hoc human judgment with systematic protocols enables reproducible problem-solving pipelines, facilitating scientific evaluation and iterative improvement
of the methodology. We are also extending our evaluation to additional challenging benchmarks
beyond IMO P6 to validate generality.

Proof Trace Summary

This appendix summarizes the complete reasoning trace, which contains over 100 AI turns across
2,941 lines. We highlight the key milestones and turning points in the solution process.

A.1

Phase 1: Answer Discovery with GPT-5

Step 1: Problem Formalization. GPT-5 first abstracted the grid covering problem into a
permutation framework. A valid configuration corresponds to a permutation π where row i has
its hole at column π(i). The task becomes: find the minimum number of axis-aligned rectangles
needed to cover all cells except the n holes (i, π(i)).
Step 2: Small Case Enumeration. GPT-5 wrote a backtracking enumeration script to find
exact minimum tile counts for small n. Initial results suggested M (n) = 2(n − 1), but this was
quickly disproved when the human prompted verification—exact search showed M (4) = 5 ̸= 6.
Step 3: Pattern Discovery for Perfect Squares. Following a human meta-prompt to
focus on n = k 2 (since 2025 = 452 ), GPT-5 analyzed optimal permutations for n = 4, 9, 16, 25
and discovered the “residue block” structure: positions are grouped by i mod k and blocks are
arranged in descending order. This led to the conjecture:
M (k 2 ) = k 2 + 2k − 3
For n = 2025 = 452 : M (2025) = 2025 + 90 − 3 = 2112.
Step 4: Construction Verification. GPT-5 verified the construction by generating explicit
ASCII visualizations of optimal coverings for n = 4, 9, 16, confirming that the residue permutation achieves the conjectured minimum.

Phase 2: Lower Bound Proof with Gemini 3 Pro

Step 5: Proof Strategy Selection. Given the conjectured answer, Gemini 3 Pro proposed
several proof strategies, and after several trials, it identified the Fooling Set method as the
appropriate framework. A Fooling Set is a collection of cells where any axis-aligned rectangle
containing two cells must also contain at least one hole.

Step 6: Fooling Set Construction. Gemini 3 Pro developed the “Adaptive Orthogonal

1. Base layer (n cells): For each hole at (i, π(i)), select one adjacent cell in the “outward”
direction.
2. LIS/LDS augmentation: For positions in the Longest Increasing/Decreasing Subsequence, add cells in multiple directions.
