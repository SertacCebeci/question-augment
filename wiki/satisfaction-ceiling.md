# Satisfaction ceiling

> The asker's rating of the exchange starts near the top of the scale, so the
> measure has no room to record an improvement in the answerer's comprehension
> behaviour — and a null on it is not evidence that the behaviour did not
> matter.

## Definition / Mechanism

In **Dewdney's (1986) field experiment** — the design described in[^s1]
`interview-skill-transfer` (draft dropped) — the asker side was instrumented: **236 users**
completed a questionnaire rating selected dimensions of the answer received and
of the librarian's behaviour, before and after the librarians were trained.

Two things came out of it, and they point in opposite directions:

- Users received **significantly more helpful answers from trained than from
  untrained librarians** — one dimension did separate the groups.
- Dewdney's conclusion on the rest: *moderate changes in the librarian's
  communication behaviour may not affect the user's perception of the service
  when that perception is initially very positive.*

The second sentence is the concept. The transcripts show the behaviour moved
(`interview-skill-transfer` (draft dropped)); the asker's perception, already high before any
training, largely did not. The measure's **headroom**, not the intervention's
size, is what limited what could be observed.

What is established here is about **one instrument in one experiment**: an
asker-side questionnaire whose ratings were already very positive before the
intervention. Set beside it, the other outcome measures this literature uses
are stated as proportions well short of 1 — 55% answer accuracy (Hernon &
McClure 1986) and 59.7–65% return intention (Dewdney & Ross 1994; Nilsen
2004), figures that reach this note only through [[willingness-to-return]] and
are attributed there. *This wiki's inference, drawn from that single study:* a
measure most askers endorse before anything is done to the answerer cannot
rank answerers, so a pre-saturated asker-side instrument is the wrong outcome
for an intervention on comprehension. Whether satisfaction questionnaires in
this tradition sit high **generally** is not something anything read here
establishes.

## Why it matters

1. **A null on the asker's rating does not clear the answerer.** Anyone
   evaluating a clarification policy against user ratings must first show the
   measure has headroom, or the experiment can only fail to reject.
2. **It sets a direction for instrument design.** The dimension that did
   separate trained from untrained answerers was the *helpfulness of the answer
   received*, not global satisfaction — so an asker-side instrument that names
   a specific property of the answer has a chance where a summary judgement
   does not.
3. **It is a hazard for any answerer optimised against asker ratings.** *This
   wiki's inference:* a reward built from the asker's approval of an exchange
   carries almost no gradient in the region where approval is already near the
   maximum, which is where comprehension improvements would show. Nothing read
   here tests that on a built answerer; it is the same structural worry as
   [[sycophantic-capitulation]] arriving through variance rather than through
   bias.

## Transfer

| | |
|---|---|
| **Home field** | evaluation of a public library reference desk: a short post-transaction questionnaire put to a user who chose to come, about a service that is free |
| **Here** | any asker-side rating used as the outcome for an intervention on how an answerer comprehends — user studies, preference data, post-hoc helpfulness ratings |
| **What survives** | the demand that an outcome measure's pre-intervention distribution be reported before a null is interpreted; and the observation that a specific, named dimension of the answer discriminates where a global judgement does not |
| **What does not** | the cause of the height. Free, voluntary, face-to-face service with a courteous professional is a setting with its own reasons for generous ratings; a paid, adversarial or anonymous setting may have headroom this one lacked, and nothing read here measures that |

## Limits

- **Only the dissertation's abstract was read.** The instrument, its scale, and
  the actual distribution of ratings were not seen; "initially very positive" is
  Dewdney's characterisation, not a distribution this note has inspected.
- The abstract's sentence about pre- and post-training ratings is elliptical in
  the machine-readable copy read for this note. That copy appears to report a
  significant **decrease** for the control-group librarians `[?]` — a reading
  the verifier did not confirm, and nothing in this note builds on it.
- **The generalisation in *Definition / Mechanism* is this wiki's, from one
  study.** That asker-side satisfaction instruments in reference evaluation are
  generally pre-saturated is not claimed: one instrument, in one 1986
  experiment, was. The contrast with the 55% and 59.7–65% figures is a
  juxtaposition of measures from the same literature, not a demonstrated causal
  link between them.
- **The note's own claim rests on one reading of the null**: that the ratings
  were high because the instrument lacked headroom, rather than because the
  service genuinely was good and the trained behaviours genuinely did not
  improve it. Dewdney's conclusion takes the first reading; a true null is not
  excluded by anything read here.
- One asker-side dimension did move (helpfulness), so the ceiling is a property
  of some items on this questionnaire, not of asker-side measurement in
  general.
- Single study, 236 respondents, three Ontario public libraries, 1986. No
  replication was read this pass, and the generalisation in *Why it matters* 3
  is this wiki's, untested.
