# Subgroup sensitivity

Fix a task t and the family of ways a user from subgroup g might phrase it, X(t, g). The joint entropy of the phrasing X and the model's answer Ŷ splits into two terms: "We can decompose the joint entropy of (X, Ŷ )"[^c1] as H(X|t, g) + H(Ŷ|X, t, g). The first term is the wording diversity inside the group, the stylistic spread of the same request. The second term is what the paper calls subgroup sensitivity: the answer uncertainty that remains once the phrasing is held fixed, averaged over the phrasings the group produces. It is defined as "the conditional entropy H(Ŷ|X, t, g)," and it "measures the uncertainty or variability the LLM exhibits when"[^c2] it is "exposed to prompt stylizations characteristic of subgroup g."[^c3]

The mechanism is a plain conditional entropy. What is new is the conditioning variable. An ordinary answer entropy conditions on the question, and so it cannot tell a model that answers the same question the same way under every wording from one that answers it differently depending on who phrased it. Conditioning on the phrasing separates the two. A large value says the answer is moved by wording inside one group; a small value says the wording inside that group does not move it, whatever the group is. Read this way, the term is a property of one group's stylistic family, not of the question alone.

On the Adult income task, with five rephrasings per base prompt generated for each of four race-by-gender groups,[^c4] the paper reports Black-female prompts as having "the lowest accuracy and the highest sensitivity" among the four groups. In its Table I the sensitivity is 0.28 for that group and 0.04, 0.11 and 0.09 for the other three. The same term is also reported for the BOLD prompts in its Table II, where the Black-female group again has the highest value (0.69).[^c5]

The paper pairs the term with a mitigation. Majority voting over several paraphrases of one prompt is said to act on this term: "In the setting with ground truth, this strategy reduces subgroup sensitivity by dampening variance due to linguistic style, as the majority aggregation tends to align with the task-consistent label y."[^c6] The reasoning is that the vote pools several draws of wording, so the spread that comes from wording is averaged away. My inference is that a vote over discrete labels can only shrink variance that is visible in the label; a variance in reasoning that ends in the same label would not be counted, so the term can fall without the answer becoming more correct.

## Limits

- The text I read does not say how H(Ŷ|X, t, g) was estimated for Tables I and II: the number of samples per phrasing, the estimator, and any interval are not given. A conditional entropy estimated from a handful of draws is biased, and I did not check the size of that bias here.[^c7]
- Each group is represented by five rephrasings of each base prompt, generated by one paraphrasing model. The sensitivity is a property of those five wordings, not of the full range of wordings a group would type.
- The answer space sets the entropy's ceiling. In the no-label experiments the answers are first embedded and clustered with k-means at k = 4, so the value depends on that partition; a different k gives a different number.[^c8]
- The abstract credits the drop in divergence to "our neutralization and multi-generation" strategies together,[^c9] while the Figure 3 text credits majority voting alone. The paper does not separate the two, so the effect of voting on the sensitivity term is not isolated.
- Everything is one base model, OpenThinker2-7B for the labelled task.[^c10] Nothing here shows the term orders models or groups the same way under another model.
- Sensitivity measures spread, not error. A group can have low sensitivity and a wrong answer, and the labelled experiment is the only place where accuracy is reported next to it.
