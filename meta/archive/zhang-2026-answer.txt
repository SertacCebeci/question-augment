To Answer or to Abstain: Mitigating Search-Agent Hallucinations via
Abstention-Aware Reinforcement Learning
Fengji Zhang1 , Tianyu Fan2 , Yuxiang Zheng2 , Xinyao Niu2 ,
Chengen Huang2 , Jacky Keung1 , Bei Chen2
1
City University of Hong Kong 2 Alibaba Group

arXiv:2607.10738v1 [cs.LG] 12 Jul 2026

Abstract
Recent advances in equipping Large Language Models (LLMs) with search tools and
outcome-reward reinforcement learning (RL)
have achieved new state-of-the-art results on
open-domain QA tasks. However, we argue
that current training paradigms harbor a critical
vulnerability: they predominantly reward correct answers but fail to penalize fabricated ones
when retrieval fails, thereby implicitly exacerbating hallucinations. To address this, we propose Abstention-Aware Reinforcement Learning (AWA-RL), which dynamically shapes the
abstention reward utilizing the model’s queryspecific prior capabilities and continuous onpolicy training observations. We also introduce a novel metric, RA-F1, to measure the
capability-reliability trade-off. Compared to
non-abstaining baselines, AWA-RL boosts absolute precision by up to 10.3% and overall RA-F1 by 2.9%, with only marginal sacrifice in raw accuracy. These results confirm
that AWA-RL successfully yields highly capable and reliable search agents. The code, data,
and model weights are publicly available at
https://github.com/zfj1998/AWA-RL.

Introduction

Hallucination remains a long-standing and notoriously difficult challenge in LLMs. It is challenging to ensure the factual grounding of generated
output. (Bang et al., 2025; Kalai et al., 2025) A
prevailing approach to mitigating hallucinations is
providing sufficient context to ground the answers.
Progressing from early Retrieval-Augmented Generation methods, (Agrawal et al., 2024; Béchard
and Ayala, 2024) recent advancements have introduced more advanced search agents. (Li et al.,
2025b,a) By leveraging outcome-reward RL, these
agents learn to interact with search environments
iteratively to arrive at ground-truth answers.
However, this training paradigm harbors a critical vulnerability: current RL objectives predom-

inantly incentivize models to output the correct
answer, but lack a mechanism to reward abstention. (Jin et al., 2025) Consequently, when an agent
encounters queries beyond its capability boundaries or fails to retrieve sufficient evidence, it tends
to fabricate an answer rather than safely abstain.
This “over-answering” phenomenon could exacerbate hallucinations. (Song et al., 2025) The critical
research question thus arises: How can we equip
search agents with the self-awareness to recognize
their capability boundaries and confidently abstain
from ungrounded answers?
Learning to abstain is a non-trivial objective. Existing abstention-related studies (Xu et al., 2024b;
Song et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026; Zheng et al.,
2025; Xu et al., 2024a) primarily suffer from three
major limitations1 : (1) Mismatched Scenarios:
They predominantly focus on single-turn QA rather
than multi-turn agentic settings, where capability
boundaries dynamically shift based on tool-use and
search results. (2) Degradation of Capabilities:
Most approaches employ post-hoc pipeline training, which first trains for capability, then tunes
for abstention. This disconnected process often
severely compromises the model’s original search
and reasoning abilities. (3) Reliance on Heuristics:
They heavily rely on synthesizing new unanswerable datasets, which inevitably introduces human
heuristics that fail to generalize across models with
varying intrinsic capabilities.
To address these challenges, we propose AWA-RL
(Abstention-aWAre Reinforcement Learning). The
core intuition behind AWA-RL is that the incentive
to abstain should not be a universal constant but
rather dynamically calibrated to the model’s intrinsic problem-solving capability for each specific
query. At a high level, we first estimate the model’s
prior success rate for a given question to establish
a query-aware refusal baseline. Second, to counter1

factor. Finally, during the RL training phase, we
continuously monitor the deviation between the
agent’s actual abstention behavior and the initial
capability estimate, dynamically updating the abstention reward in real time.
Through extensive experiments, we reveal exactly why conventional, intuitive approaches fail
to establish a reliable capability-reliability boundary. First, simply manipulating data proportions,
such as injecting unanswerable queries or refusal
trajectories into the training data, results in highly
unpredictable behavior. Second, applying a static
refusal reward acts as a blunt instrument that ignores query difficulty variance. Especially in RL
from scratch settings, even a minimal static reward
triggers catastrophic reward hacking.
In contrast, AWA-RL cleanly overcomes these limitations. Compared to standard non-abstaining RL
baselines, our method dramatically boosts absolute precision by up to 10.3% and improves overall
RA-F1 by 5.2%, at the cost of only a marginal drop
in raw accuracy. Crucially, empirical results confirm that the “courage” factor is remarkably easy to
tune, and the policy’s abstention behavior is highly
responsive to its adjustments. In conclusion, the
dynamic reward mechanism of AWA-RL provides a
vastly more robust and scalable pathway toward
genuinely reliable search agents.

Methodology

2.1

Task Formulation

We formulate agentic-search-based QA as an interactive decision-making process. Given a query
q ∈ Q, the agent policy πθ iteratively retrieve evidence. The process terminates with a final output y ∈ A ∪ {⊥}, where A is the space of valid
answers and ⊥ denotes the abstention or refusal
action (e.g., outputting “not enough information”
when evidence is insufficient).
2.2

Outcome-Reward RL Preliminaries

SOTA search agents are predominantly trained using outcome-reward RL, optionally preceded by
cold-start SFT. During RL, the environment provides a sparse binary reward R(y, y ∗ ) ∈ {0, 1},
indicating whether the final answer y matches the
ground truth y ∗ . We adopt Group Relative Policy
Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) to up-

date πθ . For each q, GRPO samples a group of
G trajectories and optimizes the policy using the
relative advantage Ai , computed by normalizing
the outcome rewards within the group.
2.3

AWA-RL: Abstention-Aware RL

The standard binary reward paradigm fundamentally fails to supervise the abstention action ⊥. To
address this and explicitly optimize the capabilityreliability trade-off, AWA-RL reformulates the final
environment reward R(y, y ∗ , qi ) provided to the
GRPO algorithm. We unify the reward structure by
introducing a dynamically adjusted refusal reward
rref (qi ) specifically for abstention:

Estimating the Base Refusal Reward (Rbase ).
Before abstention-aware RL training, we gauge the
model’s baseline capability by evaluating a standard, abstain-free checkpoint to compute the estimated correctness probability pi = P (y = y ∗ |qi )
for each training query qi . To prevent premature
abstention on inherently difficult questions, we apply a lower bound clip pclip = max(pi , ϵ1 ) (e.g.,
ϵ1 = 0.05). Furthermore, because executing a
multi-step search is computationally and logically
harder than simply generating a refusal token, models are naturally biased toward “lazy refusal.” To
explicitly counteract this inertia and encourage
search exploration, we apply a non-linear transformation p∗i = (pclip )γ . γ ∈ (0, 1] is the “courage”
factor, which scales the model’s confidence. The
query-specific base refusal reward is then defined
as Rbase (qi ) = 1 − p∗i . This creates a theoretical equilibrium: for queries with high estimated

capability, the expected reward of attempting an answer (p∗i ) mathematically exceeds that of refusing
(1 − p∗i ), actively driving the agent to answer.

Experimental Setup

Datasets & Implementaion Details. We evaluate on three challenging multi-hop QA benchmarks: HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), 2WikiMultiHopQA (Ho et al., 2020), and MuSiQue (Trivedi
et al., 2022). Our primary experiments utilize
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024) as the
base model. To establish the cold-start setting,
the model is first fine-tuned on 5,000 searchaugmented reasoning trajectories generated by a
stronger teacher model (GPT-oss-120b2 ). This

huggingface.co/openai/gpt-oss-120b.

bootstraps the agent’s initial multi-step search capabilities while preserving sufficient action-space
entropy. Subsequently, we conduct reinforcement
learning via GRPO on 5,000 filtered queries sampled from the training splits. The agent interacts with a standard Wikipedia search environment.
During the RL phase, we employ GPT-oss-120b
as the external LLM judge to automatically evaluate both final answer correctness and intermediate
citation quality. More implementation details can
be found in Appendix C.
Evaluation Metrics. To comprehensively assess
the capability-reliability trade-off, let Nc , Nr , and
Nw denote the number of correct, refused (abstained), and wrong responses, respectively, where
Ntotal = Nc + Nr + Nw . Following the evaluation
principles of Xu et al. (2024b), we measure policy
performance using three metrics. Accuracy (Acc),
defined as Nc /Ntotal , evaluates the model’s overall
helpfulness and serves as our Recall. Precision
(Prec), computed as Nc /(Nc + Nw ), reflects the
model’s self-knowledge by measuring correctness
strictly when it chooses to answer. Finally, we introduce Reliability-Aware F1 (RA-F1). Calculated
as the harmonic mean of Precision and Accuracy
(RA-F1 = 2 · Prec · Acc/(Prec + Acc)), this single
metric holistically captures the optimal balance between answering confidently and abstaining safely.
Baselines. We compare AWA-RL against three representative abstention paradigms. SFT-Mixed injects 10%–30% teacher-generated refusal trajectories into the SFT data to test supervised elicitation. RL-Unanswerable mixes 10%–30% inherently unanswerable queries into the RL training
set, optimizing via standard binary-reward GRPO.
Finally, Static Reward applies RL with a fixed
refusal reward rref (e.g., 0.5 or 0.9), explicitly discouraging answers unless the model’s confidence
exceeds this static threshold.

In contrast, AWA-RL cleanly resolves these limitations by dynamically anchoring rewards to queryspecific prior capabilities (Table 3). The “courage”

dynamics (Figure 1). Instead of wild oscillations or

5

Conclusion

We introduced AWA-RL to resolve the critical
capability-reliability trade-off in LLM agents.
While conventional methods, such as data manipulation or static rewards, inevitably trigger stubborn overconfidence or catastrophic reward hacking, AWA-RL dynamically anchors the refusal reward to the model’s query-specific prior capabilities. Controlled by a single, highly responsive
“courage” factor (γ), our framework successfully
decouples capability acquisition from abstention
calibration. By maximizing overall system utility (RA-F1), AWA-RL provides a robust and scalable
pathway toward genuinely reliable search agents.

Limitations
While AWA-RL demonstrates significant improvements in optimizing the capability-reliability tradeoff, we acknowledge a few limitations that pave the
way for future research.
Scale and Task Modality. Our current empirical evaluations are primarily focused on the
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model and multi-hop agentic search QA tasks. Although the theoretical formulation of our dynamic reward shaping is inherently model-agnostic and broadly applicable to
general agentic RL domains (e.g., complex tooluse, mathematical reasoning, and coding), scaling
the validation to larger foundational models and a
wider spectrum of task modalities remains an important next step to confirm its universal efficacy.
Dependence on the Courage Factor. AWA-RL
successfully distills the complex balance between
answering and abstaining into a single, intuitive
hyperparameter: the courage factor γ. As demonstrated in our training dynamics, the policy is
highly responsive to γ, making it practically
straightforward to tune. Nevertheless, identifying
the strictly optimal value still requires empirical
adjustments based on specific dataset difficulties
and model initializations. Our future research will
explore fully end-to-end, adaptive mechanisms that
can auto-calibrate this confidence mapping without
human intervention.
Computational Overhead for Prior Estimation. Establishing the optimal base refusal reward
(Rbase ) requires a preliminary inference pass over
the training set to calculate the prior success rate
(pi ). While this offline sampling introduces an
initial computational cost, we argue it is a highly
worthwhile trade-off: it provides the most faithful
and unbiased estimation of the model’s intrinsic
capabilities, which is the cornerstone for precise
refusal boundary control during RL. Still, developing sample-free or computationally lighter proxy
metrics to estimate model capability presents an
exciting avenue for subsequent optimization.

Ethical Considerations
Our work primarily contributes to the safety and
reliability of Large Language Models by explicitly training them to abstain from answering when
sufficient evidence is lacking. By addressing the
capability-reliability trade-off in agentic QA sys-

tems, our AWA-RL framework directly mitigates the
risks of hallucination and overconfident factual errors, which is a crucial step toward deploying trustworthy AI in real-world scenarios.
Despite these positive impacts, several ethical
implications warrant careful consideration:
Disparities in Abstention. In our framework, the
abstention boundary is intrinsically anchored to the
model’s prior capability estimation (pi ). It is welldocumented that pre-trained language models often
exhibit performance disparities, generally performing worse on topics related to underrepresented demographics, minority cultures, or low-resource languages. Consequently, our dynamic reward shaping might disproportionately encourage the agent
to refuse queries from these marginalized domains.
While abstaining is safer than hallucinating, this
behavior could inadvertently exacerbate inequalities in information access, as users querying nonmainstream topics might consistently receive “not
enough information” responses.
Bias in External Knowledge and Evaluators.
Our agent’s search and reasoning capabilities are
optimized interacting with an external corpus (e.g.,
Wikipedia) and supervised by an LLM-based judge
for citation rewards. Any inherent biases, historical
prejudices, or misinformation embedded within the
retrieval environment can shape the agent’s definition of “truth.” Furthermore, LLM judges may
harbor implicit preferences for certain linguistic
styles or mainstream viewpoints. If the judge incorrectly penalizes valid but minority perspectives,
the policy might learn to improperly reject valid
queries or confidently output biased information.
Future deployments of abstention-aware agents
should pair our dynamic reward mechanisms with
rigorous bias audits, ensuring equitable capability
expansion across diverse user groups and topics.

Related Work

Agentic Search for Open-domain QA. Recent
progress in open-domain QA has increasingly
shifted from one-shot retrieval-augmented generation to agentic search, where LLMs interleave
reasoning with explicit actions such as query reformulation, retrieval, and evidence integration. Early
paradigms such as ReAct (Yao et al., 2022) and
Toolformer (Schick et al., 2023) established the
value of coupling reasoning with external actions
and tool use, showing that iterative interaction with
external knowledge sources can substantially improve factuality and task performance. Building
on this line, more recent search and deep research
agents train models to perform multi-turn retrieval
and synthesis in a more end-to-end manner. For
example, Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025) optimizes interleaved search-and-reason trajectories with reinforcement learning and a simple outcome-based reward, while recent deep research systems combine
iterative web search with preference tuning, supervised fine-tuning, or RL to improve long-horizon
reasoning and report generation (Yamada et al.,
2025; Hu et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025; Zhang et al.,

2025). These advances have significantly strengthened the capability frontier of search-based QA
agents.
However, existing agentic search training
paradigms are still largely correctness-centric. In
representative RL formulations such as Search-R1,
the reward is defined only over the final outcome
correctness, with no explicit incentive for refusing unsupported answers (Jin et al., 2025; Li et al.,
2025a; Team et al., 2025; Chu et al., 2026; Tao
et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025). Likewise, recent
deep research systems primarily optimize answer
quality, factuality, or task success, rather than calibrating when the agent should abstain. As a result,
once retrieval fails, evidence is insufficient, or the
problem lies beyond the agent’s effective capability
boundary, the policy is still biased toward producing an answer. Our work addresses precisely this
missing dimension: instead of treating abstention
as a post-hoc patch, we incorporate it directly into
the same RL process that trains search behavior, so
that capability improvement and reliability calibration are optimized jointly rather than separately.
Abstention Training. A growing body of work
studies how to make LLMs refuse questions that
fall outside their knowledge boundary. One line
of research formulates refusal as a reliabilityalignment problem. RLKF (Xu et al., 2024b)
trains a reward model from knowledge feedback
so that refusal is preferred on out-of-knowledge
questions, while EKBM (Zheng et al., 2025) explicitly models knowledge boundaries through a
fast-slow framework that refines uncertain cases
with an additional reasoning stage. Another line
focuses on the adverse effect of capability-oriented
post-training: Song et al. (2025) show that standard
reinforcement finetuning can sharply reduce refusal
behavior and increase hallucination on synthetic
unanswerable math problems, and Kirichenko et al.
(2025) further show that modern reasoning models
often exhibit degraded abstention despite higher
reasoning accuracy. More task-specific approaches
include training abstention on temporal QA with
CoT supervision and abstention-aware RL (Zhou
et al., 2026), as well as reliability alignment for tool
use by expanding the action space with indecisive
actions and optimizing on synthesized data (Xu
et al., 2024a).
Despite their promise, these methods leave several open issues for search agents. First, most
prior abstention work is developed in single-turn or

otherwise non-agentic settings, such as math QA,
temporal QA, dialogue state tracking, or tool selection, where the model’s capability boundary is
comparatively static (Xu et al., 2024b; Song et al.,
2025; Zhou et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2025; Xu
et al., 2024a). In contrast, for search agents, the
answerability of a question changes dynamically
as the agent interacts with the environment and
accumulates evidence. Second, many approaches
rely on synthesized unanswerable data, abstained
variants of existing benchmarks, or auxiliary reward models/modules, which may introduce taskspecific heuristics and reduce portability across
models with different intrinsic capabilities (Song
et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2024b,a).
Third, existing reward designs often treat abstention with a fixed bonus or through a separate posthoc stage, which makes it difficult to preserve
the model’s willingness to answer when sufficient
evidence is actually available (Zhou et al., 2026;
Zheng et al., 2025). Our proposed AWA-RL is designed to fill this gap: it learns abstention within
search-agent RL, uses a dynamically shaped refusal
reward tied to an estimated prior capability rather
than a static refusal bonus, and adds an explicit
generation-encouragement term to avoid collapse
into lazy refusal. In this way, our method targets
the core unresolved problem left by prior work:
how to jointly optimize search capability and abstention reliability under a unified, lightweight RL
objective.

Additional Recipes for Stable RL

In this section, we provide detailed descriptions of
the auxiliary training mechanisms utilized to stabilize the reinforcement learning process and to
continuously enhance the underlying search capabilities of the agent.
B.1

Reward Stabilization via EMA
Smoothing and Step-wise Updates

During the on-policy rollout phase, the empirical
refusal rate (µ̂ref ) can fluctuate significantly from
step to step simply due to the random sampling
of prompts and the inherent stochasticity of the
model’s generation. If the dynamic penalty P (Et )
is updated immediately based on these noisy stepwise fluctuations, the reward landscape will become overly jittery, leading to unstable policy updates and preventing the model from converging to
an optimal abstention boundary.

• Citation Coverage (Rcov ): The judge evaluates whether the cited documents contain
all the necessary logical hops to derive the
final answer, outputting a continuous score
Rcov ∈ [0, 1].

To mitigate this variance, we introduce two robust stabilization mechanisms:
1. Exponential Moving Average (EMA)
Smoothing: Instead of using the instantaneous excess refusal rate Et , we maintain a
smoothed running average Ēt across training
steps:

• Citation Redundancy (Rred ): To prevent “citation hacking” (where the agent trivially cites
every retrieved document to maximize coverage), the judge simultaneously checks for irrelevant citations, assigning a discrete penalty
score Rred ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 1}.

Ēt = αĒt−1 + (1 − α)Et
where α ∈ [0, 1) is the decay factor (set to
α = 0.5 in our experiments). This EMA
acts as a low-pass filter, ensuring that the
penalty only responds to sustained trends in
over-refusal or under-refusal rather than transient batch noise.
2. Step-wise Quantization: To further prevent
microscopic, continuous shifts in the refusal
reward, we enforce discrete, step-wise updates by quantizing the smoothed excess rate
Ēt . Given a predefined step-tier threshold τ
(e.g., τ = 0.1), the effective excess rate is
discretized as:
Ẽt = τ ⌊Ēt /τ ⌉
where ⌊·⌉ denotes rounding to the nearest integer.
Ultimately, the dynamic penalty is calculated as
P (Ẽt ) rather than P (Et ). This quantization creates a “staircase” effect: the final refusal reward
rref remains strictly constant unless the model’s
smoothed refusal behavior crosses a significant
threshold, thereby providing a much more stable
optimization target for the GRPO algorithm.
B.2

Dense Citation Reward and Composite
Objective

Relying exclusively on the sparse outcome reward,
which assigns 1 for correct, 0 for incorrect, and rref
for abstention, can be highly inefficient for training
complex agentic tasks. When the model receives a
0, it lacks granular feedback on why it failed (e.g.,
did it fail to retrieve the right documents, or did it
fail to reason over them?).
To provide a dense learning signal that continuously bolsters the model’s multi-hop search and reasoning skills, we introduce an LLM-judged citation
reward. During generation, the agent is prompted
to cite specific supporting document IDs for its
claims. An external, powerful LLM acts as a judge
to evaluate these citations across two dimensions:

For the final GRPO update, we combine these
dense signals with our sparse outcome reward. To
prevent rewards of different scales from dominating
the gradient, we independently whiten (z-score normalize) each component within the sampled generation group. Let Wh(·) denote this intra-group
whitening operation. The final composite advantage Ai for a specific trajectory is formulated as a
weighted sum:
Ai = 2·Wh(R(y, y ∗ , qi ))+3·Wh(Rcov )−1·Wh(Rred )
This composite objective represents a highly effective decoupling strategy. By heavily weighting
the dense citation metrics (ratio of 3 for coverage),
the agent steadily optimizes its fine-grained search
and grounding skills. Meanwhile, the dynamically
scaled outcome reward (ratio of 2) is freed up to
be exclusively dedicated to balancing the overarching capability-reliability (answer-versus-abstain)
trade-off.

C

Detailed Experimental Setup

In this section, we provide the comprehensive implementation details, environment configurations,
and hyperparameters omitted from the main text.
C.1

Datasets and Search Environment

Evaluation and Training Data. For the main
evaluation, we randomly sample 1,000 queries
from the official validation sets of HotpotQA,
2WikiMultiHopQA (2Wiki), and MuSiQue. For
the RL training phase, we utilize the training splits
of 2Wiki and MuSiQue. To ensure the efficiency of
the GRPO updates, we conduct a preliminary test
rollout on the training set to filter out queries that
are either trivially easy (where the model answers
correctly in all sampled trajectories) or impossibly hard (where the model fails in all trajectories).
Such queries provide zero relative advantage and

contribute little to policy learning. From the remaining filtered pool, we randomly sample 5,000
queries for the final RL training.
Search Environment. We adopt a search environment configuration identical to Search-R1.
Specifically, we use the 2018 Wikipedia dump, partitioned into 100-word chunks. These chunks are
embedded using the E5 model (Wang et al., 2022)
and indexed using FAISS (Douze et al., 2024) for
efficient dense retrieval. At each query step, the
retriever returns the top-5 passages based on embedding cosine similarity.
C.2

Implementation and Hyperparameters

Model Initialization. All primary experiments
are conducted using the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
model, which inherently possesses strong foundational potential for tool use. For our cold-start
setting, we generate 5,000 distinct, successful trajectories using a highly capable teacher model
(GPT-oss-120b). The base Qwen model undergoes Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on this dataset
for 2 epochs, successfully bootstrapping its search
and reasoning skills before RL intervention.
