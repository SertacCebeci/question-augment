Abstract

Traditional Agent

Most conversational agents (CAs) are designed
to satisfy user needs through user-driven interactions. However, many real-world settings,
such as academic interviewing, judicial proceedings, and journalistic investigations, involve broader institutional decision-making
processes and require agents that can elicit information from users. In this paper, we introduce Information Elicitation Agents (IEAs)
in which the agent’s goal is to elicit information from users to support the agent’s institutional or task-oriented objectives. To enable systematic research on this setting, we
present YIELD, a 26M-token dataset of 2,281
ethically sourced, human-to-human dialogues.
Moreover, we formalize information elicitation as a finite-horizon POMDP and propose
novel metrics tailored to IEAs. Pilot experiments on multiple foundation LLMs show
that training on YIELD improves their alignment with real elicitation behavior and findings are corroborated by human evaluation.

The task of creating IEAs is challenging for
several reasons. First, the agent must act in an
extremely high-dimensional action space, where
every utterance can shift the trajectory of the dialogue. Second, defining what makes information
“informative” is inherently nuanced and contextdependent, bringing subjectivity into both training
and evaluation. These difficulties are further amplified in long dialogues, where agents must maintain
topic coherence over many turns while responding
adaptively to unpredictable or even adversarial user
replies. Additionally, the amount of publicly available data for training IEAs is limited. To the best
of our knowledge, no existing dataset is available
to support the development of IEAs. In this paper,
we make the following contributions:

standard, full dialogue version of the dataset
and a rolling-window segmented version used
for the experiments in this study.
• We propose and operationalize a few IEAspecific evaluation metrics: Conformity, Progression, and Turn-Length Ratio.
• We further formalize information elicitation as
a finite-horizon Partially Observable Markov
Decision Process (POMDP) and use offline
reinforcement learning (ORL) with a minimal
proxy reward to show that dialogue-level optimization with YIELD is competitive with
supervised fine-tuning (SFT).
• Pilot experiments across multiple foundation
LLMs show that fine-tuning on YIELD improves distributional and behavioral alignment
with human elicitation dialogues. These findings are confirmed by human evaluations.

Dataset Construction

Our dataset construction process consists of three
stages: data acquisition, annotation, and dataset
standardization.
3.1

Data Acquisition

We chose the data sources with the following aims:
(1) to maximize coverage of elicitation settings and
(2) to ensure legal usability through public domain
or Creative Commons licensing. Many of the files,
particularly PDF and DOCX transcripts, to the best
of our knowledge, had not previously been processed into any dialogue dataset. We categorized
the identified sources into four domains of elicitation dialogues. For a full description of the data
sources, see Appendix A.1.

There is growing interest in role-aware CAs,
which adopt defined personas and pursue structured goals. These agents have gained popularity
in a range of applications, from mental health support (Fitzpatrick et al., 2017) to legal assistance
(Thomson Reuters, 2025). Common development
approaches use foundation LLMs fine-tuned with
domain-specific datasets, often combined with reinforcement learning and human feedback to maintain role consistency and behavioral alignment
(Kong et al., 2023). In parallel, research on proactive CAs explores how conversational systems can
drive interactions forward, such as initiating questions, shifting topics, and helping users make decisions (Deng et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2023). These
systems have been used in exploratory search and
evaluative tasks such as LLM-as-an-Interviewer
(Kim et al., 2024) and LLM-as-a-Judge (Zheng
et al., 2023). Both role-aware and proactive agents
benefit from curated dialogue datasets tailored to
their specific domains and objectives.

3.2

Annotation

The collected transcripts appeared in various formats, including HTML, TXT, PDF, and DOCX.
Unifying these different formats and heterogeneous
materials required extensive pre-processing. Automated text extractors regularly introduced errors
during conversion and sources contained individual issues such as speaker names that appeared
without distinguishing markers and often varied in
form within the same document. These and other
issues made automatic parsing and tagging unreliable. Therefore, we manually corrected and annotated the transcripts over several weeks, removing
extraneous text, standardizing speaker references,
and marking turns and speaker roles. Further details on the annotation and preprocessing pipeline
are provided in the dataset documentation.

In the traditional POMDP formulation, the agent
maintains a belief state St , which is a probability distribution over possible hidden environment
states Xt . This distribution encodes the agent’s
available information about the hidden variables
governing the environment at time t and serves
as the agent’s basis for decision making. In the
dialogue setting, however, explicitly maintaining
such a distribution is not feasible as Xt represents
unbounded and continuously changing information (everything the respondent might know, think,
or believe). To make the problem tractable, we
represent the information available to the elicitor at time t with token-level hidden representations St = fθ (Hts ) produced by a causal language
model, where fθ (Hts ) denotes the model’s forward
pass over the full segment Hts parameterized by θ.
Because the model is autoregressive, each token’s
representation depends only on the tokens preceding it, meaning that the hidden states corresponding
to the context Ht encode everything the model has
“seen” in the current turn’s context. St provides
the sufficient context from which the likelihood
of At can be evaluated, functionally mirroring the
role of the belief state in the traditional POMDP
formulation.

As a first-step reward formulation to validate the
proposed elicitation framework, we operationalize
rewards r using a factual novelty signal as proxy
for informational gain, measuring how much previously unmentioned content an elicitor action surfaces. We apply a spaCy-based NER model (Honnibal et al., 2020) to each utterance and maintain
a cumulative set of previously mentioned entities,
events, or facts. After the elicitor’s turn, the respondent’s reply is scanned for entities not yet observed; the elicitor is rewarded in proportion to
each newly introduced entity. Formally, the reward
is Rt+1 = Et+1 \ E≤t , where Et+1 denotes the set
of entities extracted from the respondent’s response,
E≤t the accumulated set from all prior turns, and
\ denotes the set difference operator. To mitigate
potential reward-gaming behaviors, we compute
factual novelty under several constraints. Entities
produced by the elicitor are also added to the cumulative entity set, but they do not contribute reward,
so that entities subsequently repeated by the respondent are not misattributed. Repeated entities yield
no additional credit, therefore, agents that allow
conversations to linger on the same content accrue
fewer reward. While entity count alone could be
gamed in unconstrained settings (e.g., a model providing a list of entities as utterance), this risk is
mitigated here because rewards are computed on
curated, real human–human dialogues.

Prompted and SFT Models

We compare against two versions of the foundational models: (1) prompt-only and (2) supervised
fine-tuning (SFT). Prompt-only models use the

6.5

Evaluation

For evaluation, each six-turn segment is truncated
by removing the final elicitor turn, and the model is
tasked with generating that turn given the preceding
five turns. This is repeated for all segments in the
testing set, which models have not seen yet. Sample
outputs are demonstrated in Appendix A.7.

6.5.3

Turn-Length Ratio

Table 8 presents turn-length ratio results, with the
shuffled dataset also included. In the shuffled baseline, elicitor and respondent turns are randomly
reassigned, causing answers to appear as questions
and vice versa. This role mixing neutralizes the systematic asymmetry present in real dialogues, leading to ratios close to 1. Meanwhile, real dialogues
show ratios well above 1 across domains, reflecting the elicitation dynamic in which elicitor turns
prompt longer respondent utterances. Both SFT
and ORL closely reproduce this asymmetry, learning to limit elicitor verbosity in proportions similar
to human interviewers, a behavior that prompted
models largely fail to exhibit. As with response
length, this gap cannot be reliably closed through
additional prompting, since doing so does not make
models internalize elicitor behavior.

6.6.1

Potential Misuse and Mitigations

Information elicitation agents raise ethical concerns due to their goal of guiding conversations
toward information that serves external objectives,
which can introduce risks of biased or leading questioning, power imbalances, and undue influence,
particularly in domains such as journalism, legal
proceedings, or public opinion polling where neutrality is critical. These risks are compounded in
settings involving sensitive personal information
(e.g., healthcare or counseling), where issues of
data privacy, storage, and potential misuse arise.
Mitigations include enforcing constraints on questioning strategies (e.g., prompt-level guidelines or
post-generation filtering) and extending evaluation
protocols to detect and penalize biased behavior.
Systems should also implement data minimization,
secure storage, and clear user consent and transparency practices. In high-stakes or sensitive contexts, IEAs should only be deployed under appropriate institutional and regulatory oversight (e.g.,
IRB or clinical governance), with human monitoring and auditing to ensure compliance with ethical
and legal standards, rather than relying solely on
model-level safeguards.

Human Evaluation

We collected human ratings of elicitor question
quality using Amazon Mechanical Turk. For each
HIT, workers reviewed the five-turn dialogue context followed by corresponding real, prompted,
SFT, and ORL responses. We randomized the order
of the response sources across HITs to prevent order effects. Workers scored questions on five-point
scales representing different aspects of elicitation.
The full set of instructions given to participants
is available in Appendix Table A.6. In total, 300
dialogue evaluations were collected (100 dialogue
blocks sampled with stratified sampling, rated by
three workers). Workers were required to have
more than 500 prior HITs and approval rate above
90%. Workers were compensated at a rate consistent with U.S. minimum wage, based on estimated
task completion time.

real utterances than prompted models, supporting

Conclusion

Our evaluation demonstrates YIELD effectively
supports the development of models for the elicitation setting and our finite-horizon POMDP formulation with offline reinforcement learning shows
that dialogue-level optimization is competitive with
SFT even when trained with a minimal reward signal. This work opens several directions for future
research including extended reward formulations
that capture domain-specific notions of informational value, interactive evaluation with live respondents, and additional evaluation dimensions that
capture a broader range of elicitation characteristics.

The reward formulation used in offline reinforcement learning is intentionally minimal and may not
adequately capture informational value across elicitation contexts. Additionally, evaluation is limited
to offline, next-turn generation on fixed contexts
rather than interactive settings. Moreover, some
proposed metrics are sensitive to design choices
(e.g., Progression depends on the choice of embedding model and hyper-parameters) and may not
capture all aspects relevant to effective elicitation
behavior. Finally, this work focuses on modeling
the base elicitor agent and does not address conversation management. While a fine-tuned LLM can
serve as the core of an elicitation system, it is likely
insufficient on its own for sustaining structured and
effective long-horizon interactions.

Baseline’s Prompt

The following is the prompt used on the baseline
models. The prompt was not tailored to any specific
model and was generated using an LLM to avoid
researcher bias.
You are participating in an oral history interview
as the "elicitor" (interviewer). Your role is to
elicit personal stories, reflections, and details from
the "respondent" (interviewee) through natural,
conversational questions.

Metrics:
• Conformity (1–5)
How well does the question match the
expected style of the speaker, per the
Dialogue Setting?
1 = Very unnatural / out of character
3 = Neutral / acceptable but not strong
5 = Perfectly fits the expected style

Follow these principles:
• Be friendly, engaged, and curious.
• Ask clear, open-ended questions.
• Let the conversation flow naturally build on
what the provider just said.
• Use informal, real spoken language (e.g.,
“So tell me. . . ” or “What was that like?”).
• Occasionally use acknowledgments or restatements like “OK” or “Wow” to maintain
flow.
• Focus on memories, motivations, feelings,
and personal experiences.

• Progression (1–5)
Does the question move the conversation
forward?
1 = Off-topic or repetitive
3 = Somewhat helps the discussion
continue
5 = Strongly advances the discussion
• Outcome Relevance (1–5)
Is the question relevant to the goal of the
