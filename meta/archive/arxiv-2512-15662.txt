Abstract
Human beings solve complex problems
through critical thinking, where reasoning and
evaluation are intertwined to converge toward
correct solutions. However, most existing large
language models (LLMs) treat the reasoning
and verification as separate processes: they either generate reasoning without explicit selfchecking or rely on external verifiers to detect
errors post hoc. The former lacks immediate feedback, while the latter increases system
complexity and hinders synchronized learning.
Motivated by human critical thinking, we propose Stepwise Think-Critique (STC), an endto-end trainable framework in which a single
LLM emits a structured, step-level critique inline with each reasoning step. STC is trained
with reinforcement learning that complements
reasoning rewards with a critique-consistency
reward derived from final-answer correctness,
jointly optimizing reasoning correctness and
critique reliability. On five mathematical reasoning benchmarks, STC improves Pass@1 by
7.2% over the 1.5B base model and attains
67.4% step-level critique F1, surpassing seven
external process reward models evaluated at
their per-dataset oracle thresholds—a step toward LLMs with built-in critical thinking.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in solving complex
reasoning tasks. Techniques such as Chain of
Thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022) and
reinforcement-based reasoning approaches (e.g.,
DeepSeek-R1) (Guo et al., 2025) enable models
to decompose problems into multi-step reasoning
trajectories, leading to improved performance in domains such as mathematical problem-solving, program synthesis, and complex question-answering
(Guo et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025).

Despite these advances, a fundamental limitation persists: current approaches lack an intrinsic,
on-the-fly mechanism to assess the correctness of
intermediate reasoning steps. Such a mechanism
is highly desirable because i) in domains such as
mathematics, science, and finance, users require
transparent judgments on whether each reasoning
step is correct; ii) integrated self-critique allows
models to adapt their reasoning trajectories dynamically, enabling self-assessment during inference;
and iii) stepwise evaluation could provide dense
feedback, facilitating more effective policy optimization.
Most existing reasoning models (Guo et al.,
2025; OpenAI, 2025) focus solely on generating
reasoning and final answers, as illustrated in Figure 1 (a). Some studies have explored post-hoc verification, where an external verifier evaluates the correctness of the reasoning process (see Figure 1 (b)).
For example, process reward models (PRMs) have
been developed to evaluate intermediate reasoning
steps produced by a policy model (Zhang et al.,
2025; Zou et al., 2025; Khalifa et al., 2025; Xiong
et al., 2025). Although such methods can detect errors, the post-hoc nature (i.e., the separation of reasoning and verification) prevents timely feedback,
and maintaining separate reasoning policy and verifier models increases system complexity, hindering
seamless deployment in real-world pipelines.1
The ability to interleave reasoning and evaluation is a hallmark of human problem solving. Humans naturally engage in critical thinking, continuously reflecting on intermediate conclusions,
assessing their validity, and adjusting subsequent
steps accordingly. This process allows humans
not only to reach correct solutions more efficiently
but also to produce reasoning traces that are interpretable and trustworthy. Inspired by this, we

• We propose Stepwise Think-Critique (STC), a
framework in which a single LLM emits a structured, step-level critique inline with each reasoning step, eliminating the post-hoc reasoner–
verifier split.
• We design an end-to-end RL training scheme
that jointly optimizes reasoning and critique
within a single model: a critique-consistency reward derived from final-answer correctness is
propagated only to critique tokens via masking,
for accurate credit assignment.
• On five math benchmarks, STC improves Pass@1

Our unified framework eliminates the requirement
of an external verifier and enables on-the-fly selfcritique. This represents a crucial step towards
building LLMs with built-in critical thinking,
bridging the gap between human-like problem solving and reliable machine reasoning. At inference
time, we can optionally enable the critiquing for enhanced interpretability—providing judgments on
where and why mistakes occur—or disable it to
reduce token usage.

“thinking”—in LLMs, with works such as
DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025), DAPO (Yu et al.,

sequences of reasoning actions that lead to correct
solutions.
Actor–critic methods like VAPO (Yue et al.,
2025) use a value model to estimate expected return but do not explicitly assess the correctness of
reasoning steps or provide interpretable feedback.
Existing RL frameworks thus lack intrinsic, finegrained self-assessment capabilities.

2.2

Process Reward Modelling

Process reward models (PRMs) provide another
perspective on stepwise evaluation, focusing on the
intermediate reasoning steps rather than solely on
the final answer (Zhong et al., 2025). By offering
finer-grained feedback, PRMs could improve credit
assignment, help identify local errors.
Designs of PRMs have evolved along multiple
axes. Early approaches adopt a discriminative format, where a classification head predicts step-level
correctness scores (Lightman et al., 2023; Zhang
et al., 2025). More recent works embrace generative approaches, producing natural language judgments for each reasoning step (Zhang et al., 2024;
Zheng et al., 2025; Khalifa et al., 2025; Zhao et al.,
2025). ThinkPRM (Khalifa et al., 2025), for instance, generates a verification chain-of-thought
(CoT) that critiques and justifies each step post hoc,

improving interpretability and error localization.
Beyond scoring individual steps, PRMs are also
applied during policy optimization, where they provide step-level reward signals that guide model
updates. Despite their utility, most PRMs are
trained offline on curated datasets and remain
frozen, which can make them brittle under distribution shifts. PRIME (Cui et al., 2025) mitigates this limitation by deriving implicit process
rewards from outcome labels through SFT, while
TANGO (Zha et al., 2025) jointly trains a generator and PRM via RL. Nevertheless, these methods
rely on separate models, which adds complexity,
communication overhead, and memory usage. In
contrast, STC integrates the critic into a single
model, avoiding extra communication and deployment overhead.

3

Stepwise Think-Critique (STC)

3.1

Overview of STC

We introduce Stepwise Think-Critique (STC), a
unified framework that interleaves reasoning and
critique within an output sequence. Unlike prior
paradigms that either perform pure reasoning or
rely on external post-hoc verification, STC equips a
single model with the ability to generate reasoning
steps and simultaneously assess their correctness.
Formally, given an input problem x, the model
generates a sequence that alternates between reasoning step {rt }Tt=1 and critique step {ct }Tt=1 ,

where T denotes the number of steps that varies
for different sequences. As illustrated in Figure 2,
each reasoning step rt advances the solution, while
the corresponding critique ct provides a naturallanguage judgment and score for assessing the correctness of rt . The final solution is produced in
the last reasoning step, followed by its associated
critique.
We first train the model with supervised finetuning (SFT) on our synthesized reasoning–critique
data, enabling it to learn the interleaved reasoningcritique patterns and to acquire basic critique ability (for cold start). We then further train the
model with RL using reasoning reward, critiqueconsistency reward, and format reward, to boost
critical thinking capability.
3.2

Interleaved Reasoning-Critiquing

For an LLM, to enable interleaved generation, we
design instruction formats that explicitly encourage the model to alternate between reasoning and
critique. Specifically, the prompt specifies the required structure:
r1 → c1 → r2 → c2 → · · · → rT → cT .

(1)

Reasoning steps are partitioned according to
the generated \n\n. Each critique step is expressed in natural language and consists of two
parts: (i) a concise justification, and (ii) a binary correctness label, where 1 denotes “Correct” and 0 denotes “Incorrect.” The model outputs critiques using the following tagged format:
<critic>{justification}</critic> and a binary
judgment in the format <score>{1 or 0}</score>.
This structured format allows the model to explain
errors in context while keeping the output compact
and informative.
During inference, through prompting alone, we
support two modes:
• Compact mode: reasoning only, omitting critiques to save tokens.
• Full mode: reasoning and critique are interleaved,
providing detailed step-level judgment, enabling
error localization and enhancing interpretability.
This dual-mode design offers a flexible trade-off
between efficiency and interpretability, allowing
users to select the mode that best suits their needs.
3.3

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

We begin with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to
equip the model with interleaved reasoning-critique

generation. We synthesize SFT data by sampling
reasoning trajectories (r1 , . . . , rT ) from the base
model on training problems, then prompting a
strong LLM (e.g., GPT-5) to produce stepwise critiques cn = (jn , sn ) following the format in §3.2,
where jn is a natural-language justification and
sn ∈ {0, 1} a binary correctness label. To remove
unreliable supervision, a rule-based verifier V
checks the final answer rT , and we discard trajectories where the strong LLM’s score on rT disagrees
with V . We then fine-tune the model on the resulting D = {(x, τ )} with τ = (r1 , c1 , . . . , rT , cT ) by
minimizing LSFT (θ) = −E(x,τ )∼D log πθ (τ | x).
3.4

RL with Joint Reasoning and Critiquing

While SFT provides initial alignment, it is limited
by data coverage and generalization. We therefore further post-train the model using Grouped
Reinforcement Policy Optimization (GRPO) with
designed rewards.

Experiments

4.1

Experimental Setup

4.1.2 Implementation Details
We use DS-Qwen-1.5B (Guo et al., 2025) as
the 1.5B-parameter base model and train on
DeepScaleR-Preview-Dataset (Luo et al., 2025).
Hyperparameters, max response lengths, and the
system prompt are in Appendix A.
4.1.3 Evaluation Protocol
We evaluate both the reasoning and critique capability of models. Evaluations are performed with
a maximum response length of 8,192 tokens for
reasoning and 4,096 tokens for critique by default.
For reasoning quality, we report Pass@1 (P@1
for short) and Pass@8.
For critique quality, we evaluate both the answer
level and the process level. At the answer level,
correctness is directly verifiable from the final solution (see Eq. 4). At the process level, because
step-level human labels are unavailable, we use a
strong LLM judge (GPT-5-mini) to produce pseudo
labels for stepwise critiques.
We report three metrics: Correct Accuracy
(Corr.), Error Accuracy (Err.), and their harmonic mean F1; higher is better for all (↑). Corr.
measures how well the critic accepts truly correct
items, Err. measures how well it rejects truly incorrect items. Following (Zheng et al., 2025), we use
F1 to assess the overall critic capability. This
metric penalizes degenerate solutions that achieve
high correct accuracy but fail to detect errors, or
vice versa. Detailed metric definitions (including
step-level and answer-level) are provided in Appendix B.

Evaluation Datasets

We evaluate our framework on multiple mathematical reasoning benchmarks: AIME24, MATH-500,

4.2

Results

We evaluate STC on three axes: intrinsic reasoning quality (§4.2.1), critique quality (§4.2.2), and
critique quality relative to seven external PRMs
(§4.2.3). For the first two axes we compare three
model variants: DS-Qwen-1.5B (base model), STCSFT (after supervised fine-tuning), and STC (RLtrained). §4.2.4 shows qualitative trajectories.
4.2.1

Reasoning Performance

Table 1 reports reasoning accuracy across all benchmarks. i) SFT reduces average Pass@1 accuracy
by 2.1% relative to the base model. We attribute
this drop to enabling stepwise critique, which shifts
the model’s output distribution and temporarily degrades reasoning performance. ii) Subsequent RL
training recovers and surpasses this gap: STC (full)
outperforms STC-SFT by 9.3% at Pass@1 and
8.5% at Pass@8 on average, and also exceeds the
base DS-Qwen-1.5B by 7.2% Pass@1, showing
that the inline-critic objective is compatible with
strong reasoning.
Full mode vs. compact mode. STC can operate
in two inference modes: compact mode, where critique generation is disabled and the model outputs

only reasoning, and full mode, where the model performs both reasoning and stepwise critique. The
two modes share the same model weights and differ only in the system prompt (highlighted in orange in Tables 11 and 15). As shown in Table 1,
both modes outperform STC-SFT significantly and
achieve comparable reasoning accuracy: since the
reasoning capability is acquired during training,
toggling critique generation at inference time does
not affect it. Users can flexibly choose the mode
based on whether intermediate critiques are needed,
without sacrificing reasoning performance.

To further validate the reliableness of GPT-5mini step labels, we use a stronger (more expensive)
LLM, Claude Opus 4.7 (with maximum reasoning budget and no truncation), as a reference and
re-evaluate the AIME24 labeled corpus. Treating
Claude as the gold standard, GPT-5-mini achieves
a balanced F1 of 87.3% (Corr. 88.3%, Err. 86.4%,
raw agreement 88.1%). To balance performance
and cost, we use GPT-5-mini as the judge. More
analysis can be found in Appendix C.1.

Stepwise critique as a dense reward. We also
explored stepwise critiques as a dense RL reward;
the formulation we tried did not yield robust gains
(see Appendix C.5).

5

Conclusion

We presented Stepwise Think-Critique (STC), a
unified framework that interleaves reasoning and
self-critique within a single model. Across multiple mathematical reasoning benchmarks, STC
demonstrates strong reasoning and critiquing capabilities and produces more interpretable process
traces. These results support STC as a practical
step toward LLMs with built-in critical thinking,
bridging the gap between human-like problem solving and reliable machine reasoning.
We hope this work motivates further research on
critical-thinking mechanisms that jointly improve
transparency, reliability, and reasoning quality.

6

Limitations

While STC demonstrates promising results on
mathematical reasoning, several limitations remain.
Scale and single-seed evaluation. We validate
the framework on a single 1.5B-parameter base

model (DS-Qwen-1.5B) under a fixed random seed;
multi-seed experiments were not run due to compute cost (one full SFT+RL run requires ∼5 days
on 16×40GB A100). Extension to 7B/14B+ models and to multimodal settings is left as future work.
Domain coverage. Our experiments cover only
text-only English mathematical reasoning across
five benchmarks. We do not evaluate STC on code
synthesis, agentic / tool-use tasks, multilingual reasoning, multimodal (vision+math) problems, or
open-ended generation. While the framework itself is domain-agnostic, our claims about “critical
thinking” should be read narrowly as referring to
step-level error detection in mathematical chain-ofthought.
Critic reliability. STC’s step-level balanced F1
of 67.4% leaves substantial room for improvement,
especially on Minerva (52.7% F1) where critic reliability is weakest.
Step granularity vs. PRMs. STC’s \n\ndelimited atomic steps are substantially finer than
the paragraph-level granularity of most external
PRMs. The PRM comparison (Table 4) therefore
operates at different base rates across systems and
should be read as evidence that STC reaches a
strong calibration-free operating point, not as a
direct step-by-step claim over PRMs in their native
granularity.

7

Ethical Considerations

STC is a methodology study evaluated on standard public mathematical reasoning benchmarks; it
does not introduce new user-facing systems, sensitive datasets, or task domains beyond those already
established in the LLM reasoning literature. Nevertheless, we identify a concrete risk that warrants
disclosure.
Critique reliability. As shown in Table 2 of the
main manuscript, STC’s error-detection accuracy

on the answer level remains well below 100% (averaging 42.6%); a positive critique should therefore
not be interpreted as a correctness guarantee, particularly in high-stakes settings such as education,
tutoring, or scientific computing where users may
over-trust an explicit score-1 signal.
We do not foresee misuse pathways unique to
STC beyond those already common to LLM-based
mathematical assistants.

A

Implementation Details

SFT data construction. We randomly select 10k
samples and generate one reasoning rollout per
sample using the base model. We then filter out
samples with reasoning token length larger than
8,192, and use GPT-5 to generate critiques for each
reasoning step. Next, we apply a three-stage filter to ensure: (i) structural integrity (reasoning
and critique blocks are strictly paired); (ii) answer–
critique consistency with the ground-truth critic.
This yields 5,056 high-quality samples (about 51%
retention). For each retained sample, we interleave
reasoning and critique steps to construct a unified
trace. The dataset spans a wide range of complexities with token lengths up to 15,130: about 75% of
samples fall between 2,048 and 8,192 tokens, 24%
exceed 8,192 tokens, and 10% exceed 10,240 tokens, providing sufficient depth for complex multistep reasoning.
Hyperparameters and lengths. SFT uses batch
size 64 for 550 steps. RL with GRPO uses group
size G=8, batch size 32, λformat =0.05, and trains
for ∼1200 steps total (two-stage schedule below).
Maximum response lengths are 8,192 tokens for
reasoning and 4,096 tokens for critique. The system prompt is shown in Table 11 (highlighted in
orange).
RL training schedule. For RL training, we use a
two-stage schedule with different hyperparameter
settings. In Stage 1, we set λcrit = 0, λreason = 1,
and λf ormat = 0.05, and train for about 450 steps
to improve reasoning ability and adherence to the
reasoning–critique format. In Stage 2, we alternate between two settings every 40 steps for approximately 450 steps: (λreason , λf ormat , λcrit ) =
(1, 0.05, 0) and (0, 0.05, 1). This stage is designed
to improve both reasoning ability and critique capability. We alternate these two objectives to mitigate
gradient conflict between reasoning and critique
optimization.

B

Evaluation Protocol Details

We evaluate both the reasoning and critique capability of models. Evaluations are performed with
a maximum response length of 8,192 tokens for
reasoning and 4,096 tokens for critique by default.
For problem solving capability, we evaluate
model reasoning performance using Pass@1 (P@1
for short) and Pass@8, which capture singleattempt accuracy as well as success rates across
multiple sampled attempts.
For critique capability, we evaluate models at
both the final answer level and the reasoning-step
level. i) For the final answer, the correctness
of the critique is directly verifiable (see discussion for Critique-consistency reward in §3.4.1).
ii) For intermediate reasoning steps, however, no
human-annotated ground truth exists. Therefore,
we employ a strong LLM (GPT-5-mini) as an
automatic judge to assess the correctness of the
model’s stepwise critiques. We denote the pseudo
ground-truth critic labels for a trajectory as z g =
(z1 , z2 , · · · , zT ). Note that we use GPT-5-mini instead of GPT-5 here in considering that GPT-5mini is much faster than GPT-5 while they achieve
similar agreement rate with verifiable critics when
evaluated at answer level.
Sampling protocol for critique evaluation. To
bound LLM-judge cost (GPT-5-mini step labels
are billed per token; per-trajectory labeling cost
is dominated by the ∼250 atomic steps in each
trajectory), we evaluate critique on a fixed sample of 240 STC trajectories per benchmark: the
first 30 prompts of each dataset × 8 STC rollouts
per prompt. This sample is reused for all PRM

comparisons (Table 4) and all critic tables (Tables 2, 3). AIME24 has exactly 30 prompts and
is therefore fully covered; AMC23 (83 prompts),
MATH-500 (500), Minerva (272), and OlympiadBench (675) are each represented by their first 30
prompts. Reasoning (Pass@1/Pass@8) is evaluated
on each benchmark’s full prompt set. Extending
critique evaluation to full benchmark coverage is a
straightforward follow-up bounded only by LLMjudge cost.
Since our work is (to our knowledge) the first to
unify stepwise reasoning and critique in a single
model, there is no off-the-shelf evaluation metric.
Previous PRM benchmarks and evaluation metrics
(Zheng et al., 2025) are designed for post-hoc verification and are not suitable for our evaluation since
they assume a separate verifier with access to full
trajectories and gold step labels, while our setting
evaluates a jointly generated step-and-critique process.
Motivated by (Zheng et al., 2025), we design
task-aligned metrics that measure correct accuracy
(Corr.), error accuracy (Err.), and their harmonic
mean (F1).

C.8

Failure Case

Following §4.2.4, Table 19 shows a failure case
where STC’s critic itself is unreliable: at step
3 it confirms a wrong unit conversion (“3 Mpc
equals 3000 parsecs”) with <score>1</score>.
The pseudo-GT GPT-5-mini judge identifies “Incorrect unit conversion: 1 Mpc = 106 pc, so
3 Mpc = 3,000,000 pc, not 3,000 pc.” This example shows that, while STC’s natural-language
critique offers step-level interpretability, the critic
itself is not 100% reliable; further improving its
performance remains a direction for future work.

D

Additional Discussion

D.1

Computation Cost

We validate STC on a 1.5B model. We set the maximum response length as 8,192 tokens for reasoning
and 4,096 tokens for critique. Running 1200 RL
steps requires approximately 5 days on 16×40GB
A100 GPUs.
Our goal is to establish the core mechanism; the
framework is model-agnostic and extendable to
other models. Due to limited resources, we did not
extend the validation to larger scale or multimodal
experiments. We hope this work motivates further
research on critical-thinking mechanisms.

D.2

About Reward Hacking

One may wonder whether our scheme faces reward
hacking. Actually, we did not observe evidence of
reward hacking in extensive inspection.
First, as shown in Table 6, STC identifies 21% of
final answers as incorrect, rather than collapsing to
predicting all answers as either correct or incorrect.
Similarly, STC identifies 23% of steps as incorrect
on average, close to the pseudo ground-truth ratio
of 19.1% (see Table 7).
Second, as shown in Table 11, our critic correctly flags a bogus alternative computation that
the model floats mid-trace, providing valuable information rather than merely stylistic output.
D.3

Inference Cost

The full mode interleaves critiques with reasoning
and consumes additional tokens at inference time.
Naive deployment without exposing the compactmode fallback could increase cost, latency, and
energy use in user-facing applications. We recommend defaulting to compact mode and enabling
full mode only when interpretability is required.
