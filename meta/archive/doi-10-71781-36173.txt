Résumé

La génération augmentée par récupération est une approche prometteuse pour la questionréponse financière, où les réponses doivent être appuyées par des documents sources primaires. Dans ce contexte, la principale difficulté vient souvent de la récupération d’information plutôt que de la génération seule. Les documents financiers sont longs, répétitifs et
riches en tableaux, et les éléments de preuve pertinents peuvent se trouver dans des documents de plusieurs centaines de pages. Ce mémoire place donc la récupération au cœur de
l’étude de la question-réponse financière et analyse l’effet des erreurs de récupération sur la
qualité des réponses générées.
Nous introduisons un cadre d’évaluation fondé sur des conditions oracles qui sépare les
erreurs de récupération aux niveaux du document, de la page et du segment. Appliquée à
FinanceBench, cette analyse montre que les systèmes retrouvent souvent le bon document
tout en manquant la page qui contient la réponse. Ce constat motive l’étude de stratégies
d’adaptation pour la récupération financière, notamment la récupération hiérarchique pagepuis-segment, l’adaptation d’un bi-encodeur au niveau des pages, le réordonnancement par
encodeur croisé, la reformulation de requêtes et le classement visuel des pages.
Ce mémoire évalue aussi la génération selon la taille des modèles, les types de questions
et les types de documents financiers. Les résultats montrent qu’une meilleure récupération
mène à de meilleures réponses, surtout pour les questions numériques qui exigent des preuves
exactes, mais que ce gain varie selon les catégories de documents et de questions. Une
extension de benchmark fondée sur FinQA montre aussi qu’aucune méthode de récupération
n’est la plus forte sur tous les jeux de données. Dans l’ensemble, ce mémoire soutient qu’une
question-réponse financière fiable sur de longs documents nécessite une évaluation au niveau
où la récupération échoue, ainsi que de meilleures stratégies pour localiser les preuves au
niveau des pages.
Mots-clés : Génération augmentée par récupération, question-réponse financière, recherche d’information, adaptation au domaine.

Abstract

Retrieval-augmented generation is a promising framework for financial question answering,
where answers must be grounded in primary-source documents. In this setting, the main
difficulty is often retrieval rather than generation alone. Financial filings are long, repetitive,
and rich in tables, and the relevant evidence may appear on one or more pages inside a
document spanning hundreds of pages. This thesis studies retrieval as a central bottleneck
in financial question answering and analyzes how retrieval failures affect downstream answer
quality.
We introduce an oracle-based evaluation framework that separates retrieval errors at
the document, page, and chunk levels. Applied to FinanceBench, the analysis shows that
systems can often retrieve the correct filing while still missing the answer-bearing page.
This motivates a study of adaptation strategies for financial retrieval, including hierarchical
page-then-chunk retrieval, page-level bi-encoder adaptation, cross-encoder reranking, query
reformulation, and visual page scoring. These experiments examine how domain adaptation,
task adaptation, and page representation affect evidence localization.
The thesis further evaluates generation across model sizes, question types, and filing
types. Results show that improved retrieval leads to better answers, especially for numerical
questions requiring exact evidence, but that the benefit varies across document and question
categories. A benchmark extension based on FinQA further shows that no single retrieval
method is uniformly strongest across datasets. Overall, the thesis argues that reliable financial question answering over long documents requires evaluation at the level where retrieval
fails, and that future systems should combine stronger page-level evidence localization with
adaptive or more universal retrieval strategies.
Keywords: Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Financial Question Answering, Information Retrieval, Domain Adaptation.

Introduction

The financial industry runs on documents. Each quarter, thousands of publicly traded
companies file detailed disclosures, accumulating a vast record of annual reports, quarterly
filings, earnings transcripts, and special notices that constitutes the primary evidence base for
analysts, investors, and compliance professionals. Answering precise questions about these
filings (documents) is not only useful but essential to work in finance. Such questions range
from what a company’s revenue was in a given fiscal year, to how management characterized
a particular risk exposure, to what the terms of a recent acquisition were. Until recently,
answering such questions required a human reader to navigate these documents manually, a
process that is time-consuming, expensive, and prone to the errors that come with searching
through hundreds of dense pages under pressure.
Large language models have recently introduced a new possibility, namely a system that
reads a question in natural language and produces an answer drawn directly from the relevant
context. The paradigm that makes this practical is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG),
which connects the reasoning and fluency of a generative model with a retrieval component
that identifies the most relevant passages from an external document corpus. The appeal
for financial applications is considerable. Such a system can ground its answers in primary
sources, support citation and auditability, and remain current as new filings are published
without requiring the underlying language model to be retrained. Yet the gap between this
description and a reliable system in practice is substantial, and understanding it requires
examining the retrieval component as well as the generation component.
The difficulty is not primarily one of language generation. Modern generative models are
remarkably capable of synthesizing financial information when given the right context, and
we believe that the primary challenge is retrieval. The system must first locate, within a
corpus that may contain hundreds of lengthy filings, the specific page and passage containing
the evidence needed to answer a given question. Financial filings are not only long (an
annual report can exceed two hundred pages) but structurally repetitive. For example,
revenue related information may appear in several sections of the same filing. The same
section headers appear across companies and across years. Dense tables present critical

numerical facts in layouts that standard text extraction handles poorly. A retrieval system
must navigate these challenges to surface not just a topically relevant passage but the specific
context a particular question requires to generate an adequate answer.
This thesis takes the retrieval challenge as its central object of study. Its starting point
is an observation that has received limited (systematic) attention in the literature. Namely,
retrieval failure in long-document financial question answering is not a single phenomenon.
A pipeline can fail because it does not retrieve the correct document, or because it retrieves
the correct document but misses the relevant page within it, or because it locates the correct
page but still fails to surface the most informative passage. Each failure mode has a different
cause and different implications for how a system should be improved.
The first contribution of this thesis is an oracle-based evaluation framework that decomposes retrieval failure across multiple levels of granularity. By restricting the search space to
the correct filing (oracle document) or to the correct pages within it (oracle page), the framework establishes empirical upper bounds at each level and measures the gap between current
systems and those bounds. The framework further quantifies generative performance by measuring the maximum answer accuracy gain that better page retrieval alone can deliver, with
the generator held fixed. Applied to FinanceBench [Islam et al., 2023], the primary benchmark for PDF-native financial question answering, this analysis reveals a consistent finding
across retrieval methods spanning sparse, dense, hybrid, and hierarchical approaches. While
current systems achieve reasonably high success at identifying the correct filing, their ability
to find the relevant page within that filing falls substantially short of the empirical ceiling.
Thus the dominant bottleneck is within-document page retrieval, not document discovery.
The second contribution is a diagnostic study of adaptation strategies for financial retrieval over long filings. Motivated by the oracle analysis, this thesis evaluates a hierarchical
page-then-chunk retrieval design in which a Stage-1 scorer first ranks pages from the full corpus and Stage-2 dense retrieval is then restricted to chunks from the highest-ranked pages.
This setup tests whether page-level selection can reduce noise from irrelevant filing sections
while preserving fine-grained retrieval within the most promising regions of each document.
Within this framework, a BGE-M3 bi-encoder fine-tuned on FinanceBench page-level annotations through cross-validation is used as a Stage-1 page scorer. This experiment is
interpreted as a test of page-level task adaptation rather than as a standalone deployable
model. ColQwen2 [Faysse et al., 2024] is also evaluated as a visual page-scoring alternative,
replacing extracted text with patch embeddings to assess whether bypassing text extraction
improves retrieval over financial documents. Separately, a cross-encoder reranker fine-tuned
on FinQA [Chen et al., 2021] financial page data is applied zero-shot over standard BGEM3 dense retrieval, isolating the effect of out-of-domain financial adaptation without page

2

pre-selection. Together, these experiments study how domain adaptation, task adaptation,
and page representation affect retrieval performance in financial QA.
The third contribution is a systematic evaluation of retrieval and generation performance
across multiple dimensions. On the retrieval side, all configurations are evaluated at multiple
levels of granularity, document recall, page recall, and context quality, and broken down
by question type and filing type to surface where different methods succeed and fail. A
qualitative failure analysis then examines the recurring reasons behind retrieval failures. On
the generation side, the thesis evaluates answer quality across generator sizes and question
types and analyzes the residual errors that persist even under perfect retrieval conditions.
Finally, the fourth contribution broadens the scope of evaluation through a benchmark extension pipeline. The thesis develops a procedure for aligning questions from the
LoFIN/HiREC benchmark [Choe et al., 2025], which is composed of two existing financial
question answering (QA) datasets, to gold PDF pages in financial documents retrieved from
EDGAR, a publicly available financial database. The aligned result serves as a starting point
toward a larger evaluation set that goes beyond FinanceBench’s open-source 150 questions,
and offers a preliminary test of whether retrieval performance holds on a separate question
source. The scope of the current alignment is however limited by practical constraints discussed in the thesis, and does not yet achieve the document and question type diversity that
motivates the extension.

1.1. Introduction
This chapter surveys the research landscape underpinning the contributions of this thesis.
The narrative follows the logical progression of the problem itself, beginning with retrievalaugmented generation as a paradigm, tracing where it came from, how it has evolved, and
where it currently stands. It then turns to the information retrieval methods that form the
technical foundation of retrieval-augmented generation systems, examining their strengths
and failure modes with the financial retrieval problem in mind. From there the focus narrows
to financial question answering as an application domain, characterizing the properties of
financial documents that make retrieval especially challenging and surveying the benchmarks
that the field has developed to measure progress. The chapter then examines strategies
for retrieval over long, structured documents, surveying hierarchical retrieval approaches
from general-purpose multi-stage indexing systems to domain-specific financial pipelines, and
treating chunking as a deliberate design variable rather than a fixed preprocessing choice.
It closes with treatments of domain adaptation for financial retrieval and visual document
retrieval as an emerging alternative paradigm.

1.2. Retrieval-Augmented Generation
1.2.1. From Parametric Knowledge to Grounded Generation
Large language models encode a remarkable breadth of world knowledge in their parameters, acquired through exposure to vast text corpora during pre-training [Brown et al.,
2020]. Yet this parametric approach to knowledge carries inherent limitations that become
acute in high-stakes application domains. A model’s knowledge is frozen at the time of its
training and grows stale as the world changes. It also cannot easily cite the specific sources

Fig. 1.1. Standard retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline. A query is used to

that support a given claim, which makes its outputs difficult to verify or audit. Because the
model must compress what it has learned into a fixed parameter count, it may also generate
plausible but factually incorrect statements, a failure mode commonly called hallucination.
This risk is especially high for facts that are rare, recent, or domain-specific [Brown et al.,
2020, Ji et al., 2023]. In professional domains such as finance, where users need answers they
can verify against primary sources and where a subtly incorrect number can have material
consequences, these limitations make RAG necessary in high-stakes domains.
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) addresses these limitations by separating the
language model’s role as a generator and reasoner from the task of knowledge storage and
information retrieval. Figure 1.1 illustrates the standard RAG pipeline and highlights the
central role of retrieval, whose effectiveness ultimately constrains the quality of generation.
Rather than asking the model to recall facts from its parameters, a RAG system maintains an
external corpus of documents that can be updated, versioned, and inspected independently.
At inference time, a retrieval component identifies the most relevant passages from this
corpus and supplies them to the language model as context, after which the model synthesizes
those passages into a coherent answer. This architecture reduces hallucination by anchoring
generation in retrieved evidence, enables provenance by allowing the retrieved passages to be
surfaced alongside the answer, and supports knowledge currency by allowing the document
corpus to be updated without retraining [Lewis et al., 2020]. The formal framework can be
expressed concisely. Let D = {d1 , . . . , d|D| } be a corpus of documents, and let C denote the
set of all textual chunks obtained by segmenting the documents in D. Given a query q, a

1.2.2. The Origins of RAG and Open-Domain QA
The formal introduction of the RAG framework is typically attributed to Lewis et al.
[2020], who combined a pre-trained neural retriever with a sequence-to-sequence generator and demonstrated substantial improvements over closed-book baselines on knowledgeintensive natural language processing tasks. Their formulation treats retrieved passages as
a latent variable that the generator marginalizes over, establishing a probabilistic grounding
for the retrieve-then-generate pipeline that subsequent work has largely preserved in spirit
even while modifying its implementation substantially.
This architecture built directly on the work of open-domain question answering research
that had already demonstrated the value of retrieval-based approaches [Chen et al., 2017,
Lee et al., 2019]. What the integration with large pre-trained language models added was
a qualitative shift in generation quality. Rather than extracting a span from a single retrieved passage, the model could synthesize information from multiple passages, paraphrase,
aggregate, and reason in ways that earlier extractive systems could not. Fusion-in-Decoder
[Izacard and Grave, 2021] extended this insight by encoding each passage separately and
fusing them in the decoder’s attention, enabling more robust multi-passage reasoning and
providing a strong generation backbone that many subsequent RAG systems have built upon.

1.2.3. The Evolution of RAG Paradigms
The simplest implementations of retrieval-augmented generation, which the survey paper
from Gao et al. [2023b] characterize as Naive RAG, follow a three-stage pipeline. Documents
are indexed offline as dense vector representations, a query is encoded at inference time and
matched to the nearest indexed vectors, and the top-k retrieved passages are concatenated
with the query and passed to the language model for generation. This design is appealing for
its simplicity and modularity, and it achieves strong results on general-domain benchmarks
7

where queries are short, documents are compact, and relevant passages are relatively easy
to distinguish from irrelevant ones.
In more demanding settings, the limitations of this approach become apparent. Chunking documents into fixed-size windows can fragment evidence that spans multiple passages,
leaving no single chunk containing all the information the generator needs. Retrieving by
vector similarity favours passages that are topically related to the query but may not contain
the specific evidence it requires, a problem that grows more severe as documents become
longer and more internally repetitive. And because the retriever and generator are trained
independently, the retriever has no direct signal about which passages are actually useful
for downstream generation. These limitations set the research grounds for what Gao et al.
[2023b] call Advanced RAG.
Advanced RAG systems introduced targeted interventions at both the beginning and
the end of the retrieval process. Pre-retrieval optimization focuses on improving the match
between the query and the indexed passages before any retrieval takes place. Query reformulation methods expand or decompose the original question, they can generate a hypothetical
answer document whose embedding may align better with relevant passages, or break a
complex question into simpler sub-questions that can each be matched to a specific passage
[Gao et al., 2023a, Ma et al., 2023, Karpukhin et al., 2020]. Index optimization techniques
restructure the document representation to make retrieval more reliable, for instance by
preserving table structure, encoding section metadata, or building hierarchical indices that
capture document organization [Gao et al., 2023b].
Post-retrieval optimization intervenes after the initial candidate set has been assembled.
Reranking passes retrieved candidates through a more powerful but computationally expensive scoring model that can evaluate query-passage relevance more accurately than the
first-stage retriever [Nogueira et al., 2019]. Context compression removes passages judged
redundant or irrelevant, allowing the generator to focus on the most pertinent evidence [Xu
et al., 2023].
More recent developments have moved toward what Gao et al. [2023b] describe as Modular RAG, in which retrieval and generation components are decomposed into independently
configurable modules that can be orchestrated in diverse patterns depending on the task.
Iterative retrieval allows the system to perform multiple rounds of retrieval interleaved with
partial generation, using the model’s intermediate outputs to guide subsequent queries [Shao
et al., 2023]. Adaptive retrieval enables the system to decide based on the model’s assessed
uncertainty whether a given question requires retrieval at all or can be answered from parametric knowledge [Jiang et al., 2023]. The current state-of-the-art is one in which the core
retrieve-then-generate pipeline is well understood, and research attention has shifted toward

8

specialized domains, improved diagnostic evaluation, and the identification of the specific
failure modes that aggregate metrics conceal.

1.3. Information Retrieval Methods for RAG
The practical performance of a RAG system is determined in large part by the quality
of its retrieval component. This section surveys the main classes of retrieval methods that
appear in the RAG literature and in the experimental evaluation of this thesis, examining
their respective strengths and failure modes with the financial retrieval problem specifically
in view.

relevance as their cosine similarity. In many modern retrieval models, including the setup
used in this thesis, the same parameterized encoder is applied to both the query and chunk

1.3.3. Hybrid Retrieval and Reciprocal Rank Fusion
Sparse and dense methods exhibit complementary failure modes. Sparse methods fail
when query and document vocabulary diverge, but succeed when queries contain rare or
10

distinctive terms that must appear verbatim. Dense methods succeed at semantic matching
but can be distracted by topically related passages that do not contain the specific evidence
required. Hybrid retrieval combines both to exploit their complementarity, typically by

1.3.4. Query Reformulation and Hypothetical Document Embeddings
A structural challenge in retrieval for question answering is the asymmetry between the
short, high-level phrasing of a natural language question and the long, detailed phrasing of a
relevant passage. A query of the form “What was the company’s FY2022 net revenue?” may
share few tokens with the income statement row it is trying to locate. Query reformulation
methods address this asymmetry by transforming the query before retrieval to better match
the style and vocabulary of relevant passages.
Hypothetical Document Embeddings (HyDE) [Gao et al., 2023a] address this problem
directly. Rather than embedding the question itself, HyDE prompts a language model to
generate a hypothetical passage that would answer the question, then uses that hypothetical
passage’s embedding for retrieval. The generated passage does not need to contain accurate
facts, it simply needs to be phrased in a style and vocabulary that aligns with the documents
in the corpus. For financial queries, this means the hypothetical passage is written in the
tone of a financial report, with appropriate terminology and sentence structures, which
can substantially improve the match between the query embedding and the embeddings of
genuine evidence passages. Multi-HyDE, a variant evaluated in this thesis, averages the

1.4. Financial Question Answering
1.4.1. The Distinctive Challenges of Financial Documents
Financial question answering is a demanding application domain for retrieval systems
because it requires searching over documents that exhibit multiple challenging properties,
both individually and in combination. A typical 10-K annual report spans between one hundred and three hundred pages and follows a standardized structure mandated by regulation,
covering the business description, risk factors, financial statements, management’s discussion
and analysis, and a collection of exhibits [Islam et al., 2023]. This standardization means
that similar section headers appear across every company and every fiscal year, creating a
retrieval challenge distinct from that of a heterogeneous document corpus. A query about
operating expenses is relevant to the income statement, but the income statement in one
year’s 10-K is structurally nearly identical to the income statement in every other year’s 10K from every other company. Retrieval systems relying on topical similarity must therefore
make fine-grained distinctions that first-stage bi-encoders frequently fail to make reliably.
Financial documents are also numerically dense in a way that matters for question answering. Many of the most important questions that analysts care about, including revenue
figures, earnings per share, capital expenditures, and debt ratios, require extracting or computing specific numerical values from dense tabular contexts [Chen et al., 2021]. These values
appear as entries in tables whose structure encodes essential context.
Finally, financial questions require precision in a sense that general-domain questions do
not. A question asking for FY2022 revenue must be answered with the value from the 2022
filing, not the 2021 or 2023 filing, even though all three filings are from the same company
and discuss the same concept in nearly identical language. This temporal specificity imposes
a retrieval requirement that goes beyond topical relevance and extends to exact contextual
matching of year references, quarter identifiers, and other temporal markers. Combined with
the structural repetitiveness described above, this creates a retrieval setting in which passages
from different reporting periods are often semantically and structurally near-identical. As a
13

result, retrieval is not simply a matter of identifying the correct topic or year, but of resolving
fine-grained distinctions within highly similar content, making accurate ranking non-trivial.

1.4.2. The Evolution of Financial QA Benchmarks
The benchmark landscape for financial question answering reflects a progression from
closed-book settings, where the relevant document or passage is provided to the model,
toward open-book settings where the model must retrieve its own evidence from a realistic document corpus. This progression matters for interpreting what different benchmarks
measure and what limitations they impose.
The earliest influential benchmarks in the space, including FinQA [Chen et al., 2021]
and TAT-QA [Zhu et al., 2021], focused primarily on numerical reasoning capability. Both
datasets provide question-answer pairs grounded in short document contexts that contain
the necessary evidence, essentially supplying the retrieval result to the model and asking
only whether it can reason over what has been handed to it. FinQA in particular annotates
reasoning programs that trace the multi-step calculations required to derive each answer,
making it a valuable resource for studying arithmetic reasoning. ConvFinQA [Chen et al.,
2022] extended this framework to conversational settings, introducing multi-turn dependencies where each question builds on the previous one. These benchmarks established that
language models can perform sophisticated numerical reasoning over financial tables and
narrative text, but because they supply the evidence rather than requiring its retrieval, they
do not measure the retrieval component of financial QA.
More recent benchmarks have introduced retrieval requirements in varying forms. SECQA [Lai et al., 2024] provides a systematic evaluation corpus for financial QA over SEC
filings with an emphasis on cross-company comparisons, though its use of template-based
question generation produces questions that may not capture the heterogeneous, narrativedriven nature of genuine analyst queries. LoFIN [Choe et al., 2025] offers a large-scale
open-domain benchmark with QA pairs spanning a very large number of SEC documents,
enabling evaluation under conditions where the system must identify the relevant company
and filing period before locating specific evidence.

1.4.3. FinanceBench as the Primary Evaluation Standard
Among existing benchmarks, FinanceBench [Islam et al., 2023] stands out as the most
directly relevant to the work of this thesis. As far as we know, it is the only publicly
available open-source benchmark that requires end-to-end retrieval over real PDF filings
with explicit page-level evidence annotations, making it possible to evaluate retrieval quality
at the granularity this thesis is most concerned with.
14

FinanceBench comprises question-answer-evidence triplets based on real SEC filings from
forty U.S. publicly traded companies spanning nine GICS sectors. SEC filings are mandatory
financial disclosure documents submitted by public companies to the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, while GICS sectors refer to the industry categories defined by the Global
Industry Classification Standard. Each sample includes a natural language question, a reference answer, a verbatim evidence string extracted from the source document, and metadata
identifying the source filing and the page number on which the evidence appears. This pagelevel annotation is what makes FinanceBench uniquely valuable, it provides ground-truth
labels for page-level retrieval evaluation, enabling the oracle analysis framework described
in Chapter 3 to operate at the granularity where the critical failure mode lies.
The questions in FinanceBench fall into three types, with fifty questions of each kind
in the open-source subset. Domain-relevant questions ask about facts broadly applicable
across companies, such as dividend policies or executive compensation structures, and often
require locating narrative statements that may appear in varying sections of different filings.
Metrics-generated questions require extracting or computing specific financial indicators from
dense tabular contexts, these are especially demanding for retrieval because the answer lies
in a specific table cell, and topically similar passages from adjacent pages or adjacent fiscal
years serve as near-perfect retrieval decoys. Novel-generated questions are company-specific
and require reasoning beyond simple extraction. The document types in the benchmark are
dominated by 10-K annual reports at approximately 75%, with the remainder drawn from
10-Q quarterly reports, 8-K current reports, and earnings call transcripts.

1.4.4. Limitations of Existing Benchmarks and the Motivation for
Extension
While FinanceBench provides an invaluable structured testbed, its 150-question opensource subset imposes real constraints on what can be concluded from experiments conducted
on it alone. The strong skew toward 10-K filings means that findings about retrieval difficulty,
chunking effects, or performance on annual reports may not generalize to quarterly reports,
earnings transcripts, or more specialized filing types. The limited number of open-source
questions means that stratified analysis by question type or filing type is based on small
subgroups where individual question characteristics can have an outsized effect on aggregate
metrics. Finally, the lack of separate train, dev, and test sets limits the generalization claims
of domain fine-tuning for PDF-native benchmarks.
These limitations directly motivate the benchmark extension described in Section 3.6. At
a broader level, they reflect a genuine gap in the financial QA literature. Most benchmarks
that require retrieval either operate in a closed-book setting, assume that text has already
15

been extracted from the original document, have limited or no publicly available data, or
restrict evaluation to a single narrow filing type. A benchmark that stresses end-to-end PDF
retrieval at scale across diverse filing types and question styles remains an open contribution,
and the extension work of this thesis is a step toward filling it.

1.4.5. Recent RAG Systems for Financial QA
Several recent systems have pushed state-of-the-art performance on FinanceBench and
related benchmarks, and understanding their contributions is important for contextualizing the contributions of this thesis. FinSage [Wang et al., 2025] introduces a multi-aspect
RAG framework that combines domain-specific preprocessing, multi-path retrieval over different document representations, and a reranker fine-tuned using direct preference optimization (DPO) on financial query-document pairs. The preprocessing pipeline is particularly
thoughtful, preserving table structure, encoding section metadata, and constructing semantically coherent chunks that respect the document’s visual layout, an approach that the
authors show improves retrieval precision substantially over naive fixed-size chunking.
Kim et al. [2025] propose a three-phase pipeline integrating query expansion, hybrid
retrieval with fine-tuned embedders, and a DPO trained reranker with document selection,
achieving strong improvements in retrieval precision and downstream answer accuracy across
several financial QA datasets. These systems demonstrate that substantial performance gains
are achievable through careful engineering of the full RAG pipeline.
What neither system provides, however, is a systematic decomposition of where their improvements come from. Neither study separates document-level retrieval performance from
page-level retrieval performance, nor establishes oracle bounds that quantify the headroom
remaining at each level of the retrieval hierarchy. It is therefore difficult to know from their
reported results whether further investment in retrieval quality would yield significant returns, or whether the remaining performance gap is attributable to generation limitations
that better retrieval cannot address. The oracle-based analysis framework introduced in this
thesis is designed to fill precisely this diagnostic gap.

1.5. Hierarchical Retrieval Strategies for Long Documents
A fundamental challenge in building retrieval systems for long documents is selecting
the appropriate granularity for the units that will be indexed and retrieved. Retrieving at
the level of full documents preserves all context but makes it impractical for the retrieval
system to identify which part of a long filing contains the relevant evidence, forcing the
16

generator to process an enormous amount of irrelevant text. Retrieving small chunks enables
precise localization but risks fragmenting evidence that spans multiple passages and loses
the surrounding context that helps the generator interpret what it has found. Hierarchical
retrieval approaches address this tension by organizing documents into multi-level structures,
using coarser levels to quickly identify the most promising regions of the document space and
finer levels to precisely localize answer-bearing evidence within those regions. This section
surveys the main families of hierarchical retrieval method, from general-purpose recursive
indexing schemes to specialized pipelines designed for long financial documents.

1.5.1. Multi-Stage Retrieval and Hierarchical Indexing
A prominent family of approaches addresses the granularity tension by constructing hierarchical document representations offline, which are then searched in a coarse-to-fine manner
at retrieval time.
RAPTOR [Sarthi et al., 2024] constructs a tree of abstractive summaries by recursively
clustering document chunks and generating summary nodes at increasing levels of abstraction. A leaf node represents a short passage, its parent summarizes a cluster of related
passages, and the root node represents a high-level synopsis of the full document. At retrieval time, the system can be queried at any level of the tree or across multiple levels
simultaneously, allowing high-level questions to be matched to high-level summaries and
detailed questions to be matched to fine-grained passages. On long-document benchmarks,
this design substantially improves recall on questions requiring the synthesis of information
from multiple disparate sections, because the summarization step aggregates evidence that
no individual passage contains. The principal limitation is that recursive summarization
can introduce information loss and hallucination at higher levels of abstraction, making the
quality of the intermediate summaries a critical determinant of overall retrieval performance.
More generally, two-stage coarse-to-fine pipelines interleave a cheap first-stage retriever
over coarse units (documents or sections) with a more expensive second-stage ranker over
fine-grained units (passages or chunks) within the top-M coarse candidates. This design
concentrates the second stage on a small, pre-filtered candidate pool, allowing a computationally expensive ranker to operate efficiently while covering the full document corpus at
the first stage. The architecture introduces a new failure mode, where any relevant passage
whose parent document or section is not retrieved at the first stage cannot be recovered,
making the coarse retrieval component a hard ceiling on overall recall.
17

1.5.2. Parent-Child Structures and Decoupled Retrieval Granularity
A closely related pattern is the parent-child document structure, in which fine-grained
child chunks are indexed for retrieval precision while coarser parent chunks are returned to
the generator to provide broader context [Gao et al., 2023b]. After retrieving the top-k child
chunks by similarity to the query, the system maps each child to its parent, deduplicates,
and returns the unique parent chunks as the context for generation. This decoupling of
retrieval granularity from generation granularity addresses a challenge, the optimal unit size
for retrieval is often not the optimal unit size for generation, because retrieval benefits from
short, discriminative passages while generation benefits from longer passages that supply
complete context. In practice, a common instantiation uses sentences or short fixed-size
spans as children and paragraphs or pages as parents, though the appropriate granularity
depends heavily on the document type and the nature of the questions being asked.
The parent-child pattern is relevant to long financial documents because the evidence for
a single question often occupies a coherent physical unit such as a financial statement page
or a management discussion subsection, while precise retrieval requires matching the query
against specific sentences or table rows within that unit. Indexing at both granularities,
without committing to either, is a design principle that recurs across several recent financial
retrieval systems.

1.5.3. Chunking as a Granularity Decision
Chunking sits at the boundary between document processing and retrieval design, and
is most naturally understood as the decision that defines the leaf nodes of the retrieval
hierarchy. Its importance is widely acknowledged, but it is rarely treated as a primary
variable of study. Most financial RAG papers fix a chunking configuration early in system
design and optimize other components without varying it, so the literature offers many data
points about what retrieval performance is achievable with a given strategy but little evidence
about the interactions between chunking choices and retrieval architecture.

1.5.4. Hierarchical Retrieval for Long Financial Documents
Financial QA introduces additional structure that specialized hierarchical systems can
exploit. A typical end-to-end pipeline must navigate from a large corpus of filings (thousands
of documents) to a single filing, then from that filing’s many pages to the specific page containing the answer, and finally to the precise passage or table row within that page. Treating
these as separate retrieval problems rather than one allows each stage to be optimized for
its specific discriminative challenge.
The LoFIN benchmark and HiREC system provide a direct illustration of this principle for
open-domain financial QA [Choe et al., 2025]. LoFIN is a large-scale evaluation corpus that
requires a system to identify the relevant company and filing period from a collection of over
145,000 SEC documents before locating specific evidence. HiREC addresses this challenge
with a hierarchical retrieval pipeline that first identifies related documents to resolve company
and period ambiguity, and then selects the most relevant passages within those specific
documents. This is followed by an evidence curation step that filters out irrelevant data and
generates complementary queries if the initial retrieval is insufficient. The system’s explicit
separation of the document retrieval process from the within-document passage selection
provides a principled way to reduce confusion caused by the repetitive boilerplate content
and similar table structures inherent in standardized financial reports.
19

Within financial documents, the page provides a practical intermediate unit between the
full filing and individual text chunks. Pages often correspond to coherent units of presentation, such as complete financial statements or self-contained subsections, meaning that their
boundaries tend to align with meaningful semantic structure. In contrast, section boundaries require additional parsing and are not consistently defined across filings, while fixed-size
chunking can arbitrarily fragment structured content. Because page boundaries are explicitly
defined by the document format and consistently available across filings, they offer a stable
and interpretable granularity for retrieval. More broadly, this reflects a general observation
in retrieval systems that the choice of representation granularity can significantly impact
retrieval effectiveness [Izacard and Grave, 2021, Liu et al., 2024].

1.6. Visual Document Retrieval
1.6.1. The Text Extraction Bottleneck
All the retrieval methods described in the preceding sections share a fundamental assumption, namely that documents can be adequately represented as sequences of text tokens. For
financial filings, this introduces a systematic source of information loss. In practice, important aspects of these documents are encoded through visual structure rather than purely textual content. Layout plays a functional role, column alignment defines relationships between
entities and values, and formatting cues such as headings and spacing convey hierarchical
organization. Standard text extraction and parsing pipelines recover most narrative text
reliably, but they can fail on complex multi-column tables, sometimes producing linearized
representations in which values are separated from the headers that give them meaning [Xu
et al., 2020]. For metrics-generated questions, whose answers often lie in these tabular structures, such extraction errors can propagate directly to retrieval, limiting the effectiveness of
downstream models.

1.6.2. Vision-Language Models and the ColPali Architecture
Vision-language models offer an alternative approach that bypasses text extraction entirely by processing document pages as images. A vision encoder, typically a Vision Transformer that divides each page image into a grid of patches, represents the visual content
of the page as a sequence of patch embeddings, preserving spatial structure rather than
linearizing it [Dosovitskiy et al., 2020]. These patch embeddings can then be processed by
a language model backbone to produce contextualized representations that integrate visual
layout with semantic content.

1.6.3. Recent Advances and Remaining Challenges
A limitation of the flat patch-aggregation approach in ColPali is that all patches of a
page are indexed at a single level of granularity, regardless of their visual complexity or their
relevance to typical queries. Recent work has explored multi-resolution indexing strategies
that extend the patch-based paradigm to multiple spatial scales. Roy et al. [2025] propose
a three-pass pyramid indexing framework that constructs separate visual representations at
page, region, and patch levels, allowing the retrieval system to match queries at whichever
visual granularity is most discriminative. On document-heavy benchmarks, this multi-scale
approach improves recall on questions that require matching fine-grained visual elements
such as specific table cells or column labels, where full-page patch aggregation tends to
dilute the relevant signal across too many patches.
Despite these advances, visual retrieval methods face a set of challenges that remain
partially unresolved. The resolution at which VLMs process document pages is constrained
by the input size of the vision encoder, many financial tables use small fonts and dense layouts
that are difficult to read at the resolutions typically used for training. Domain mismatch
is also a concern, since most VLM-based retrieval models are trained on general document
corpora and may not have seen sufficient examples of the standardized typography and table
structures used in SEC filings to develop discriminative representations for that domain.
21

Finally, the computational cost of storing full patch embedding sequences for large document
corpora is substantially higher than storing single-vector chunk embeddings, limiting the
scalability of late-interaction visual retrieval. Whether domain-specific fine-tuning, higherresolution encoders, or hybrid approaches combining text and visual signals can address
these gaps is an open and active area of research.

1.7. Domain Adaptation for Financial Retrieval
Embedding models trained on general-domain corpora face a systematic mismatch between the distributional properties of their training data and the properties of financial
documents and queries. Financial reports use a specialized vocabulary in which terms such
as “EBITDA,” “net tangible assets,” and “diluted earnings per share” carry precise technical meanings that diverge from their general-language senses or have no general-language
counterpart. Queries about financial metrics are typically short, formally phrased, and saturated with abbreviations, while the relevant passages are long, structurally repetitive, and
express the same concepts using standardized financial language of SEC filings. This querypassage asymmetry is quite prominent in the financial domain, and it is compounded by
the specificity of financial questions. A model that has not seen many examples of matching
FY2022 queries to FY2022 evidence, rather than to structurally identical FY2021 or FY2023
passages, has no learned basis for that distinction.
The standard approach to bridging this domain gap is fine-tuning. Given domain-specific
query-passage pairs, the embedding model is trained to score relevant pairs higher than irrelevant ones. For bi-encoder fine-tuning, the Multiple Negatives Ranking (MNR) loss is commonly used. It treats all other passages in the minibatch as implicit negatives for each query,
creating a computationally efficient approximation to listwise ranking. For cross-encoder
rerankers, binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss over explicit positive and sampled-negative pairs
is preferred, as it calibrates a pointwise relevance score rather than requiring exhaustive
listwise normalisation. Hard negatives, drawn from topically similar but answer-irrelevant
passages rather than at random, play a critical role in shaping what either model learns, particularly in the financial domain where boilerplate language makes random negatives trivially
easy to dismiss. Kim et al. [2025] demonstrate that fine-tuning BGE-M3 on financial querypassage pairs improves FinanceBench retrieval substantially relative to the zero-shot model,
with the fine-tuned model showing improved ability to match financial terminology and temporal expressions. FinSage [Wang et al., 2025] applies direct preference optimization (DPO)
on financial query-document pairs to train a reranker that learns fine-grained distinctions,
including between passages from the same document that discuss the same topic in different
fiscal years. The specific loss formulations, negative sampling strategies, and hyperparameter
22

choices for both the bi-encoder page scorer and the fine-tuned cross-encoder reranker used
in this thesis are presented in detail in Chapter 3.

1.8. Summary
This chapter has traced the evolution of retrieval-augmented generation from its foundational contributions through the current state of the field, examined the information retrieval
methods that form the technical core of RAG systems, and situated financial question answering as a domain that stresses these methods in ways that general-domain evaluations do
not reveal. The survey of benchmarks established FinanceBench as the primary evaluation
standard for this thesis while motivating its extension to address limitations in document
diversity and coverage.
The treatment of hierarchical retrieval surveyed the landscape from general-purpose recursive indexing systems such as RAPTOR to domain-specific two-stage pipelines for financial documents. It treated chunking as an integral granularity decision within that hierarchy
rather than a separate preprocessing step, identifying the interaction between chunk size and
retrieval architecture as an understudied variable that this thesis addresses experimentally.
Domain adaptation established the contrastive fine-tuning approaches that allow general embedding models to acquire the financial retrieval competence needed to distinguish temporal
near-duplicates and match domain-specific terminology. The closing section on visual document retrieval introduced the ColPali architecture and recent multi-scale extensions as an
emerging complementary paradigm whose applicability to financial documents is evaluated
in the methodology that follows.
The literature reviewed in this chapter shows that financial RAG poses a retrieval problem
that is more difficult than standard end-to-end evaluation. Long, repetitive, and table-heavy
filings create challenges that motivate more fine-grained retrieval analysis and specialized
retrieval architectures. The next chapters therefore turn to the datasets, indexing decisions,
and evaluation setup used to study these challenges.

2.2.2. Benchmark Extension: FinQA-503
FinanceBench’s 150-question open-source subset covers a limited range of companies,
years, and document types. To study generalisation beyond this distribution, we construct
an extended evaluation set from LoFIN, the benchmark introduced with HiREC [Choe et al.,
2025], which combines the FinQA test set [Chen et al., 2021] and the SEC-QA test set [Lai
et al., 2024].
FinQA [Chen et al., 2021] contains a total of 8,281 question-answer pairs across its official
splits, with 6,251 training examples, 883 development examples, and 1,147 test examples.
The dataset is built from 10-K filings of S&P 500 companies and focuses on multi-step
numerical reasoning over financial tables and surrounding text. Each record includes a
question, a structured numerical answer, and pre_text/post_text passages bracketing the
relevant table (example in Figure B.1 in the Appendix). Because these annotations refer to
source passages rather than PDF page numbers, linking FinQA to our corpus requires the
alignment step described in Section 3.6.
Of the 1,147 FinQA test-set questions, 530 could be matched to downloadable PDFs and
503 were successfully aligned to gold PDF pages through our evidence-matching procedure,
forming the FinQA-503 evaluation subset. SEC-QA was not included in this extension.
Although SEC-QA is described as both a framework for dynamically generating financial
multi-document QA tasks and the set of questions produced by that framework, we could
not identify a publicly released version of the benchmark with the annotations needed for our
page-level evaluation. In addition, the SEC-QA-style records available through LoFIN did
not contain gold evidence text that could be used as an anchor for alignment to extracted
PDF pages. Unlike FinanceBench, the FinQA-503 corpus is homogeneous in document type,
with all 115 documents being annual 10-K reports spanning fiscal years 2008–2019. Answers
are almost exclusively single numerical values, with a mean answer length of approximately
one token.

2.3. PDF Acquisition Pipeline
To build a consistent PDF corpus from EDGAR, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission’s (SEC) public database of company filings, we implemented a retrieval pipeline
that reconstructs filings from benchmark document identifiers. For each filing, the pipeline
parses the document name, maps the ticker to its Central Index Key (CIK), queries the SEC
submissions metadata to recover the matching accession number, and then parses the filing
index to identify the main filing document. If a native PDF is available, it is downloaded
directly. Otherwise, the filing is retrieved as HTML and converted to PDF for downstream
use.

2.3.1. HTML-to-PDF Conversion
Many EDGAR filings, especially older 10-K and 10-Q submissions, are available only as
HTML. In these cases, the HTML is rendered to PDF using a headless browser. Before
rendering, linked images, stylesheets, and other referenced resources are inlined so that the
document becomes self-contained.
To better preserve the original filing structure, the converter first detects likely page
boundaries from layout cues in the HTML, such as explicit page containers, horizontal rules,
and page-number markers. It then removes source page-break rules and inserts controlled
page breaks before rendering. Oversized sections are scaled when necessary to avoid overflow
across pages. PDFs are rendered using Letter-sized pages, print-media settings, background
printing, and zero page margins. After rendering, the output is checked for successful PDF
generation and page counts are recorded when readable. Page alignment was additionally
verified manually.

2.4. Document Indexing and Retrieval Infrastructure
2.4.1. Text Extraction and Page Representation
For each filing in the corpus, page text is extracted using PyMuPDF via LangChain’s
PyMuPDFLoader interface [Chase, 2022], preserving page boundaries as metadata tuples (d, p),
where d is the document identifier and p is the 0-indexed page number. All retrieval methods
share this same extractor.
PyMuPDF was chosen as the default extractor because it is fast, stable, directly supported by LangChain’s PDF loading interface, and because FinanceBench [Islam et al., 2023]
adopts it as its reference extractor. A comparison of extraction methods on the FinanceBench
corpus is reported in Appendix D.2. Overall, the differences across extractors are small (at

2.4.2. Chunk Construction
We build the chunk corpus C by splitting page text into overlapping spans. Each chunk
is represented as a tuple (c, d, p) carrying the chunk text, the source document identifier, and
the page index. The default configuration uses a recursive character splitter with a window
of 1024 tokens and an overlap of 128 tokens, following Yepes et al. [2024]. To verify the
suitability of this 1024-token configuration for the FinanceBench corpus and to ensure our
baseline was optimized, we conducted a preliminary ablation over varying chunk sizes. The
results and configurations of these variants are detailed in Appendix D.3.
We first test naive fixed size chunking, where text is split at fixed character or token
boundaries without regard to sentence or paragraph structure. We also use recursive character splitting, where the splitter attempts to split on a priority-ordered list of separators
(paragraph breaks, newlines, periods, spaces), recursively falling back to shorter separators
when a chunk exceeds the target size. This is the default configuration that we use in the
thesis (1024-token window, 128-token overlap). Finally, we evaluate a parent-child retrieval
strategy, as an additional chunking variant. In this setup, documents are first split into larger
parent chunks of 2048 tokens with 256-token overlap, and each parent is then subdivided
into smaller child chunks of 512 tokens with 64-token overlap. Only the child chunks are
embedded and retrieved with BGE-M3, while the corresponding parent chunk is returned
as the context for generation. This separates retrieval granularity from generation context,
allowing small chunks to support precise matching while larger parent spans provide more
surrounding evidence.

2.4.3. Vector Store: ChromaDB
All dense retrieval experiments use ChromaDB [Team, 2023] as the vector store. ChromaDB was selected over FAISS [Douze et al., 2025] primarily for its native support for
structured metadata filtering. Each chunk in the index carries doc_name and page_num
as metadata fields, and ChromaDB’s where filter allows oracle document and oracle page
retrieval conditions to be implemented as first-class query constraints rather than as postretrieval filtering heuristics.

2.5. Retrieval Systems
We evaluate multiple retrieval configurations, all operating over the same shared multidocument index unless otherwise noted. This shared-index design controls for implementation differences and ensures that metric differences across methods reflect genuine retrieval
quality rather than index construction artefacts.

2.5.1. Baseline Retrievers
Dense retrieval maps the query and each chunk into a shared vector space using a biencoder, then ranks chunks by cosine similarity [Karpukhin et al., 2020]. We evaluate three
dense backbones. The primary dense retriever is BAAI/BGE-M3 [Chen et al., 2025], a multilingual and multi-granularity model that produces 1024-dimensional vectors and has strong
performance across retrieval benchmarks. It is also used as the base model for the learned
page scorer. We include sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2 [Song et al., 2020]
as a smaller general-purpose encoder that produces 768-dimensional vectors. This provides a
lighter baseline and helps separate the effect of model capacity from the effect of the retrieval
pipeline itself. Finally, we evaluate FinLang/finance-investopedia-embeddings [FinLang
Team, 2024], a domain-specific encoder pre-trained on Investopedia text that also produces
768-dimensional vectors. This model is included to test whether exposure to financial vocabulary alone, without retrieval-specific contrastive training, is sufficient to outperform
general-purpose encoders on SEC filing retrieval.
For sparse retrieval, BM25 [Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009] is implemented using the
rank-bm25 library [Brown, 2022] with a finance-aware tokenizer that preserves currency
symbols, percentage signs, and formatted numbers. SPLADE [Formal et al., 2021] expands
query and document representations via a masked language model into sparse term-weighted
vectors over the full vocabulary. We use the naver/splade-cocondenser-ensembledistil
checkpoint [Formal et al., 2022], truncating inputs to 512 tokens and retaining the top 256
weighted terms per representation for index efficiency.
The hybrid baseline combines BM25 and BGE-M3 rankings using Reciprocal Rank Fusion
(RRF) [Cormack et al., 2009]. For a chunk at rank position r, its RRF score is

2.5.2. Query Reformulation
A deterministic preprocessing step replaces common financial abbreviations (e.g., EPS,
EBITDA, FY) with their full-form equivalents and normalizes fiscal year references to a canonical format (e.g., “FY2022” to “fiscal year 2022”), improving lexical overlap with filing text
without requiring a generative model.
Hypothetical Document Embeddings (HyDE) [Gao et al., 2023a] generate a hypothetical
passage that would answer the query and embed that passage rather than the query itself. We
generate one hypothetical passage per query using Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct at temperature 0.7
and embed it with BGE-M3. Multi-HyDE [George et al., 2025] reduces variance by sampling
four independent hypothetical passages per query and averaging their BGE-M3 embeddings
into a single query representation. The prompt template is reproduced in Appendix A.2.

2.5.3. Reranking
We first implement a zero-shot cross-encoder via BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3 [Li et al.,
2023] serving as a reranker. The dense retriever first produces N = 20 candidate chunks, the
cross-encoder then assigns a scalar relevance score to each (query, chunk) pair via full crossattention, and the top k = 5 chunks by reranker score are passed to the generator [Nogueira
et al., 2019]. BGE-M3 + Multi-HyDE + ReRanker, which applies query augmentation before
retrieval and reranks the resulting candidates, constitutes the strongest non-learned baseline.
We additionally fine-tune BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3 on FinQA financial page-level data
to adapt the reranker to the domain. The training procedure and loss function are described
in Section 3.4, and at inference time it operates identically to the zero-shot variant.

2.6. Generation Setup

retrieval methods so that observed differences in answer quality can be attributed primarily
to retrieval. In the retrieval experiments, we use Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct [Team, 2025] as the
primary generator. The prompt injects the retrieved filing context together with the question,
and when supported by the tokenizer it is wrapped using the model’s chat template before
generation. The generator is run with a maximum of 256 new tokens. When running on
CUDA, the model is loaded with bitsandbytes 8-bit quantization by default, which reduces
memory requirements relative to full-precision loading and makes single-GPU inference more

Methodology

3.1. Overview
This chapter presents the methodological components for the thesis. The first is an
oracle-based evaluation framework that decomposes retrieval failures into document-level
and page-level components, providing empirical upper bounds at each granularity and a
diagnostic framework for attributing performance deficits to their underlying causes. The
second is a hierarchical page-then-chunk retrieval pipeline which leverages a learned page
scorer by domain fine-tuning a bi-encoder based on BAAI/BGE-M3 that treats pages as
the main retrieval unit. The third is a domain fine-tuned cross-encoder reranker based on
BGE-reranker-v2-m3, trained on FinQA data and evaluated zero-shot on FinanceBench,
enabling a controlled comparison between zero-shot and domain-adapted reranking. The
fourth evaluates ColQwen2 visual page scoring as an alternative Stage-1 ranker within the
same pipeline architecture, allowing a direct comparison between text-based and image-based
page representations on financial documents. The fifth describes the benchmark extension
FinQA alignment pipeline used to extend FinanceBench with verified page-level evidence
annotations from EDGAR filings, including the fuzzy-matching procedure and the exclusion
criteria applied to unverifiable examples.

3.2. Oracle-Based Evaluation Framework
A RAG pipeline can fail at two distinct stages. The retriever may fail to surface the
relevant evidence, or the generator may fail to answer correctly even when the relevant
evidence is present. Measuring end-to-end accuracy alone conflates these two sources and
makes it impossible to identify the primary bottleneck. To separate them, we construct
oracle retrieval conditions that progressively restrict the candidate pool to the gold evidence,
yielding empirical upper bounds on what retrieval improvements alone could achieve.

G-Score Gθ (q, Rk (q; C)) ≤ G-Score Gθ (q, Rk (q; C doc )) ≤ G-Score Gθ (q, Rk (q; C page )) .
(3.2.5)
Here, a robust metric refers to one that is monotonic with respect to the inclusion of gold
evidence and aligned with task correctness. On the retrieval side, metrics such as DocRec@k
and PageRec@k satisfy this property, as they depend only on whether gold documents or
pages are retrieved and therefore cannot decrease when distractors are removed. On the
generation side, metrics such as human evaluation are more robust, as they directly reflect
factual correctness. In contrast, surface-level similarity metrics such as ROUGE-L, BLEU,
or embedding-based scores may not be monotonic, as they can be influenced by irrelevant
context or lexical variation.
These bounds are defined with respect to a fixed retrieval model and therefore characterize
the performance ceiling achievable under that specific embedding and similarity function.
When evaluating optimized retrieval models, we compare their performance to these oracle
results as empirical reference points under a fixed retriever, rather than as direct upper
bounds across different embedding models. The retrieval bounds in Equation (3.2.4) hold
because removing non-gold documents and pages eliminates distractors that would otherwise
displace gold chunks from the top-k list. The generation bound in Equation (3.2.5) follows
from the fact that the generator Gθ is fixed across conditions, so improvements in retrieved
evidence quality propagate to downstream answer quality.
Equation (3.2.5) provides the diagnostic core of this framework. Any gap between endto-end performance under standard retrieval and under oracle page retrieval is attributable
purely to retrieval failure, since the generator is identical across conditions and differs only
in the evidence it receives. These oracle conditions should be read as empirical diagnostic
bounds rather than mathematical guarantees for every possible model. They restrict the
candidate set but do not directly optimize the final ranking, so a learned reranker or a
domain-tuned retriever can exceed an oracle-document score by using ranking signals that
the fixed oracle retriever does not use. This is why the experiments interpret oracle results
as reference points for localizing failure, not as absolute ceilings on all architectures.

3.3. Hierarchical Page-then-Chunk Retrieval
3.3.1. Motivation and Architecture
Standard chunk retrieval searches C directly, competing against all chunks in the corpus simultaneously. For long financial filings with repeated templates, dense tables, and
structurally similar sections across years, this means that the retriever must simultaneously
distinguish the correct filing from thousands of similar passages and locate the correct page
within hundreds of pages in that filing. These two tasks have different natural difficulty
levels, and conflating them in a single retrieval step means that the method optimized for
document-level discrimination may not be optimal for page-level localization within the correct document.
The proposed hierarchical pipeline separates these tasks explicitly. In Stage-1, a learned
page scorer ranks all pages in the corpus and selects the top-P pages. In Stage-2, standard
42

dense chunk retrieval is applied, but restricted to chunks from those P pages. This design
reduces the effective search space for chunk retrieval from |C| to approximately P · n̄ chunks,
where n̄ is the average number of chunks per page. With P = 20, this represents a reduction
of roughly two orders of magnitude for a corpus of the FinanceBench scale. The filtering
also concentrates chunk retrieval on the most semantically relevant regions of the corpus,
reducing noise from unrelated sections while preserving fine-grained selection within the
selected pages.

distribution over companies, years, filing types, and evidence style. This may bias the scorer

pages only, unannotated but still relevant pages are treated as negatives implicitly, which
may further distort the learning signal.

3.3.4. Key Design Choices
Several design choices distinguish the learned page scorer from prior hierarchical retrieval
methods.
The choice of pages as the retrieval unit, rather than arbitrary passages or sections, is
motivated by their unambiguous boundaries, their correspondence to coherent presentation
units in financial filings, and their direct annotation in FinanceBench, which provides supervision unavailable for sub-page passage boundaries. Out of all 150 FinanceBench questions,
all individual evidence is self-contained in one page, thus we assume that a page will be
self-contained.
The choice to fine-tune a separate page encoder rather than reusing the zero-shot chunklevel retriever explicitly teaches the model the asymmetry between short analytical queries
and long structured financial page text, as noted in Section 2.2.4.
The use of document-level cross-validation splits prevents the model from memorizing
filing-specific patterns and provides a cleaner estimate of generalization. Using questionlevel splits instead would allow questions about the same filing to appear in both training
and test folds, inflating reported performance.
Finally, using in-batch negatives from the gold pages of other questions provides hard
negatives without requiring explicit offline negative mining, which would require a separate
retrieval pass and introduces risk of false negatives (pages that are actually relevant but not
annotated as gold).

3.4. Fine-Tuned Cross-Encoder Reranker
3.4.1. Motivation
The zero-shot cross-encoder reranker (Section 2.5.3) applies a general-purpose relevance
model to financial passages without any domain-specific adaptation. While this is a strong
baseline, financial filings have a distinctive vocabulary and structure that a general model
may not score optimally. To quantify how much domain fine-tuning helps on the reranker,
we train a second cross-encoder on FinQA financial page data and evaluate it on the held-out
FinanceBench benchmark.

For negative supervision, we sample m = 3 pages from other training questions, preferring
examples whose source document differs from that of the current sample. These negatives
therefore act primarily as inter-document negatives. They are financially plausible pages,
often containing similar accounting language, tabular structure, and reporting patterns, but
they are not relevant answers to the current question. If the cross-document pool is too
small, the implementation falls back to sampling from any other training page. This strategy
provides a simple way to expose the reranker to realistic non-relevant financial pages without
relying on additional retrieval passes or manually curated adversarial examples.
At the same time, the negatives used in this setup are not hard negatives in the strict
retrieval sense, since they are sampled from other gold pages rather than mined as the most
confusable candidates for the current query. This design choice means that some realistic
inference-time confusions are not explicitly modeled during training, such as highly similar
passages from the same filing, adjacent pages within the same report, or near-duplicate tables
from the same company across different reporting periods. The resulting supervision should
therefore be understood as a pragmatic and domain-relevant approximation, rather than as
an exhaustive construction of the hardest possible reranking negatives.
At inference time, the fine-tuned cross-encoder reranker operates identically to its zeroshot counterpart. BGE-M3 dense retrieval first produces N = 20 candidate chunks, the
reranker scores each (query, chunk) pair in batches of 64, and the top-5 chunks by reranker
score are returned to the generator.

3.6. Benchmark Extension: FinQA Alignment Pipeline
The benchmark extension used in this thesis is based on the FinQA labels provided by
LoFIN only [Choe et al., 2025]. While HiREC releases the broader LoFIN benchmark, the
local SEC-QA records did not contain gold evidence text or page-level annotations that
could be reliably mapped to PDF pages. SEC-QA was therefore excluded from the pagelevel benchmark, as downloadable PDFs alone are not sufficient for PageRec@k evaluation,
which requires verified gold pages.
Each LoFIN FinQA example contains a structured identifier encoding the company, fiscal
year, and source document. This identifier is matched to the corresponding original FinQA
record, from which the surrounding evidence text is extracted. To recover page-level supervision, we construct an evidence anchor by concatenating a fixed number of sentences from
the end of the pre_text field and the beginning of the post_text field, capturing the local
context in which the answer appears.
For each candidate filing, page text is extracted from the corresponding PDF, as in Section
2.4.1. Page-level alignment is then performed using token-set fuzzy matching implemented
via rapidfuzz [Bachmann, 2023]. Specifically, we compute a similarity score between the
anchor and each page using the token_set_ratio function, which is robust to partial containment of the anchor within a longer page. This is well-suited to our setting, as the anchor
typically represents a short excerpt of the full page content. The highest-scoring page is
selected and accepted as the gold page only if its similarity exceeds a threshold τ = 0.70,
otherwise the example is excluded from page-level supervision.
From the LoFIN FinQA test split (1,147 rows), 530 examples were successfully matched
to FinQA records and had candidate PDF mappings identified via identifier matching and
PDF lookup. Of these, 503 (94.9%) produced PDF-verified page evidence. From the 503 set,
fuzzy-match scores range from 0.71 to 1.00 and a mean of 0.88. The remaining 27 aligned
examples were excluded from page-level supervision since 13 fell below the fuzzy-matching
threshold, and 14 did not have a valid PDF available in the local corpus at the time of
processing.

A clear structural pattern emerges across all methods, in particular that document-level
retrieval is largely strong, while page-level localization remains the dominant bottleneck.
High-performing dense retrievers consistently achieve DocRec@5 above 0.90, yet PageRec@5
remains substantially lower, even for the strongest non-oracle systems. This persistent gap
reflects a fundamental limitation of similarity-based retrieval in long, internally repetitive
documents. Financial filings contain many near-duplicate sections (e.g., repeated tables
across years), so identifying the correct document is relatively easy, but distinguishing the
specific evidence-bearing page requires fine-grained reasoning beyond coarse semantic similarity.
53

The relative performance of sparse, dense, and hybrid methods is best understood through
their treatment of query–document mismatch as seen in Section 2.2.4. Sparse retrieval,
represented by BM25, performs poorly across all metrics, reflecting its reliance on exact
lexical overlap. Queries in this benchmark are short and analytical, while evidence pages are
expressed in formal, domain-specific language and often embedded within tables, limiting
the effectiveness of term matching. SPLADE improves substantially over BM25 by learning
vocabulary expansion, but remains below dense retrieval methods, as its representations
are still constrained to the vocabulary space and cannot fully capture semantic equivalence
[Formal et al., 2021].
Dense retrieval models overcome this limitation by embedding queries and documents
into a shared semantic space. General-purpose encoders such as MPNet already outperform
sparse methods, while BGE-M3 achieves near-saturated document recall, indicating that
semantic matching is sufficient for identifying the correct filing. However, the gap between
BGE-M3 and its reranked variant highlights the limitation of independent encoding. Because
bi-encoders compute representations separately, they cannot model fine-grained interactions
between query and passage. Adding a cross-encoder reranker (BGE-M3 + RR) improves
PageRec@5 substantially, consistent with prior findings that joint encoding enables more
precise relevance estimation in reranking settings [Nogueira et al., 2019].
Hybrid retrieval does not consistently improve performance on this benchmark. While
hybrid methods are often effective when sparse and dense signals are complementary, the
results here suggest that the lexical signal contributes limited additional benefit. As the
sparse weight increases, performance tends to degrade, and even dense-heavy fusion does
not consistently exceed the dense baseline. Since there is a query-document asymmetry on
this benchmark, where queries are short and do not contain distinctive lexical anchors (e.g.,
exact table headers or entity names), term-based matching is limited. These findings suggest
that on this benchmark, dense representations provide the dominant retrieval signal, while
the contribution of sparse features appears more limited.
The largest gains among non-supervised approaches arise from query reformulation.
Methods such as HyDE and Multi-HyDE consistently improve PageRec@5 relative to the
dense baseline, indicating that transforming the query into a document-like representation
reduces the mismatch between query and evidence distributions. This behaviour is consistent with the formulation of HyDE, which uses generated pseudo-documents to better
align embedding spaces [Gao et al., 2023a]. The additional improvement from Multi-HyDE
suggests that averaging over multiple generations stabilizes the representation and reduces
the variance introduced by any single synthetic query. When combined with reranking, this

produces the strongest off-the-shelf baseline, highlighting that query-side improvements and
ranking refinements address complementary aspects of the retrieval problem.
The proposed methods further demonstrate that explicitly modelling the structure of
the retrieval task yields substantial gains. The page-then-chunk pipeline achieves a large
improvement in PageRec@5, closing most of the gap to the oracle document condition. This
improvement arises from search space restriction. By first selecting a small set of highprobability pages, the system reduces noise and increases the density of relevant candidates
before performing fine-grained retrieval. This coarse-to-fine strategy is consistent with prior
work on hierarchical retrieval, which shows that multi-stage filtering improves performance
in long-document settings [Gao et al., 2023b]. The fine-tuned reranker (BGE-M3 + MultiHyDE + FT-RR) achieves comparable performance through a different mechanism, learning domain-specific relevance signals that improve ranking without explicitly restricting the
search space.
The visual variant of the proposed pipeline, based on ColQwen2, underperforms its textbased counterpart across all metrics. While it retains moderate document-level recall, its
PageRec@5 and context overlap scores remain substantially lower. This reflects a mismatch
between the inductive biases of vision-language models and the structure of financial documents. Visual encoders are designed to capture layout and high-level semantic patterns, but
in this domain the discriminative signal lies in precise numerical content and subtle textual
differences between structurally similar tables. As a result, visual similarity tends to emphasize layout correspondence rather than factual correctness, leading to confusion between
near-identical pages.
Finally, context quality (BLEU@5 and ROUGE-L@5) highlights the role of ranking beyond document-level recall. Both proposed methods outperform all baselines and match or
exceed the oracle document condition, indicating that optimizing the ordering of retrieved
content is as important as identifying the correct document. The page-then-chunk model
achieves the highest ROUGE-L@5, benefiting from in-domain training on FinanceBench and
restriction to a small set of high-probability pages. However, this approach relies on supervision from the target dataset and may not generalize as effectively. The ColQwen2 variant
further illustrates the limitations of alternative representations, with substantially lower page
recall and context overlap. In contrast, the fine-tuned reranker (BGE-M3 + Multi-HyDE +
FT-RR), trained out-of-domain on FinQA, achieves comparable context quality, indicating
that domain-adaptive reranking provides a more robust and transferable improvement. The
remaining gap to the oracle page condition confirms that page-level retrieval remains the
primary limitation.

4.2.5. Retrieval Failure Analysis
The aggregate and stratified results reveal high-level structural patterns in where and
why retrieval fails. The following qualitative analysis provides a closer look at the underlying
mechanisms. Three failure modes are distinguished. A total failure occurs when neither the
gold document nor any of its gold pages appear in the top-k results. A partial-document
failure occurs when the gold document is retrieved but the gold pages are missed. A partialpage failure occurs when the question has multiple gold pages and only a subset are recovered.
The subsequent analysis examines each question type through this lens, grounded in concrete
examples where they best illustrate the underlying mechanism.
4.2.5.1. Metrics-Generated Questions. A dominant failure mode for metrics-generated
questions under dense retrieval alone is temporal confusion. In these cases, the system
retrieves a structurally correct and plausible page from the correct company, but from
the wrong reporting period. This constitutes a total retrieval error rather than a partialdocument failure, as the gold filing itself is not retrieved. Financial statements are highly
standardized across years, with nearly identical layouts, row labels, and column structures,
differing primarily in the reported values and year identifiers. Dense similarity scores collapse this distinction, treating pages from different years as near-duplicates. Consider the
following question from FinanceBench,

What is the year-end FY2018 net PP&E for 3M? Answer in USD billions.

Table 4.4 decomposes the retrieval outcome. As can be observed, every structural dimension
is correct, the retrieved page is a balance sheet, from the correct company, with the PP&E
row and the year-end column. The issue is that the filing year is wrong. The system returns
the FY2022 balance sheet instead of FY2018, yielding a generated answer of $9.17B rather
than the correct $8.70B. The side-by-side pages are reproduced in Appendix C.1.

4.2.5.2. Novel-Generated and Domain-Relevant Questions. Novel-generated and
Domain-relevant questions require company-specific reasoning, synthesis across adjacent passages, or computation that cannot be answered by locating a single sentence or table cell.
The most consequential failure mode is a lexical gap between the query vocabulary and the
vocabulary of the answer-bearing page. The following example illustrates this directly (see
Appendix C.2 for the full document pages and additional examples).

The query uses informal language (“liquidate assets per shareholder”) that does not lexically match the financial term TBVPS reported in the filing. Retrieval consequently fails

to surface the correct capital-metrics page and instead ranks balance sheet pages containing
assets and liabilities. While these pages are semantically related, they require additional
derivation and context to produce the answer. The generator performs a from-scratch calculation using retrieved values and produces a numerically plausible but incorrect estimate,
rather than reading off the directly reported metric. This failure mode is particularly resistant to reranking because the reranker also lacks a signal connecting “liquidate assets”
to “tangible book value”, it would require query expansion or domain glossary mappings to
bridge the vocabulary gap at retrieval time.
This example illustrates a broader failure pattern shared by both novel-generated and
domain-relevant questions. When the query uses language that does not align with how the
relevant concept is expressed in the document, retrieval surfaces pages that are topically
related but do not contain the exact evidence required. In such cases, the correct page
is often absent from the candidate set, limiting the effectiveness of reranking. While finetuning improves performance by learning stronger query–passage interactions, its gains are
concentrated on questions where the target evidence follows more familiar patterns. Queries
requiring implicit mappings or less direct terminology remain challenging, highlighting the
importance of improving query representation or vocabulary alignment at the retrieval stage.
4.2.5.3. Annotation Limitation. A recurring source of false retrieval failures is benchmark annotation that designates a single gold page per question even when the text required
to answer appears on other pages of the same filing. Under the failure taxonomy, this example is classified as a partial-document failure, as the correct document is retrieved but
the annotated gold page is not present within the top-k. Because PageRec@5 is computed
against the annotated gold set, retrieving an equally informative page that was not annotated
counts as a miss, artificially deflating recall while the downstream generator still produces
the correct answer. The following example illustrates this directly.

4.2.5.4. Failure Mode Summary. Across question types, retrieval failures can be attributed to a small number of recurring mechanisms. For metrics-generated questions, the
primary issue is temporal confusion, where structurally similar pages from different reporting
periods are indistinguishable under dense similarity. For domain-relevant questions, ambiguity and domain-specific terminology lead to multiple plausible matches or missed evidence,
limiting the effectiveness of both reranking and query reformulation. For novel-generated
questions, lexical mismatch between query phrasing and document terminology causes retrieval to surface related but incorrect pages, with fine-tuning providing the most consistent
improvements in this setting. In addition, annotation limitations introduce systematic discrepancies between measured and actual performance, as valid evidence may appear on
non-annotated pages. These findings highlight that retrieval failures arise not only from
recall limitations, but from mismatches between query language, document structure, and
evaluation assumptions.
This motivates the generation failure analysis in Section 4.3.5, which examines the residual errors that persist even when retrieval succeeds.

affected by both retrieval and generation difficulty. The larger model is better able to reason
over the correct narrative evidence, but this advantage is mostly hidden under standard
retrieval because the necessary evidence is frequently absent or poorly ranked.
Novel-generated questions are the setting where model scale has the strongest effect under
realistic retrieval. With Multi-HyDE + FT-RR, human accuracy increases from 0.48 for the
7B model to 0.62 for the 14B model. This is the largest top-k scaling gain among the
three question types, suggesting that novel questions benefit more from the larger model’s
ability to interpret imperfect or partially relevant context. The automatic metrics follow
the same broad direction. The proposed method slightly improves over the dense baseline,
while oracle pages provide the strongest scores, reaching ROUGE-L 0.25 and BERTScore
F1 0.24. However, the oracle gain for the 7B model on novel questions, from 0.48 to 0.60,
is smaller than the corresponding gain on metrics-generated questions. This suggests that
novel questions are not limited only by evidence localization. They also require broader
synthesis and answer formulation, for which generator capacity becomes more important.

4.3.5. Generation Failure Analysis
Under oracle page conditions, Numeric Match is 0.70, meaning roughly 30% of numeric
errors persist even with perfect retrieval. Similarly, we achieve 72.67% human accuracy in
the Oracle framework. These residual failures fall into three primary patterns.
The first major pattern is positional bias and long context, which occurs when a question
requires evidence from two pages simultaneously and both must appear in the retrieved
context for the generator to produce the correct answer. When one of the required pages is
ranked low and consequently appears at the tail of the context window, the generator tends
to overlook it. This behaviour is consistent with the well-documented lost-in-the-middle
phenomenon in long-context LLMs [Liu et al., 2024].
A second pattern involves a domain knowledge deficit, where certain questions require the
generator to select the correct inputs to a formula from among several plausible candidates
that appear on the same page. Without sufficient domain knowledge to distinguish which
inputs are appropriate, the generator may apply a formula that is conceptually incorrect,
producing a wrong answer despite correct retrieval.
The third pattern is multi-step arithmetic failures when questions require the generator
to combine two or more figures from the retrieved context to compute a final answer, rather
than reading a directly stated value. While the 14B model handles this substantially better
71

than the 3B and 7B models, errors in multi-step computation remain common when the
model attempts to synthesize multiple data points.
We can observe how these errors manifest, and frequently compound, in the following
two examples. Example 6 illustrates positional bias and noisy context.
Example 6 | Positional Bias
Question. What is the FY2015 operating cash flow ratio for Adobe? Operating cash
flow ratio is defined as cash from operations divided by total current liabilities. Round
to two decimal places.
Expected pages. p. 58 (total current liabilities) and p. 62 (cash from operations).
Retrieved pages. p. 62 (1st), p. 64 (2nd), p. 60 (3rd), p. 71 (4th), p. 58 (5th).
Expected answer. $1,469,502 ÷ $2,213,556 ≈ 0.66
Generated answer. “The net cash provided by operating activities for FY2015 is
$1,469,502 thousand. However, the total current liabilities are not directly provided in
the given context. We cannot compute the operating cash flow ratio.”
Both required pages are retrieved, but the page containing total current liabilities (p. 58)
is ranked 5th and falls at the tail of the context window. The generator correctly reads the
first required value from the top-ranked page but fails to attend to the second value, which
is buried in a dense table at the edge of its attention span. Consequently, it produces a “not
determinable” response despite the evidence being present.

arithmetic failure.

To evaluate whether the retrieval pipeline’s performance generalizes beyond the 150question FinanceBench subset, we expanded our evaluation using the FinQA subset of the
LoFIN/HiREC benchmark. As detailed in Section 3.6, this extension aligns new financial
questions to verified gold PDF pages from SEC filings, addressing the document diversity limitations of the original dataset. Combining these aligned examples with the original dataset
creates a much larger global index, testing the system’s ability to maintain precise page-level
localization when faced with a significantly expanded pool of distractor documents. The
following statistics outline the composition of this extended global corpus before examining
the resulting retrieval performance.

4.4.3. Analysis
Table 4.12 reveals several inversions between the two corpora that are directly informative
about what each retrieval method actually learns.
The most striking inversion is BM25, which achieves PageRec@5 of 0.11 on FinanceBench
but 0.42 on FinQA, the second best result on that corpus. This performance gap can be
related to the query-corpus lexical asymmetry quantified in Section 2.2.4. FinQA questions
were written by annotators working directly from specific tables in filings, so the questions
reproduce exact company names, fiscal year labels, and table header terms that appear
verbatim on the answer page. This structural alignment yields a much lower median OOV
rate of 0.46 (Table 2.3), giving BM25 a strong lexical signal. Conversely, FinanceBench
questions are concept-based and rarely share tokens with the answer page. The resulting
vocabulary mismatch, evidenced by a severe median OOV rate of 0.70 and a nearly disjoint
JSD of 0.95, renders pure lexical matching highly ineffective. Hybrid RRF 50/50 benefits
directly from this dynamic, incorporating BM25’s strong lexical signal lifts PageRec@5 to
0.43 on FinQA, while its FinanceBench score (0.28) reflects the noise that BM25 introduces
on concept-based queries.
The opposite inversion occurs for Multi-HyDE, which is among the strongest methods
on FinanceBench (PageRec@5 of 0.45) but falls below the dense baseline on FinQA. HyDE
generates a hypothetical passage that would answer the query. On FinanceBench, this hypothetical resembles the evidence page because questions ask about specific reported values.
On FinQA, many questions require numerical computation over a table, so the hypothetical
75

answer is a single computed number rather than a passage resembling the source table. Thus
the generated hypothesis actively points the retriever away from the structured evidence.
Dense BGE-M3 shows the smallest performance gap between the two corpora (0.34 vs
0.30), confirming it as a stable backbone across query styles. The proposed method (MultiHyDE + FT-RR) is the strongest on the FinanceBench subset (0.46) but does not outperform
the plain dense baseline on FinQA. The off-the-shelf cross-encoder reranker performs even
worse on FinQA, below the dense baseline. Two factors explain this underperformance. First,
the cross-encoder lacks exposure to financial tables and tends to down-rank pages where the
extracted text does not read like natural language. Second, the fine-tuned reranker suffers
from a format mismatch. It was trained on FinQA evidence formatted as structured JSON
fields, whereas the evaluation index uses raw PDF extractions where tables are linearized
differently. This discrepancy limits the reranker’s effectiveness even on the corpus it was
nominally trained for.
Ultimately, these cross-corpus inversions demonstrate that retrieval performance in financial QA is highly sensitive to both query construction and document formatting. While
dense embeddings provide a relatively stable baseline, specialized techniques, whether lexical matching, query generation, or fine-tuned reranking, are brittle when applied outside their optimal conditions. This highlights a central challenge for end-to-end financial
RAG pipelines, achieving robust generalized performance requires either dynamically routing
queries to the most appropriate retrieval strategy, or developing more unified architectures
capable of natively handling both conceptual narratives and structured tabular data without
succumbing to format mismatch.

Conclusion

This thesis studied retrieval-augmented generation for financial question answering over long
SEC filings, with retrieval as its central object of study. The main argument is that retrieval
failure in this setting should not be treated as a single end-to-end error. A pipeline can
fail because it retrieves the wrong filing, because it retrieves the correct filing but misses
the relevant page, or because it finds the right page but still provides the generator with
incomplete or noisy context. Separating these failure modes is necessary for understanding
where the system should be improved.
The main finding is that financial RAG systems often perform reasonably well at identifying the correct document, but still struggle to locate the answer-bearing page inside that
document. This gap is especially important in financial QA, where the answer often depends
on a specific table, reporting period, or numerical field inside a long and repetitive filing. In
this setting, finding the right document is only a first step. Reliable generation depends on
retrieving the exact local evidence needed to answer the question.

5.1. Summary of Contributions
The first contribution is an oracle-based evaluation framework that decomposes retrieval
failure across document, page, and chunk levels. By restricting the candidate set first to
the gold filing and then to the gold pages, the framework provides empirical upper bounds
at different levels of granularity. It also defines diagnostic gaps that measure how much
performance is lost because the retriever searches the full corpus rather than the correct
filing, and how much downstream answer quality can improve when the generator is given the
correct evidence pages. Applied to FinanceBench, this analysis shows that the main retrieval
bottleneck is not simply document discovery. The larger gap lies in within-document page
localization.
The second contribution is an experimental study of adaptation strategies for financial
retrieval over long filings. Building on the oracle analysis, this thesis evaluates a hierarchical
page-then-chunk retrieval design in which pages are ranked first and chunk retrieval is then

restricted to the highest-ranked pages. This setup tests whether page-level selection can
reduce noise from irrelevant filing sections while preserving fine-grained retrieval within the
most promising parts of the corpus. Within this framework, a BGE-M3 bi-encoder is finetuned as a page scorer using FinanceBench page-level supervision under cross-validation.
This experiment should be interpreted as a diagnostic test of page-level task adaptation
rather than as a fully independent algorithmic contribution. In parallel, a cross-encoder
reranker fine-tuned on FinQA financial page data is applied zero-shot to FinanceBench, allowing the effect of out-of-domain financial adaptation to be evaluated separately. ColQwen2
is also tested as a visual page-scoring alternative to assess whether page images can bypass
limitations of text extraction. Together, these experiments examine how domain tuning,
task tuning, and document representation choices affect retrieval in financial QA.
The third contribution is a systematic evaluation of retrieval and generation across multiple dimensions. Retrieval is evaluated through document recall, page recall, and contextquality metrics, and the results are further broken down by question type and filing type.
Generation is evaluated with automatic metrics, numeric match, and human accuracy across
different generator sizes and retrieval conditions. This analysis shows that retrieval quality
and generator capacity interact in different ways across the benchmark. Metrics-generated
questions are highly sensitive to evidence localization, since the model must retrieve the
exact numerical field required for the answer. Domain-relevant questions often require better reasoning over narrative evidence. Novel-generated questions benefit more visibly from
model scaling under imperfect retrieval. The filing-type analysis also shows that 10-Ks,
10-Qs, 8-Ks, and earnings transcripts induce different error patterns.
The fourth contribution is a benchmark extension pipeline based on LoFIN and HiREC.
The thesis aligns FinQA questions to verified gold PDF pages in SEC filings through fuzzy
evidence matching, producing an expanded evaluation index that combines FinanceBench
and FinQA. This extension provides a useful stress test for retrieval methods beyond the
original 150 FinanceBench questions. The cross-corpus results reveal that no single method
is uniformly strongest across FinanceBench and FinQA. BM25 improves on FinQA because
many questions are written close to the wording of filing tables, while Multi-HyDE is more
effective on FinanceBench where lexical mismatch is stronger. This method inversion shows
that retrieval performance is strongly shaped by how questions are written and how evidence
is expressed in the corpus.

5.2. Limitations and Future Work
The training procedure for the learned page scorer is limited by the available supervision and negative sampling strategy. The model is trained through cross-validation on
78

FinanceBench page-level annotations, which helps use the small dataset efficiently, but does
not provide a large independent training set. In addition, the negative examples are not
always the hardest possible financial negatives. The most difficult cases are often pages from
the same filing that discuss similar metrics, contain similar tables, or differ only by reporting period. These near-duplicate pages are exactly the cases that cause temporal confusion
in SEC filings. Future work should use intra-document hard negative mining so that the
page scorer and reranker learn to distinguish the gold page from highly confusable pages
in the same filing. Combining the page scorer as Stage 1 with Multi-HyDE and the finetuned reranker in Stage 2 is also a natural extension that remains unevaluated, where the
interaction between these two domain-adapted components could yield further gains.
Second, the generator evaluation is limited to open-source models up to 14B parameters.
The scaling results show that larger generators make better use of retrieved evidence, but
the thesis does not evaluate frontier-scale models or larger open models in the 70B range.
It is therefore unclear whether the retrieval bottlenecks observed here remain equally strong
with much larger generators, or whether stronger generators can compensate for some forms
of noisy context. Future work should repeat the oracle and top-k comparisons with larger
models to measure whether retrieval quality and model scale remain complementary.
Third, the experimental results do not fully measure variance across random seeds, training runs, and indexing choices. Some results are based on single runs, and the thesis does
not report means and standard deviations over multiple random seeds for every experiment.
This limits the strength of claims about small differences between methods. A more rigorous
follow-up should repeat fine-tuning, indexing, retrieval, and generation under multiple seeds,
then report confidence intervals and significance tests for the main comparisons.
Fourth, the stratified analyses by question type and filing type are limited by sample
size. FinanceBench contains only 150 questions, and the non-10-K filing groups are much
smaller than the 10-K group. Each question-type subgroup is also small enough that a few
examples can change the apparent trend. These breakdowns are useful for diagnosing where
methods tend to succeed or fail, but they should be interpreted as directional rather than
definitive. Future work should validate these patterns on a larger benchmark with enough
examples to support statistical testing within each question and document category.
Fifth, the benchmark itself limits the generality of the conclusions. FinanceBench is
heavily skewed toward 10-K annual reports, and many of the strongest numerical conclusions
are driven by metrics-generated questions. This means that aggregate results are influenced
by long annual filings and numerical table lookup. These are important cases, but they
do not cover the full range of financial analyst questions. The benchmark extension helps
broaden the evaluation, but it remains limited because only the FinQA portion could be

79

reliably aligned to PDF pages, while the SEC-QA portion could not be added due to missing
verifiable gold evidence text. The resulting extension is still not diverse enough in filing type,
question style, and evidence structure. A larger benchmark with verified page-level evidence
across 10-Ks, 10-Qs, 8-Ks, earnings transcripts, and more open-ended analyst questions
remains an important future contribution.
Finally, the results show that there is no universally strongest retrieval method across
question types, document types, and datasets. Dense retrieval, query reformulation, reranking, page-first retrieval, and sparse lexical matching each help in different settings. This
suggests two possible directions. One is to build a router that selects the retrieval strategy
based on the question, document type, and expected evidence structure. Another is to train
a more universal financial retriever that is robust across lexical, semantic, tabular, and conversational evidence. The results in this thesis suggest that such systems should be evaluated
across multiple datasets and stratified settings rather than only through aggregate scores.

5.3. Broader Implications
The broader implication of this thesis is that long-document RAG should be evaluated
at the level where retrieval actually fails. In financial QA, the important question is often
not whether the system found the right filing, but whether it found the right page and the
right evidence within that filing. The oracle framework provides a simple way to locate this
bottleneck before investing in more complex retrieval or generation methods.
The results also show that larger generators do not remove the need for better retrieval.
Model scaling improves answer quality, especially when the correct evidence is available,
but it cannot reliably recover information that was never retrieved. Better generators can
amplify the value of good evidence, but they do not replace the need to retrieve that evidence
in the first place.
Beyond finance, the same issue appears in other long, structured document domains such
as legal filings, clinical records, scientific papers, and technical reports. In all of these settings,
the relevant evidence may occupy only a small part of a long document, and retrieving a
broadly relevant document may still leave the generator without the information needed to
answer correctly.
