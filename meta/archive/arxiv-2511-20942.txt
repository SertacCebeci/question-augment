Symbolic-LLM Hybrid Architecture

Abstract
In procedural skill learning, instructional explanations must
convey not just steps, but the causal, goal-directed, and compositional logic behind them. Large language models (LLMs)
often produce fluent yet shallow responses that miss this
structure. We present Ivy, an AI coaching system that delivers structured, multi-step explanations by combining symbolic Task-Method-Knowledge (TMK) models with a generative interpretation layer-an LLM that constructs explanations
while being constrained by TMK structure. TMK encodes
causal transitions, goal hierarchies, and problem decompositions, and guides the LLM within explicit structural bounds.
We evaluate Ivy against responses against GPT and retrievalaugmented GPT baselines using expert and independent annotations across three inferential dimensions. Results show
that symbolic constraints consistently improve the structural
quality of explanations for “how” and “why” questions. This
study demonstrates a scalable AI for education approach that
strengthens the pedagogical value of AI-generated explanations in intelligent coaching systems.

Introduction
In Spring 2025, Ivy (Dass et al. 2025) is an AI coaching
system that was deployed in an online graduate-level AI
course at a large U.S. university [Redacted for double-blind
review], where it answered students’ questions about procedural skills. Across multiple pilot studies, Ivy’s responses
were perceived fluent and often helpful, yet we observed
a recurring gap: explanations can lack the structured logic
instructors expect for teaching procedural skills-how steps
depend on state (causality), why steps serve an overarching goal (teleology), and how problems decompose into subgoals (decomposition) (Lum et al. 2025).
This gap matters educationally. Learners increasingly
rely on AI-generated explanations when practicing procedural tasks like writing code, debugging, or constructing
arguments. But skill transfer, the benchmark of meaningful learning, depends not only on surface fluency but on
understanding why and how procedures work (Bransford
et al. 2000). Transfer requires causal insight into the logic
of tasks-a hallmark of expert reasoning that unconstrained
LLMs may fail to replicate (Bransford et al. 2000). Thus,

Currently, Ivy uses Task-Method-Knowledge (TMK)

Related Work
Structured Reasoning and Transfer
Long-standing work in the learning sciences shows that
successful skill transfer requires more than surface fluency; learners must understand underlying principles and
know when/how/why to apply them (Bransford et al. 2000).
Causal understanding in particular distinguishes expert reasoning and supports generalization across tasks (Bransford et al. 2000; Ritscher, Lincoln, and Grotzer 2003),
while compositional skill representations help learners adapt
knowledge to new contexts (Lamri and Lubart 2023).
Although instruction emphasizes mechanistic (cause-andeffect) explanations, teleological biases can reappear even
in trained adults under cognitive load (Kelemen and Rosset
2009); targeted interventions can improve mechanistic reasoning (González Galli, Thompson, and Lombrozo 2022),
and distinguishing valid purpose-based accounts from misconceptions remains pedagogically important (Kampourakis
2020). Our work operationalizes these insights by explicitly scaffolding causal transitions, goal–mechanism linkages, and problem decompositions in generated explanations.

Constrained Generation for Multi-Step Inferencing
While retrieval augmented generation (RAG) improve relevance, it does not guarantee structured reasoning in explanations (Gao et al. 2023). Recent methods add formal
constraints to LLM decoding: CRANE uses domain grammars to improve symbolic QA (Banerjee et al. 2025), and
grammar-constrained decoding can outperform in-context
examples on logic tasks (Raspanti, Ozcelebi, and Holenderski 2025). Retrieval-Augmented Thoughts (RAT) iterates retrieval and reasoning to elicit multi-hop chains (Wang et al.
2024). Our approach complements these lines by separating symbolic control from generative synthesis: structured
TMK representations define the inferential space, and the
LLM synthesizes explanations within those constraints.

Hybrid Symbolic-Neural Architectures for
Reducing Hallucinations
Surveys of knowledge-aware LLM pipelines find structured
grounding reduces hallucinations and benefits smaller models in particular (Agrawal et al. 2024). Domain systems
combining knowledge graphs with LLMs improve factuality (e.g., KGT in biomedical QA) (Feng et al. 2025), and
structured-output RAG reduces hallucinations and supports
out-of-domain generalization (Béchard and Ayala 2024).
Complementary detection methods use mechanistic signals
to flag hallucinations (e.g., ReDeEP) (Sun et al. 2025). Unlike approaches that treat structure as static context, we use
symbolic representations as active generative constraints,
yielding explanations that are both more reliable and more
pedagogically structured.

Constrained Generation Architecture
Two-layered Inferencing Architecture
We propose a two-layered inferencing architecture for AI
coaching systems that support procedural skill learning. As
an example, we ground it in a concrete skill “Applying Semantic Networks” to solve the classic Guards and Prisoners problem. Suppose a learner asks: “How do we safely
return a guard to the east bank without violating safety
constraints?” Our system answers this by coordinating two
kinds of inference. A symbolic control layer encodes the permissible logic of the goal-what moves are allowed, under
what conditions, and toward which subgoals-while a generative layer renders those constraints as a coherent, contextsensitive explanation (Figure 1).
The symbolic layer provides the structure that instructors
expect to see in procedural instruction: causal transitions
(state-dependent guards and failure branches), teleological
linkage (steps justified by the subgoals they advance), and
problem decomposition (named subgoals and atomic operations). The generative layer then verbalizes this structure so
that the answer is both fluent and faithful to the underlying
procedure.

We hypothesize that this four-stage design keeps the generative model expressive yet bounded. Stages 1–2 determine
whether TMK constraints apply to the learner’s query and
identify the relevant portions of structured knowledge; Stage
3 enforces those constraints during generation; Stage 4 polishes the output to align with instructional norms. In the next
section, we empirically discuss how the TMK-Structured
model drives the measured gains in teleology and, especially, decomposition, while maintaining strong causal coherence.

Structured Constraints And Inferential
Quality: A System-Level Evaluation
To assess the impact of TMK model constraints on the
inferential quality of AI-generated instructional responses,
we designed a structured comparative evaluation across
four system2 conditions: (1) Standard GPT (an off-theshelf ChatGPT model), (2) RAG-GPT (GPT enhanced with
retrieval-augmented generation over AI course transcripts),
(3) Ivy+TMK-Basic, and (4) Ivy+TMK-Structured. This
evaluation adopts a system-level perspective focused on inferential coherence in explanations, rather than learner outcomes.
Broad benchmarks such as LogiQA (Liu et al. 2020) and
HellaSwag (Zellers et al. 2018) evaluate general inferencing
capabilities, but their breadth makes it difficult to isolate the
specific cognitive competencies involved in complex problem solving (Liu et al. 2020; Zellers et al. 2018; Patil and
Jadon 2025). We therefore adopt a targeted, course-bound
evaluation: within a graduate-level AI course, we focus on
particular procedural skills. Following principles from cognitive evaluation research (Evans 2006), we decompose each
skill into Goals, Mechanisms, and Concepts and assess each
separately (Kiciman et al. 2024). This design yields a granular, interpretable picture of TMK capability and supports
skill-specific improvement strategies.
We curated a dataset of 20 procedural skill questions
spanning four skills modeled by Ivy (five per skill). Each
system generated a response for all 20 questions, resulting

Expert-Judged Correctness
Across 80 system outputs (5 questions × 4 skills × 4 systems; 20 per system), TMK-constrained systems substantially outperformed the baselines on expert-judged correctness. The proportion of fully correct answers (CorrectnessScore = 2) was 0.65 for both Ivy+TMK-Basic and Ivy+TMKStructured, versus 0.35 for RAG-GPT and 0.15 for Standard
GPT. Error profiles reinforce this separation: TMK-Basic
had the fewest outright failures (0.10 incorrect; 0.25 partial),
TMK-Structured traded some partials for more zeros (0.20
incorrect; 0.15 partial), RAG was volatile (0.35 correct vs.
0.35 incorrect; 0.30 partial), and Standard GPT mostly failed
or hedged (0.45 incorrect; 0.40 partial; 0.15 correct). These
patterns indicate that structured constraints reduce failure
rates and raise the ceiling on factual validity relative to unconstrained generation.

Conclusion and Future Work
This paper introduced a constrained generation architecture that separates symbolic control from generative synthesis to improve instructional explanations for procedural
skills. Ivy operationalizes this design by using TMK models to encode causal transitions, teleological linkages, and
problem decompositions, while an LLM performs contextsensitive synthesis within those constraints. Unlike retrievalonly pipelines, TMK models act as active scaffolds that
shape both what is said and how it is structured.
Across correctness and affordance-aware structure,
Ivy+TMK-Structured produces explanations that are both
more reliable (higher expert-judged correctness; fewer failures) and more pedagogically structured (higher expert
means; stronger novice agreement with expert expectations),
with the largest gains in problem decomposition—the core
capability TMK is designed to scaffold. This supports our
primary hypothesis that TMK-constrained generation can
improve the prevalence and quality of causal, teleological, and compositional inferencing relative to unconstrained
baselines.

Limitations
Our study is intentionally system-level and scoped: (i) the
dataset is modest (5 questions per system over four skills),
which limits generalizability; (ii) correctness was judged by
one domain expert per skill, so inter-rater reliability on correctness is not measured; and (iii) we evaluate explanation
quality, not learning outcomes, so effects on transfer or student performance are out of scope.

Future Work
We will automate TMK-Structured construction using a
human-in-the-loop process to cover full courses rather than a
subset of skills. Concretely, we aim to (a) induce goal hierarchies, finite-state mechanisms, and concept ontologies from
syllabi, lesson transcripts, and worked examples; (b) autoderive DimensionExpected masks from model structure; and
(c) validate models via unit tests and counterexample-guided
refinement. Our goal is to make TMK-supported coaching broadly deployable. In partnership with instructors, we
will co-design explanation templates and dimension rubrics,
then re-deploy Ivy in live courses to study learning outcomes. We will evaluate whether TMK-Structured-guided
responses improve skill acquisition and transfer, and analyze
alignment between expert expectations and novice recognition at scale. Insights will feed back into TMK authoring and
affordance modeling.

A: TMK-Structured Model for Guards and
Prisoners Problem
Before we present key aspects of the TMK-Structured
framework for modeling a skill, note that the main paper
focused on the Method component for the Guards and Prisoners Problem (GPP). Here, we complete the picture by
outlining the Task and Concept components used with that
Method, and we include representative JSON snippets from
the GPP model that Ivy uses to generate answers to learner
questions.
A Task–Method–Knowledge (TMK) model is organized
across three core components and represented in standard
JSON. This appendix adapts foundational material (Murdock and Goel 2008; Rugaber 2011; Goel and Rugaber
2017) to document the schema and usage patterns we employed. Each component plays a distinct role:
• Task: specifies the instructional objective (what is to be
achieved), including parameters, pre/postconditions, and
a pointer to its procedural realization.
• Method: specifies the procedure that achieves the
objective (e.g., a finite-state machine with transitions
guarded by data conditions).
• Knowledge: specifies domain entities, predicates, and
relations the Task and Method depend on.
In the subsections below, we summarize each component’s purpose and schema as used in TMK-Structured
model for GPP and provide representative JSON. In this
appendix, TMK values (e.g., natural-language goal descriptions) appear in italics. Formal TMK syntax elements, such
as schema keys, function names, or mechanism references,
are shown in a sans-serif font.

The TransportPassengersAcrossRiver goal declaratively
captures the overarching objective of safely transporting
all passengers across the river, while satisfying the domain constraints of guard-to-prisoner ratios on either side
of the river and boat capacity. This goal takes a single input (initialConfiguration) and produces an output (finalConfiguration)-both of which are typed as

the Knowledge component (see Section A.3), which defines the structure of a configuration, including the number
of guards and prisoners on each bank and in the boat.
The given and makes fields specify logical preconditions and postconditions for the goal. For this goal, we use
the expression safe(...) as an alias for the declarative
subgoal SafeConfiguration(...). This mapping is
formally defined in the Assertions section of the Mechanism specification, allowing a generative agent to resolve
shorthand expressions and interpret safety requirements accurately (see Section A.3 for predicate definitions).

A.2: Method Component
Importantly, the goal is operationalized via a
mechanismReference to the GPPsolution mechanism, defined in the Method component. This mechanism
encodes a complete plan as a finite-state machine (FSM),
decomposing the problem into intermediate subgoals and
guarded failure branches.
The current goal formulation generalizes across problem
setups: it is not tied to any specific initial or final configuration. Thus, the model can, in principle, solve GPP even
from an intermediate world state (e.g., after step 5 in a failed
prior plan), provided the given condition holds (i.e., the
configuration is safe). This makes the goal entry reusable in
broader contexts, including partial re-planning or interactive
explanations of incomplete solutions. In the next section, we
describe the last component of a TMK model: Knowledge.

B.1 Skill: Version Spaces
To illustrate the four-stage pipeline in action, we walk
through an example on Version Spaces which is a skill taught
in an AI class, showing how Ivy applies Algorithm 1 to process a learner’s question and produce a structured, domaingrounded answer.

Stage 2 — Knowledge Retrieval Ivy then embeds the
TMK’s Task, Method, and Knowledge entries, along with
the question, into a FAISS vector database. It determines the
verbosity level (k) based on the question’s phrasing. Here,
k=3 for a detailed but concise answer.
From the similarity search, Ivy retrieves the top-ranked
entries with most similarities to the question:
Task: GeneralizeSpecific (k=0.747): <Definition
of the goal, input/output parameters, and given/makes
constraints>.
Method: GeneralizeSpecificMechanism (k=0.739):
<FSM states and transitions for the generalization process,
including conditions to apply generalization or terminate>.
Task: SpecializeGeneral (k=0.734): <Definition of the
related goal for handling negative examples by refining the
general model>.
From above we can see it retrieves two structured goals
and one mechanism with FSM in Method component, as
this question is more of a “how” question instead of simple
“what” question. The question contains the key action “generalize” and references the “SpecificModel,” directly matching the “GeneralizeSpecific” goal and mechanism. “SpecializeGeneral” is also retrieved because the embedding space
associates generalization and specialization as complementary operations in version space. These form the structured
foundation for the response.
Stage 3 — Constrained Response Generation Using the
retrieved TMK entries as scaffolding, Ivy builds the answer
within the structural and logical constraints encoded in the
TMK. From the given conditions defined in GeneralizeSpecific and GeneralizeSpecificMechanism, the model knows
that generalization occurs when input example is positive,

the specific model exists, and it does not already include the
example.
Ivy Output: The version space should generalize the specific model under the following conditions:
1. The example is a positive example (‘example.isPositive
== true‘).
2. The specific model is not null (‘specificModel != null‘).
3. The specific model does not already include the positive
example (‘specificModel.includes(example) != true‘).
When these conditions are met, the generalization process
can effectively expand the specific model to include the new
positive example, thereby accommodating additional valid
instances that fit within the learned concept. If the specific
model already includes the example, no generalization is
necessary, and the process can conclude successfully.
Additionally, it is important to note that the version space
also involves a specialization process based on negative examples. When a negative example is encountered (‘example.isPositive != true‘), the general model should be specialized to exclude this example, ensuring that the learned
concept remains accurate and does not encompass invalid
instances.
In summary, the conditions for generalizing the specific
model are:
1. The example is positive (‘example.isPositive == true‘).
2. The specific model is not null (‘specificModel != null‘).
3. The specific model does not already include the example (‘specificModel.includes(example) != true’).
When these conditions are satisfied, the generalization
process can proceed effectively, while the specialization process can be applied to negative examples to refine the general model.
Stage 4 — Coherence Optimization The previous response correctly identified the conditions in a structured way
and provided reasoning for the necessities of the conditions.
However, the explanation was verbose and contained notable
redundancy.
In this stage, Ivy refines the generated answer for clarity
and concision, removing repetition while keeping the essential logic. The final answer reads:
The version space should generalize the specific model
under the following conditions:
1. The example is positive (‘example.isPositive == true‘).
2. The specific model is not null (‘specificModel != null‘).
3. The specific model does not already include the positive
example (‘specificModel.includes(example) != true‘).
When these conditions are met, the specific model can be
expanded to include the new positive example, allowing for
a broader representation of the learned concept. If the specific model already includes the example, no generalization
is needed.
Additionally, when encountering a negative example (‘example.isPositive != true‘), the general model should be specialized to exclude this example, ensuring the accuracy of
the learned concept.
In summary, generalization occurs when the example is
positive, the specific model exists, and it does not already
include the example.

This stage ensures the explanation is clear, logically ordered, and directly responsive to the learner’s question.
