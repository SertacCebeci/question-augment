# Counterfactual self-critique group

A single policy is asked to doubt its own answer, and the doubt is trained as part of the policy-gradient group. From a base chain-of-thought trajectory, the model writes a probe in the form of a "What if this step is wrong?" question. The appendix prompt asks it to "Identify the earliest likely incorrect step and describe" how the reasoning would change under that counterfactual.[^c1] The probe is conditioned on the base trajectory: "Each probe is conditioned on the base trajectory and prompts" the model to consider an alternative hypothesis, such as an incorrect intermediate step or a missing constraint.[^c2] Each probe then yields a new trajectory sampled from the same parameters. "Counterfactual critiques are generated by lightweight ego critics that share parameters with the base model" and introduce no extra learned component.[^c3] No second model, verifier or stored solution enters the loop.

The probes matter for training through the group. The paper's GRPO setup treats the base trajectory and its counterfactuals as one comparison set: "can be used as a lightweight verification mechanism. During training, these trajectories form a" group, each member receives a scalar reward, and the advantage is the reward minus the group mean.[^c4] The critique term is what rewards repair. In Appendix B, "Critique utility: Rcritique (S) rewards counterfactual trajectories that (i) differ from the base" when the base answer is wrong and the counterfactual answer is right.[^c5] A counterfactual is credited only when it leaves a wrong base and reaches the gold answer. On the paper's account, the base policy then absorbs the pattern: "Because all trajectories are generated by the same policy, optimization internalizes the corrective".[^c6] The phrase "internalizes" is the paper's claim. Nothing in the text measures the base policy's behaviour without counterfactual prompting in a way that separates it from the trained policy's accuracy.

The reported effects are small in absolute terms and depend on capacity. On GSM8K, the 1B Llama model starts from a 33.14% CoT baseline.[^c7] The paper's best setting, two counterfactuals, is listed at +2.70 points, and "Two egos achieve the strongest average gains".[^c8] The 0.5B Qwen model starts at 8.36% and gains +2.48 points, the largest relative effect in the paper.[^c9][^c10] The 3B Llama gains +0.18 points, described as "modest but stable,"[^c11] and Mathstral-7B gains +0.18 points, where "Performance never degrades, indicating that counterfactual".[^c12] The paper's explanation is that smaller models "produce more frequent and diverse reasoning errors and lack strong internal" verification.[^c13]

Group size is the paper's main design knob. "Adding a third ego provides smaller gains and can reduce stability,"[^c14] and the variance labels are "Nego = 3: high variance, unstable updates."[^c15] Two counterfactuals give "Nego = 2: 41.3% disagreement, indicating complementary coverage"[^c16] and the highest first-error localization, "Nego = 2: 74% success."[^c17] The mechanism is therefore a bounded-diversity bet: too little disagreement gives a weak signal, too much gives reward variance.

## Limits

- **The abstract's numbers are not in the tables.** The abstract says "CSQ improves accuracy by +6.7 to +12.4 points over standard" chain-of-thought prompting.[^c18] The tables' largest absolute lift is +2.70 points. The body gives "2–10 absolute points," which no table reproduces. (My inference: the 6.7 may be the 1B relative lift of 6.79%, not a point gain.)
- **The tables do not reconcile with themselves.** Table 1 lists 35.28 trained against a 33.14 base as +2.25; the difference is +2.14 (my arithmetic). For two egos, Table 1 gives 35.84 (+2.70) while Table 5 gives 35.58 (+2.53), under the caption "Table 5: Aggregated GSM8K results for Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct across ego configurations."
- **The baseline is defined two ways.** Section 4.2 defines the CoT baseline as "CoT Baseline: Standard chain-of-thought prompting without explicit verification, self-questioning,"[^c19] with no reinforcement learning. Section 4.1 says "baseline is measured using the same training pipeline but without ego-generated critiques, isolating".[^c20] If the table baseline is untrained, the lift includes whatever GRPO alone contributes, and no GRPO-without-critique row is reported. The share of the gain that the critique itself causes is therefore not isolated.
- **The reward needs gold answers.** The abstract stresses being "without requiring external reward models or multiple" agents.[^c21] But the reward is "derived from ground-truth correctness and ego critique quality".[^c22] The repair term uses the gold answer, so the method removes the critic model, not the labelled supervision.
- **Drift is handled heuristically.** "We approximate drift with simple heuristics (e.g.," in Appendix B.1.[^c23] The text itself concedes that "Ego-generated critiques may also drift semantically, for example by assuming nonexistent"[^c24] errors or altering constraints.
- **Variance is reported as labels.** The paper says runs use "three to four random seeds," but the text gives no standard deviations or significance tests. The "stable" claim rests on the ordinal labels "low", "moderate" and "high" variance.
- **Scope.** Evaluation is on GSM8K, MATH and Minerva-style tasks, with critiques that are "single-hop and probabilistic rather than formal or multi-step verifications, limiting".[^c25] The paper says the method "introduces additional computational overhead during training".[^c26] Its effect depends on capacity: "Because counterfactual critics share parameters with the base policy, effectiveness depends on model capacity."[^c27]
- **Group size is small.** With 2 to 4 trajectories per group, the group-mean baseline rests on very few samples. This is my inference from the design; the paper does not measure its effect on the advantage estimate.
