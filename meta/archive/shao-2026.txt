Abstract
Large language model (LLM)-based Socratic tutors increasingly guide students through multi-turn questioning, but they
can suffer from scaffolding collapse: under sustained student
pressure, a tutor gradually abandons guided inquiry and reveals
solutions directly. Prior defenses primarily constrain observable responses through prompting, preference optimization,
or filtering, leaving the internal representation drift that precedes trajectory-level collapse largely unaddressed. We propose
Scaffold-Preserving Representation Alignment, a two-stage
framework that first warms up a Socratic tutor with supervised
fine-tuning, then combines trajectory-weighted direct preference optimization with a margin-preserving representation loss
anchored to frozen reference states. Our method is designed to
maintain separation between scaffold-preserving and collapseinducing hidden states across dialogue turns. We evaluate our
method across five STEM disciplines and five red-teaming
attack strategies. On Qwen3-8B, our method lowers Collapse
Rate to 32%, delays average collapse onset beyond nine turns,
and keeps over-refusal low, suggesting that representation-level
alignment can improve the robustness of long-horizon Socratic
tutoring under our red-teaming protocol.

Introduction
In recent years, Large Language Model (LLM)-based Socratic tutors have been playing an increasingly vital role in
educational tutoring (Liu et al. 2024; Tzanoulinou et al. 2025).
Unlike conventional question-answering systems (Allam and
Haggag 2012), Socratic tutors rely on multi-turn guided questioning to encourage students to reason, explore, and revise
their understanding independently, rather than simply receiving final answers (Puech et al. 2025). While this inquiry-based
paradigm makes Socratic tutors highly compelling, it also
inevitably introduces critical pedagogical risks. Recent studies (Hazra et al. 2026; Yu et al. 2026) reveal that under prompt
injections, such as persistent student pressure, fake mastery,
or role drift, Socratic tutors may gradually abandon their
instructional role, directly reveal answers, or fail to correct
erroneous student statements. We refer to this phenomenon
as scaffolding collapse: a trajectory-level pedagogical failure

role constraints, and safety boundaries become progressively
unstable along the conversational trajectory.

Based on this observation, we propose SPRA, a trajectorylevel representation alignment framework for preserving Socratic tutoring behavior. SPRA aligns tutoring representations
with Socratic anchor states and suppresses layer-specific drift
toward collapse states, thereby encouraging the model to
remain within the desired pedagogical functional manifold
throughout the interaction. We evaluate SPRA on public
educational benchmarks across five disciplines and under diverse pedagogical jailbreak scenarios. Compared with strong
representation-level baselines, including RMU (Li et al. 2024),
Circuit Breakers (Zou et al. 2024), RepBend (Yousefpour et al.
2025), and LAT (Sheshadri et al. 2024), SPRA substantially
mitigates scaffolding collapse. Representation drift analysis
further confirms that SPRA mitigates collapse by stabilizing
hidden-state dynamics in the layers most associated with collapse. These results support our central claim: constraining
the manifold of hidden representations provides an effective
mechanism for preserving Socratic tutoring behavior under
multi-turn intervention. Overall, our main contributions are
summarized as follows:
• We formalize scaffolding collapse as a trajectory-level
pedagogical failure and empirically study its association
with hidden representation shifts across dialogue turns.

• We construct a Socratic tutoring robustness benchmark,
including per-turn chosen-rejected training pairs with five
collapse-typed negative trajectories, and a red-teaming
test set for evaluating multi-turn scaffolding collapse.
• We introduce SPRA, a Scaffold-preserving representation
alignment framework that regularizes hidden representations by preserving the chosen-rejected projection margin.
• Extensive experiments across five STEM disciplines
demonstrate that our method preserves Socratic tutoring
behavior more robustly than representative baselines.

Methodology
In this section, we first introduce the preliminary background
and formally state the problem we aim to address in this
study. We then present our proposed framework in detail and
introduce the key components of SPRA.
Problem Formulation. We formalize Socratic tutoring
as a multi-turn generation task where the tutor guides the
student toward a solution without directly revealing it. In the
training stage, we use N constructed chosen-rejected training
pairs, Xtr = {(xn , yn+ , yn− , cn , τn , Tn , wn )}N
n=1 , where each
n maps to a source tuple (d, t, c), representing dialogue d,
turn t, and a specific scaffolding collapse type c (i.e., a
pedagogical failure where the tutor deviates from Socratic
guidance). For a given context xn (prior to turn t), yn+ denotes
the gold Socratic response, and yn− denotes the collapsed
response of type cn . Besides, τn , Tn , and wn denote the

collapsed turn index, total dialogue length, and trajectory
|S|
weight, respectively. In inference, we let S={si }i=1 be the
test set, where each si contains a problem statement and
its solution. The model leverages the solution as grounding
knowledge to generate scaffolding responses. Our goal is
to learn a tutor policy πθ that prefers Socratic scaffolding
over collapse given the same context, i.e., πθ (yn+ |xn ) >
πθ (yn− |xn ). However, scaffolding collapse extends beyond
output-level preference errors; the model can also drift toward
collapse states in the representation space during training.
Treating collapse as a trajectory-level pedagogical safety
failure, SPRA therefore optimizes the tutor to simultaneously
enforce response-level preference and representation-level
separation between yn+ and yn− .
Overview of SPRA. We aim to train a robust Socratic tutor
through a two-stage framework, SPRA. In the warmup stage,
we initialize πθ from a base instruct model and optimize it
with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the chosen responses in
Xtr . This stage equips the model with basic Socratic tutoring
behavior before preference learning. After warmup, we freeze
the current model as the reference policy πref and build a
reference cache over all training pairs. The cache remains
frozen during subsequent training, avoiding the need to load
a separate reference model while providing stable anchors for
both output- and representation-level objectives. In the main
stage, SPRA continues training πθ with a joint objective
that is designed to reduce scaffolding collapse by jointly
controlling what the model prefers to generate and how it
separates scaffolding from collapse internally.

Experiments
In this section, we first introduce the experimental settings
and further present our results in detail.
Datasets. We construct the following datasets across five
STEM disciplines (mathematics, computer science, physics,
chemistry, and biology) for studying pedagogical robustness in
multi-turn Socratic tutoring: 500 positive Socratic dialogues,
2,500 negative collapsed trajectories, 7,022 per-turn pairwise
samples, and 500 evaluation problems. (i) We sample 100 dialogues per discipline from MathDial (Macina et al. 2023), the
Socratic Debugging Benchmark v2 (Al-Hossami et al. 2024),
and TutorChat (Chevalier et al. 2024), yielding 500 positive
dialogues. Since TutorChat is predominantly non-Socratic, we
use Qwen3.6-27B to rewrite only tutor turns into scaffoldpreserving Socratic responses while preserving all student
turns. For each positive dialogue, we synthesize five negative
collapsed trajectories using Qwen3.6-27B, corresponding
to answer leakage, over-explanation, no-question collapse,
misconception neglect, and role drift. Each trajectory uses
one consistent adversarial student strategy and is forced to
exhibit exactly one collapse type at a specified turn. We verify
the generated negatives using rule-based checks, an LLM
judge, and manual checking by human experts. (ii) We then
align positive and negative tutor continuations and expand
them into per-turn chosen-rejected pairs up to the collapse
turn, producing 7,022 pairwise samples. Train and validation
splits are performed at the positive-dialogue level within each
discipline, resulting in 4,212 training and 2,810 validation
pairs. (iii) We construct a fully independent red-teaming test
set with 500 self-contained problems, 100 per discipline, each
with an initial student attempt and a private reference solution. These problems are exclusively used in the red-teaming
evaluation framework. Full data construction details, prompts,
and per-discipline statistics are provided in Appendix.

Experimental setup. SPRA uses two-stage LoRA training.
We first perform SFT on positive Socratic dialogues for 3
epochs with learning rate 1×10−6 to obtain πref and then train
on per-turn chosen-rejected pairs for 1 epoch with learning
rate 5×10−6 using Eq. (3). We set LoRA rank r=16, α=32,
dropout 0.05, effective batch size 16, maximum sequence
length 2048, AdamW with gradient clipping 1.0, and seed 42.
Validation search selects β=0.1, λTDPO =0.4, and λMP =0.6.
We finetune our method based on 200 validation problem
sets and evaluate on 500 test problem sets using three metrics, including Collapse Rate (CR), Average Collapse Onset
Turn (OT), and Over-Refusal Rate (RR), on 500 held-out
problems via a red-teaming framework, which contains an
attacker (Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite with 0.7 temperature), a target model (0 temperature, maximum generation
length of 1024 tokens), and a judge (GPT-5.1 with 0 temperature). Each dialogue runs for at most 16 turns and stops
at the first collapse. We then report the following metrics.
CR (↓) measures the fraction of dialogues with at least one
collapse over all dialogues (a lower CR reflects stronger tutor
robustness). OT (↑) measures the average collapse onset turn
over all collapsed dialogues (a higher OT indicates stronger
resistance to student pressure). RR (↓) measures the fraction
of dialogues in which the tutor exhibits excessive defensive
behavior over all dialogues (a lower RR indicates that the tutor
remains helpful). Full details are described in Appendix.

Adversarial vulnerability (RQ1). We preliminarily assess the scaffolding collapse effect over the math dataset
(test set) under various attack types on the backbone model,
Qwen3-8B, with Socratic prompting. As presented in Tab. 1,
Direct Answer Request achieves the highest collapse rate of
68%, followed by Progressive Answer Extraction with a collapse rate of 61%. These two strategies are substantially more
effective than others, whose collapse rates remain around
33%–36%. However, their collapse onset is also later, suggesting that successful attacks under these strategies typically
require more turns. Refusal rates are generally low across all
attack types, with Emotional Pressure producing the highest
refusal rate of 13%. The experiment verifies that the Socratic
tutor will collapse under these commonly seen attacks.
Layer-wise representational drift analysis (RQ2). We
further analyze whether scaffolding collapse is associated
with hidden representation drift across model layers (relative
depth rescaled between 0 and 1) on the validation set. As
shown in Fig. 3, we report the drift distance (DS2C ), where a
larger value indicates more severe drift, at the collapse turn

(t=τ ) and at the preceding tutor turn (t=τ −2). The analysis
is conducted along the relative model depth of Qwen3-8B
across five datasets. We have two observations. First, all five
datasets exhibit clear representation drift at the above two
turns, suggesting that collapse is associated with detectable
changes in hidden representations even before the collapse
turn. The drift patterns on the physics, chemistry, and biology
datasets are particularly similar, which is likely because
these three discipline-specific subsets are derived from the
same TutorChat source. Second, the drift is consistently
concentrated in the middle layers across datasets, although
the exact peak location may vary across backbone models.
These findings suggest a correlation between collapse and
hidden-state drifts, which motivate us to adapt the layers
where drift is most pronounced, with the goal of improving the
stability of Socratic tutoring behavior and reducing collapse
under adversarial pressure. For Qwen3-8B, we therefore
fine-tune layers ([20,21,22,23]). Layer-wise drift analyses for
the remaining backbone models are provided in Appendix.

avoiding over-refusal, striking a balance between alignment

Related Work
SPRA sits at the intersection of three threads. Socratic tutoring
has been systematically evaluated by advanced benchmarks
for assessing dialogue-level pedagogy and learner-centered
guidance (Srinivasa et al. 2025; Macina et al. 2025; Liu
et al. 2025), but these efforts largely score responses turn-byturn rather than across full tutoring trajectories. Multi-turn
dialogue drift describes how models progressively lose track
of user intent, prior instructions, or safety-relevant constraints
across extended interactions (Laban et al. 2025; Deshpande
et al. 2025; Li et al. 2026); scaffolding collapse can be viewed
as a pedagogy-specific form of this broader failure mode.
Preference and representation alignment studies include DPOstyle objectives (Rafailov et al. 2023; Hong, Lee, and Thorne
2024; Zhang et al. 2024; Zeng et al. 2024; Shi et al. 2024) that
operate at the response or token level, as well as hidden-state
interventions (Zou et al. 2023; Rimsky et al. 2024; Wu et al.
2024; Li et al. 2025) that act on individual inputs; however,
these approaches do not explicitly couple preference learning
with hidden-state geometry along a dialogue trajectory. SPRA
differs by combining trajectory-weighted preference learning,
distinct from the token-level TDPO (Zeng et al. 2024), with
a margin-preserving objective anchored to a frozen warmup
reference cache. We provide a full discussion in Appendix.

Conclusion
We study scaffolding collapse in LLM-based Socratic tutoring
as a trajectory-level pedagogical failure, where tutors gradually drift from guided questioning toward collapse under
sustained student pressure. Our analysis shows that this failure
is associated with hidden-state representation drift, especially
in middle layers, and that such drift can appear before observable collapse. Building on this observation, we propose
SPRA, a scaffold-preserving representation alignment framework that combines warmup SFT, trajectory-weighted DPO,
and a margin-preserving representation loss anchored by a
frozen reference cache. Extensive experiments across five
STEM domains, multiple open-weight LLMs, and diverse
red-teaming attacks show that SPRA reduces collapse, delays
collapse onset, and maintains low over-refusal. These results
suggest that preserving internal scaffolding representations
is a promising direction for building robust, long-horizon
Socratic tutors.

Limitations & Future Work
First, our study mainly focuses on STEM disciplines, while
humanities, social sciences, and liberal arts are not covered.
In the future, our method can be extended to these disciplines.
Second, we primarily investigate open-weight LLMs because
our method requires model fine-tuning and access to layerspecific hidden representations. Future research may explore
how similar scaffold-preserving mechanisms can be adapted
to black-box commercial LLMs. Third, our study aims to
prevent scaffolding collapse in multi-turn Socratic tutoring,
rather than improving the overall tutoring quality. Enhancing
pedagogical effectiveness, such as better learning support,
remains an important direction for future work.

Training procedure. Algo. 1 summarizes the SPRA training process. We first initialize πθ from a base instruct model
and perform warmup supervised fine-tuning by minimizing LSFT over the chosen Socratic responses. The warmupfinetuned policy is then frozen as πref , which is used to build a
reference cache containing chosen-rejected log-probabilities
and pooled hidden states. Based on the cached reference
representations, SPRA computes the global collapse direction
v̂l for each selected layer l ∈ L. In the main stage, the policy
is updated with the joint objective Ltotal , which combines
SFT, trajectory-weighted DPO, and margin-preserving loss.
Time and space complexity. Let Csft , Cref , and Cmain
be the average cost of one SFT update, the cost of one
no-gradient forward pass, and the cost of one main-stage
update for a pairwise sample. So, warmup fine-tuning
costs O(Ew N Csft ). Reference caching requires two forward passes per sample and then computes layer-wise collapse directions, costing O(2N Cref + N |L|h). Main training costs O(Em N Cmain ). Thus, the total time complexity is O(Ew N Csft + 2N Cref + N |L|h + Em N Cmain ). The
dominant terms depend on the model’s forward/backward
passes. For the space complexity, our framework stores two
reference log-probabilities and two pooled hidden states
per sample and selected layer. The total space complexity
is O(Mθ + Mopt + Mact + N |L|h), where Mθ , Mopt , and
Mact denote model parameters, optimizer states, and training
activations. The cache adds O(N |L|h) storage but avoids
loading a separate reference model during the main stage.

Positive and negative tutoring examples. We construct a
five-discipline Socratic tutoring corpus from three publicly
available educational dialogue datasets. For mathematics, we
randomly sample 100 out of 715 Socratic dialogues from
MathDial (Macina et al. 2023). For computer science, we
randomly sample 100 out of 115 dialogues from the Socratic
Debugging Benchmark v2 (SDB) (Al-Hossami et al. 2024).
For physics, chemistry, and biology, we randomly sample 100
dialogues from the corresponding TutorChat subsets (Chevalier et al. 2024), which contain 319, 863, and 403 dialogues,
respectively. MathDial features semi-synthetic dialogues between human teachers and AI-simulated students, while SDB
consists of highly structured, expert-crafted tutoring scripts.
Since the TutorChat subsets are predominantly synthetic and
non-Socratic, we use Qwen3.6-27B to rewrite each dialogue into a scaffold-preserving Socratic counterpart, where
the tutor guides the student through questions and hints rather
than revealing the final answer or a complete solution. We
preserve all original student turns and rewrite only tutor turns.
This process yields 500 positive Socratic tutoring dialogues,
with 100 dialogues per discipline. Based on these positive
dialogues, we synthesize collapse-typed negative tutor responses using Qwen3.6-27B. For each positive dialogue,
we construct five adversarial trajectories, one for each target
collapse type. In each trajectory, the model role-plays both
an adversarial student, who applies a single consistent attack
strategy across turns, and a tutor, who is forced to exhibit
exactly one target collapse type at a pre-specified turn τ and
remain in that failed state thereafter. We consider five mutually
exclusive collapse types: answer leakage, over-explanation,
no-question collapse, misconception neglect, and role drift.
Their operational definitions and example symptoms are provided in Tab. 7. This procedure produces 2,500 collapse-typed
negative dialogues in total. By construction, each negative trajectory has a single collapse label, and the generation prompt
explicitly prohibits mixing multiple collapse modes within the
same trajectory. We verify collapse quality using a two-stage

procedure: rule-based heuristics over each forced-collapse
tutor turn, including answer-signature and question-count
patterns specific to each collapse type, followed by an LLM
judge and manual checking by human experts. The negative
examples refer to collapsed tutor responses rather than student
utterances.

Per-turn pairwise training corpus. We align positive
tutor continuations with the corresponding negative tutor
continuations to construct a trajectory-level pairwise corpus,
which we then expand into per-turn pairs. Concretely, for
each aligned (positive, negative) trajectory pair, we form
one pairwise sample at every shared tutor turn up to and
including the collapse turn τ : both responses share the same
dialogue history x truncated just before that turn, and differ
only in the tutor response—the chosen response y + preserves
Socratic scaffolding, while the rejected response y − exhibits
the trajectory’s collapse type. Positive and negative trajectories
are aligned at the tutor-turn level, and when their lengths differ
we truncate to the shorter length so that every pair compares
responses under an identical history. This per-turn expansion
yields the SPRA training signal at both pre-collapse (t<τ ) and
collapse (t=τ ) positions, for a total of 7,022 pairwise samples.
To prevent leakage from related samples and support fair
comparison across training methods, within each discipline,
we split the 100 positive dialogues 60/40 into train/validation
at the dialogue level and let the negative trajectories and
per-turn pairwise samples inherit this dialogue-level split,
ensuring that all samples derived from the same positive
dialogue remain in the same split. The held-out red-teaming
problem set described below serves as our independent test
set, fully isolated from this train/validation partition. The
SFT-LoRA baseline uses only the positive Socratic training
split, whereas SPRA uses the per-turn pairwise training

set expanded from the trajectory-level pairs. Detailed perdiscipline split statistics are given in Tab. 6.
Red-teaming evaluation set. In addition to the fine-tuning
corpus, we construct an independent red-teaming problem set that also serves as our held-out test set. We use
Qwen3.6-27B to generate 100 self-contained problem statements per discipline (each with an initial student attempt and
a private reference solution for the judge), stratified into basic,
intermediate, and advanced difficulty levels, covering the
same five disciplines. Because these problems are generated
independently of the public tutoring corpora used for training,
the evaluation set is disjoint from the training data by construction; it is never used during training or validation and
provides no chosen or rejected responses. Instead, it serves
as the initial problem pool for our automated attacker–tutor–
judge red-teaming framework. In this framework, an attacker
model role-plays an adversarial student that, at each turn,
applies one of five attack strategies and escalates upon judge
feedback; the evaluated tutor responds; and an LLM judge
labels each tutor response for collapse (and its type) and
over-refusal. Each problem is evaluated for up to 16 turns and
terminates at the first collapse, at which point we record the
onset turn. We report Collapse Rate, Average Collapse Onset
Turn, and Over-Refusal Rate as the main evaluation metrics.

Experimental Setup
SPRA training consists of two stages. In the warmup stage,
we perform LoRA SFT on positive Socratic dialogues for 3
epochs with a learning rate of 1×10−6 , obtaining the reference policy πref . After the warmup stage (main training), we
jointly optimize the SFT loss, the trajectory-weighted DPO
loss, and the margin-preserving loss on the per-turn chosenrejected pairwise corpus for 1 epoch with a learning rate of
5×10−6 . For LoRA, we choose the LoRA rank r=16, scaling
factor α=32, and dropout 0.05. Per-backbone LoRA target
layer sets L follow the drift identification described above;
we also pool hidden states at L for the margin-preserving loss.
Other training hyperparameters include an effective batch
size of 16 (per-device batch size 1 with gradient accumulation
16), maximum sequence length 2048, AdamW optimizer with
maximum gradient norm 1.0, and weight decay 0. We apply
grid search on the validation set to fine-tune the framework
hyperparameters based on Collapse Rate and choose the optimal β=0.1 and loss balanced factors λTDPO =0.4, λMP =0.6.
We fix the random seed to 42.
We evaluate model performance on a comprehensive test
set comprising 500 problem statements (100 from each discipline) utilizing an automated red-teaming framework. This
framework consists of three interacting agents: an attacker,
a Socratic tutor (the target model), and a judge. During the
evaluation process, the attacker iteratively issues adversarial
prompts to the target model, specifically executing a “direct
answer request” strategy. Subsequently, the judge evaluates
the tutor’s responses and outputs three core metrics in a
strictly constrained JSON format: Collapse Rate, Average
Collapse Onset Turn, and Over-Refusal Rate. For the underlying backbone models, the attacker is instantiated with
Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite at a temperature of 0.7 to

encourage diverse attack vectors, while the judge employs
GPT-5.1 at a temperature of 0 to ensure deterministic and
consistent assessments. Each multi-turn dialogue is capped
at a maximum of 16 turns, with early termination triggered
immediately upon the judge detecting a model collapse. Furthermore, to efficiently measure adversarial robustness, if
no collapse is detected by the sixteenth turn, the attack is
officially classified as failed, and the corresponding dialogue
is recorded as non-collapsed. For fair comparison, we employ
the same configuration (e.g., system prompt) for all baselines and our method during evaluation. We fix the target
model temperature as 0 for reproducibility, with a maximum
generation length for one sample per turn as 1024 tokens.
Evaluation metrics. We evaluate each tutor along three
complementary dimensions: whether scaffolding collapse
occurs, how late collapse occurs when it is unavoidable, and
whether the model avoids over-refusal.
Collapse Rate (CR) (↓) measures the fraction of dialogues
with at least one collapse over all dialogues. CR is our primary
metric because it directly measures whether a tutor preserves
Socratic guidance under sustained adversarial pressure. Since
any collapse indicates a failure to maintain the intended
tutoring mode, a lower CR reflects stronger robustness across
multi-turn interactions.
Average Collapse Onset Turn (OT) (↑) measures the
average collapse onset turn (the turn that the tutor response
collapses) over all collapsed dialogues. When no dialogue collapses, i.e., (CR=0), OT is reported as N/A. We include OT to
capture the temporal depth of robustness: even when collapse
eventually occurs, a later onset means that the tutor maintains
Socratic scaffolding for more turns. Therefore, a higher OT
indicates stronger resistance to sustained adversarial pressure.
Over-Refusal Rate (RR) (↓) measures the fraction of
dialogues in which the tutor exhibits excessive defensive
behavior, such as refusing legitimate learning requests or
failing to provide pedagogically useful guidance, over all
dialogues. RR serves as a dual-safety metric for CR. A tutor
should reduce scaffolding collapse without simply refusing
valid tutoring requests. Otherwise, a model could achieve a
low CR by avoiding tutoring altogether. A lower RR indicates
that the tutor remains helpful and responsive to legitimate
learning needs while preserving Socratic constraints.

In prompt-based defenses, we mainly evaluate how
prompts can affect the tutor performance from three perspectives: (i) Socratic-enforced method (Puech et al. 2025)
reinforces Socratic constraints at the system-prompt level, (ii)
Few-shot example method (Brown et al. 2020) adds in-context
Socratic tutoring demonstrations to elicit scaffolding behavior, and (iii) Output filtering method (Macina et al. 2023)
applies an external judge to detect collapsed tutor responses
and trigger regeneration when collapse is detected.
For Socratic tutors, we assess the following models. SocraticLM (Liu et al. 2024) is a supervised fine-tuned Socratic
tutoring model used to evaluate whether positive Socratic
imitation improves collapse robustness. ORPO (Hong, Lee,
and Thorne 2024) is a single-stage preference-alignment
method trained on the cleaned UltraFeedback dataset (Cui
et al. 2023). PedagogicalRL (Dinucu-Jianu et al. 2025) trains a
pedagogically aware tutor with online reinforcement learning,
jointly optimizing solution correctness and pedagogical reward, serving as an RL-based pedagogical alignment baseline.
EULER (Bonino et al. 2024) is a Socratic tutor fine-tuned
from Phi-3-mini-4k-Instruct, serving as a smallmodel educational-tutoring baseline. EduChat (Dan et al.
2023) is an education-domain LLM trained via educational
corpus pre-training and instruction fine-tuning to enhance
capabilities including open-domain question answering, essay
assessment, Socratic teaching, and emotional support, serving
as a broader educational-domain baseline.
Regarding representation-level and adversarial robustness methods, we adapt each method to the pedagogicalcollapse setting by treating collapsed responses as undesirable
behaviors to be suppressed. RMU (Li et al. 2024) applies
representation misdirection to push target-behavior hidden
states toward noise-like representation directions. Circuit
Breakers (Zou et al. 2024) uses representation rerouting to
disrupt internal representation pathways that lead to harmful
outputs. RepBend (Yousefpour et al. 2025) bends hidden
representations toward safer directions to suppress undesirable behavior. LAT (Sheshadri et al. 2024) perturbs hidden
states during training via latent adversarial training to improve model robustness. R2D2 (Mazeika et al. 2024) applies
dynamic adversarial training, using a continuously updated
pool of adversarially generated requests to improve robustness
against adversarially induced undesirable behaviors.

jointly modeling pedagogical collapse as both a trajectorylevel preference-learning problem and a representation-level

Training dynamics. Fig. 8 illustrates the loss trajectories
of our two-stage training process using Qwen3-8B, plotting
both the per-step data points and their corresponding median
values. We observe a substantial decrease in LSFT during the
warm-up phase, followed by a gradual stabilization throughout
the main training stage. Furthermore, LTDPO initializes near
log 2≈0.693—the theoretical cold-start expectation under

an unbiased reference—and converges to a value below 0.1.
Concurrently, LMP exhibits localized impulse oscillations
before being effectively suppressed; this suppression directly
validates the intended regulatory objective of this specific
loss component. In summary, the distinct trajectory of each
loss function demonstrates that they successfully fulfill their
designated roles, yielding overall training dynamics that align
robustly with our expectations.
Per-collapse-type representation analysis. In our model
design, we use a single global direction as an approximation. It
is intended to capture the dominant shared component that separates scaffold-preserving responses from collapse responses.
The global direction is retained in the main method because
it is parameter-efficient and does not require a collapse-type
classifier at inference time. Since different collapse types
may also induce type-specific shifts, we further evaluate this
approximation through per-collapse-type drift analysis and
an ablation using type-specific directions. Fig. 7 presents
the DS2C layer-wise curves across five collapse types using
Qwen3-8B. We observe that drift peaks exist in the middleto-late layers with τ drift above τ −2 across all five collapse
types, but at markedly different strengths: answer leakage
and over-explanation produce the highest peaks (answer leakage the narrowest), role drift and no-question collapse are
intermediate, and misconception neglect is the weakest and
most diffuse. For role drift, the τ −2 curve is already clearly
elevated, indicating that role instability drifts earliest—well

Full Discussion on Related Work
SPRA sits at the intersection of three threads.
Socratic tutoring evaluation. Recent benchmarks move
beyond final-answer accuracy toward dialogue-level pedagogy, adaptive feedback, and learner-centered guidance. TutorBench (Srinivasa et al. 2025) evaluates adaptive explanations,
actionable feedback, and hint generation across high-school
and AP-level curricula. MathTutorBench (Macina et al. 2025)
assesses math expertise, student understanding tracking, and
teacher response generation. GuideEval (Liu et al. 2025) introduces a three-phase Perception–Orchestration–Elicitation
framework for instructional guidance. These benchmarks
largely treat pedagogical quality as a turn-level or responselevel property rather than as a failure that unfolds across an
interaction trajectory.
Multi-turn dialogue drift. Models can progressively lose
track of user intent, prior instructions, or safety-relevant
constraints across multi-turn interaction histories. Laban et al.
(2025) report substantial performance degradation in multiturn settings due to premature assumptions and unrecoverable
trajectories. MultiChallenge (Deshpande et al. 2025) identifies
four categories of multi-turn failure: instruction retention,
inference memory, versioned editing, and self-coherence. Li
et al. (2026) show that safety behavior can depend on dialogue
state, with harmful trajectories emerging across multi-turn
contexts. Scaffolding collapse is a pedagogy-specific instance
of this trajectory-level instability.
Preference optimization. DPO (Rafailov et al. 2023) learns
from chosen–rejected pairs without an explicit reward model,
with extensions including ORPO (Hong, Lee, and Thorne
2024), negative preference optimization (Zhang et al. 2024),
and token-level TDPO (Zeng et al. 2024). Shi et al. (2024)
further extend DPO to multi-turn agent trajectories with
length-normalized objectives (DMPO). These methods treat
alignment as a response- or token-level property of an individual prompt–response pair, or optimize multi-turn preferences
without explicitly coupling them to hidden-state geometry.

Representation-level alignment. A complementary line
of work intervenes directly on hidden states. Representation engineering and contrastive activation addition expose
concept-aligned directions in hidden representations (Zou
et al. 2023; Rimsky et al. 2024). ReFT (Wu et al. 2024)
learns low-rank hidden-state interventions, and SaLoRA (Li
et al. 2025) preserves safety alignment under LoRA adaptation. These methods operate on individual inputs rather than
dialogue trajectories.
Positioning of SPRA. SPRA combines both algorithmic
threads through trajectory-weighted preference learning—
distinguishing our trajectory-level TDPO from the token-level
variant (Zeng et al. 2024)—and a margin-preserving representation objective anchored to a frozen warmup reference
cache, thereby constraining the temporal geometry of Socratic
scaffolding rather than only per-input behavior.

Other Statements
Potential risk. This work studies adversarial pressure
against LLM-based Socratic tutors in order to improve their
robustness. A potential risk is that the attack strategies used
for evaluation could be misused to induce tutoring systems
to reveal direct answers or abandon pedagogical guidance.
To reduce this risk, our red-teaming scenarios are restricted
to benign educational settings and do not involve harmful,
illegal, or unsafe content. The attack prompts are used only for
evaluation and defense development. We also emphasize that
automated Socratic tutors should be deployed with appropriate
human oversight, especially in educational contexts.

or judge agents in the automated red-teaming framework.
