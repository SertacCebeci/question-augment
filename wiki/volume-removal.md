# Volume removal

> Score a question only by how little you can predict its answer, and the
> question the answerer *cannot answer* becomes a global optimum.

## The criterion

Volume removal selects the query whose answer is expected to remove the most[^s1][^s2]
unnormalised posterior mass — introduced for active preference-based reward
learning by Sadigh et al. (2017) and stated by Bıyık et al. (2019, Eq. 1) as

$$Q^*_n = \arg\max_{Q_n} \ \mathbb{E}_{q_n}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\big[1 - P(q_n \mid Q_n, \omega)\big]$$

for a $K$-way comparison query $Q_n$, an answer $q_n \in Q_n$, and a target
parameter $\omega$. In question terms: **prefer the question whose answer you
can least predict.**

## The degeneracy (Bıyık et al. 2019, Theorem 1)

The *trivial query* — $K$ identical options — is a **global** solution.
Because $\sum_{q} P(q \mid Q,\omega) = 1$, the objective is bounded by
$1 - 1/K$, and identical options attain the bound while telling the asker
nothing about $\omega$. Their Example 1 sharpens it: a query on which the
answerer is exactly indifferent for *every* $\omega$ and a query the answerer
answers almost surely (differently in each region of $\omega$) are both optima
of the objective, and only the second identifies $\omega$. The criterion
cannot see the difference, because it reads the asker's uncertainty about the
answer without asking **where that uncertainty comes from** — the world, or
the answerer's indifference. The authors attribute the method's practical
success to the non-convexity of the optimisation: solvers converge to other
local optima, not to the trivial one.

## What mutual information does instead

Bıyık et al. (2019, Eq. 5) rewrite the information-gain objective as

$$I(\omega; q_n \mid Q_n) = H(q_n \mid Q_n) - \mathbb{E}_{\omega} H(q_n \mid \omega, Q_n)$$

— the asker's uncertainty about the answer **minus the answerer's own
uncertainty while answering**. The degenerate query raises both terms
equally, so it is no longer optimal. No easiness penalty is added: the
answerer's response model inside the likelihood does the work, which is why
[[expected-information-gain]] and a difficulty term are not two things here.

## What was measured (Bıyık et al. 2019, §7)

Four simulated domains (linear system, Driver, Tosser, Fetch): information
gain reaches higher alignment with the true reward per query than volume
removal in all four. User study: 15 participants on the simulations and 12 on
a Fetch robot, 30 queries each — 15 generated by each method, order
randomised, method hidden. Participants rated information-gain queries easier
on a 7-point scale in every environment (two-sample $t$-test, $p < 0.005$),
volume removal produced significantly more indistinguishable option pairs
(Driver $p < 0.05$, Tosser $p < 0.005$), and participants preferred the
behaviour optimised under the information-gain-learned reward (one-sample
$t$-test, $p < 0.05$).

## Transfer

| | |
|---|---|
| **In its home field** | a robot learning a linear reward $\omega$ from $K$-way trajectory comparisons, with a softmax ("noisily rational") model of the human's choice and a sampled posterior over $\omega$ |
| **Here** | any asker ranking candidate questions for a human answerer: the query is the question, the answer is the answerer's choice among the options it offers, $\omega$ is what the asker is trying to pin down |
| **What survives** | the failure structure — a criterion built on the asker's uncertainty about the answer alone is maximised by questions whose answer is unpredictable *because the answerer is indifferent* — and the repair: no separate difficulty penalty is needed once a model of the answerer's response sits in the likelihood |
| **What does not** | the proof, which needs a finite option set, fixed $K$, and $\sum_q P(q\mid Q,\omega)=1$. Free-text answers have no such normalised option set, and without a response model for the answerer the subtracted term $\mathbb{E}_\omega H(q\mid\omega,Q)$ cannot be computed — so the repair is unavailable exactly where the answer space is open |

## Limits

- Theorem 1 is a statement about the objective in their Eq. 1 over $K$-way
  comparison queries from a finite candidate set. It does not say the method
  fails in deployment — the authors report that it does not, and explain why.
- The repair inherits a different exposure: the subtracted term is computed
  from an assumed choice model (softmax, with a minimum-perceivable-difference
  parameter $\delta$), and Bıyık et al. name reliance on that cognitive model
  of the human as their own main limitation. A mis-specified answerer model
  mis-prices answerability in the same objective that is supposed to fix it.
- The user study is small (15 and 12 participants) and within-subject, on
  robot trajectory comparisons; "easier" is a Likert judgement, and the
  wrong-answer ratios in the paper come from *simulated* users, not from the
  participants.
- `sadigh-2017` is cited for the criterion's origin only; the formulation,
  the theorem and every number here are read from Bıyık et al. (2019).
- Both criteria are defined over a modelled $\omega$: a question about an
  axis outside $\omega$ scores zero under either
  ([[expected-information-gain]]).
