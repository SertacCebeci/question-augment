Hallucination in Large Language Models and RetrievalAugmented Generation: Mechanisms, Mitigation, and
Evaluation
Haopeng Yang
College of Computer and Information Science, School of Software, Southwest University,
Chongqing, China
wy810577@email.swu.edu.cn
Abstract. Large language models have demonstrated strong generative capability in question
answering, dialogue, and other knowledge-intensive tasks. However, their outputs remain
vulnerable to hallucination, including factual errors, unsupported claims, spurious citations,
and distorted reasoning. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has been proposed as a
practical remedy because it supplements parametric knowledge with external evidence
retrieved at inference time. Yet RAG does not guarantee truthfulness or attribution by
default. Errors may arise during query formulation, document retrieval, evidence
aggregation, and answer grounding. This paper reviews the relationship between
hallucination and RAG from three perspectives: mechanism, mitigation, and evaluation.
First, the major causes of hallucination in both vanilla LLMs and RAG-enhanced systems
are analyzed. Second, the principal mitigation strategies are organized into retrieval
optimization, evidence-grounded generation, and post-generation verification. Third, the
main evaluation dimensions are examined, including factuality, faithfulness, attribution
quality, and retrieval relevance. It is argued that RAG should not be treated as a complete
solution to hallucination. Its value lies in enabling externally grounded generation, but its
effectiveness depends on the reliability of retrieval, the fidelity of evidence use, and the
rigor of evaluation. Future work should prioritize attribution-aware generation, conflictsensitive reasoning, and unified evaluation protocols for trustworthy LLM systems.
Keywords: LLM, RAG, Hallucination

1. Introduction
Large language models have reshaped natural language processing by showing strong performance
across open-domain question answering, dialogue, summarization, and tool-assisted reasoning.
Nevertheless, this performance has been accompanied by a persistent failure mode: hallucination. In
this context, hallucination refers to generated content that is fluent and plausible in form but
unsupported, incorrect, or fabricated in substance. The problem is not restricted to isolated factual
mistakes. It also includes missing evidence, non-faithful synthesis, incorrect source attribution, and
reasoning trajectories that appear coherent while remaining unsound [1, 2]. The significance of

hallucination has increased as LLMs have been deployed in knowledge-intensive settings. In highstakes domains such as medicine, law, finance, and scientific assistance, unsupported outputs are not

cause is often described as overreliance on parametric memory. Since model knowledge is
compressed into parameters during pretraining, it may be incomplete, outdated, or poorly calibrated
for long-tail facts [3]. As a result, the model may answer confidently even when adequate support is
absent.
Retrieval-augmented generation has therefore been introduced as an important architectural
response. Instead of relying only on internal parameters, RAG retrieves external passages or
documents at inference time and conditions generation on those materials [4]. The core intuition is
direct: if the model is supplied with relevant evidence, unsupported fabrication should be reduced.
This idea has motivated work on retrieval design, evidence fusion, self-reflection, and grounded
answer generation [5-8]. However, the effectiveness of RAG has often been overstated. Although
retrieval may reduce hallucination caused by missing knowledge, it also introduces new failure
points. Query formulation may drift from user intent, retrieval may surface irrelevant or incomplete
evidence, and generation may ignore or distort retrieved passages. Moreover, answers may be
accompanied by citations that do not actually support the stated claims [9, 10]. Thus, the central
question is not whether RAG helps in principle, but under what conditions it produces grounded,
faithful, and attributable outputs. The present draft is revised from the uploaded project report and
reorganized around an explicit pipeline logic. To make this logic explicit, the Retrieval-Augmented
Generation function can be articulated as an equation: y = G(x, ε(x)) where x denotes the user
query, ε(x) denotes the evidence retrieved for x , and G() denotes the generator. In a non-RAG
setting, ε(x) is absent and generation relies primarily on parametric memory. In a RAG setting,
truthfulness depends not only on the model G() , but also on the quality of ε(x) and the
faithfulness with which it is used. Accordingly, this paper is organized around three dimensions:
mechanisms of hallucination, RAG-based mitigation strategies, and evaluation criteria for grounded
generation. Aims to establish a multi-dimensional evaluation framework encompassing authenticity,
fidelity, attribution quality, and retrieval relevance, thereby providing a research framework and
future directions for building more credible and reliable generative AI systems.
2. Mechanisms of hallucination in llms and RAG systems
2.1. Hallucination in parametric generation
Hallucination in standard LLMs is rooted in the mismatch between probabilistic next-token
prediction and factual correctness. The model is optimized to continue text plausibly, not to verify
claims against an external world model. Consequently, when internal knowledge is weak,
approximate, or stale, fluent error may be generated rather than explicit uncertainty [1, 2]. This can
be expressed by the standard decoding objective. The process is shown in Equation (1).

2.2. Pipeline-level hallucination in RAG
Within RAG systems, the problem is redistributed across a multi-stage pipeline rather than removed.
Four failure sources are especially relevant. First, pre-retrieval distortion may occur during query
construction. User intent can be misread, conversation history can be compressed inaccurately, and
reformulated queries may emphasize the wrong entities or constraints. The retrieval stage is then
misdirected from the beginning. Second, retrieval-side hallucination arises when relevant evidence
is not retrieved or noisy evidence is retrieved instead. Sparse retrieval may miss semantically related
passages, whereas dense retrieval may return passages that are semantically similar but factually
inappropriate. Chunking granularity also affects support quality [5, 11]. Third, evidence-integration
failure occurs when retrieved material is not used faithfully. Evidence may be ignored, partially
used, over-generalized, or merged with parametric memory in a way that changes meaning. In multidocument settings, conflicts between sources may be resolved incorrectly or not resolved at all [7–
9]. Fourth, attribution hallucination occurs after generation. The answer may include citations or
snippets that only partially support the claims, or fail to support them entirely. Citation presence
therefore should not be equated with evidential sufficiency [10, 12]. To formalize this pipeline, let
the retriever be denoted by R and the topk evidence set by Equation (2):

3. RAG-based mitigation strategies
3.1. Multistage strategy to improve recall and precision
The first mitigation category is retrieval optimization. Early work established the importance of
dense retrieval for open-domain question answering [11]. Subsequent systems adopted hybrid
retrieval, query expansion, and reranking to improve evidence recall and precision. The objective
has been to increase the probability that generation is conditioned on relevant passages rather than
distractors. More recent work has explored iterative retrieval, where generation and retrieval interact
over multiple rounds instead of a single retrieve-then-generate pass [7, 8]. The useful abstraction is
the retrieval utility. The process is shown in Equation (4):

recall alone is insufficient if noisy passages dominate the context window.

The practical implication is straightforward. Retrieval optimization without grounded decoding

supported outputs. Effective mitigation is therefore pipeline-level and compositional.
4. Evaluation of hallucination mitigation
Evaluation remains one of the weakest components of current RAG research. Traditional metrics
such as exact match, F1, or answer accuracy are insufficient. A response may match a gold answer
while relying on irrelevant evidence; conversely, a partially correct answer may be more trustworthy
if it is transparently grounded. The first major dimension is factuality, namely whether the generated
claims are true. Recent work such as FactScore, evaluates long-form outputs at the level of atomic
statements [15]. The second dimension is faithfulness, namely whether the answer is supported by
the provided evidence. An answer may be factually correct in the world yet still not be grounded in
the retrieved passages [9, 16]. The third dimension is attribution quality, which includes citation
precision, citation coverage, and evidence sufficiency [10]. The fourth dimension is retrieval
quality , since evidence cannot support generation if it was never retrieved [5].

5. Conclusion
Hallucination should be regarded as a structural problem of large language models rather than an
incidental defect. Retrieval-augmented generation introduces an important corrective by making
external evidence available at inference time. However, RAG does not remove hallucination by
default. Instead, the locus of failure shifts from internal memory alone to the full generation
pipeline, including query formation, retrieval, evidence integration, and attribution.
Based on the foregoing, the following conclusions can be drawn. First, mitigation should be
treated as a pipeline problem rather than a retrieval problem alone. Second, grounded generation
requires both relevant evidence and faithful use of that evidence. Third, evaluation should move
beyond answer accuracy toward factuality, faithfulness, attribution, and retrieval relevance. RAG
remains one of the most promising directions for reducing hallucination, but its success depends on
whether trustworthy evidence can be retrieved, preserved, and audited throughout generation. Future
work should therefore focus on attribution-aware architectures, conflict-sensitive evidence
reasoning, and evaluation protocols that measure support rather than surface plausibility.
