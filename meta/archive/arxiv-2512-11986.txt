User prompts to large language models (LLMs) are often ambiguous or under-specified, and subtle contextual cues
shaped by user intentions, prior knowledge, and risk factors strongly influence what constitutes an appropriate
response. Misinterpreting intent or risks may lead to unsafe outputs, while overly cautious interpretations can
cause unnecessary refusal of benign requests. In this paper, we question the conventional framework in which
LLMs generate immediate responses to requests without considering broader contextual factors. User requests are
situated within broader contexts such as intentions, knowledge, and prior experience, which strongly influence
what constitutes an appropriate answer. We propose a framework that extracts and leverages such contextual
information from the user prompt itself. Specifically, a reinforcement learning based context generator, designed
in an autoencoder–like fashion, is trained to infer contextual signals grounded in the prompt and use them to
guide response generation. This approach is particularly important for safety tasks, where ambiguous requests
may bypass safeguards while benign but confusing requests can trigger unnecessary refusals. Experiments show
that our method reduces harmful responses by an average of 5.6% on the SafetyInstruct dataset across multiple
foundation models and improves the harmonic mean of attack success rate and compliance on benign prompts by
6.2% on XSTest and WildJailbreak. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of context extraction for safer and
more reliable LLM inferences.

Introduction

When interacting with large language models (LLMs), users often provide prompts that might be ambiguous, underspecified, or shaped by idiosyncratic factors such as intentions, prior knowledge, and past experiences (Ouyang et al.,
2022; Yin et al., 2023; Cole et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024). These contextual factors strongly shape what constitutes an
appropriate response, and are especially critical for safety-related settings, where subtle context cues might determine
whether a model should comply or refuse a user request. Failure to interpret intent or assess potential risks may result in
unsafe outputs, while overly cautious misreading can lead to unnecessary refusals of benign requests (Bai et al., 2022;
Röttger et al., 2023; Haider et al., 2024).
To mitigate these risks, recent works has introduced reasoning models (Jaech et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Team et al.,
2025) that integrates explicit reasoning tokens, encouraging models to deliberate before producing an answer in safety
related tasks. However, recent works show that prompt-based reasoning alone often fails to reliably prevent harmful
outputs (Jiang et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025). While reasoning supervision (Guan et al., 2024; Jiang
et al., 2025) and reinforcement learning with safety data (Kim et al., 2025) offer partial improvements, these methods
remain limited when addressing ambiguous or adversarial prompts. Because in practice, model reasoning traces often
collapse into post-hoc justifications of predetermined answers rather than genuine deliberation about potential risks
particularly in safety task (Korbak et al., 2025; Mazeika et al., 2025). As a result, current reasoning-based methods
frequently blur the line between reasoning and answer generation, undermining their intended role in safety tasks.
In this paper, we aim to disentangle reasoning and answer generation by training C ONTEXT L ENS1 , i.e. a lightweight
LLM that produces additional context snippet for a user prompt: context-aware inference. This context snippet can then
be supplied to any foundation model to support safe and accurate final answer (see Figure 1). Specifically, our approach
introduces two training innovations. First, we train the context generator (e.g. Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct) to generate
context that benefits not only itself but also an external, fixed decoder model (e.g. Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct). This
promotes transferability of the generated context across models (e.g. Llama-3-8B-Instruct or GPT-4o) and prevents
co-adaptation between context snippet and response. Second, to ensure that the context snippet remains grounded in the
user prompt rather than collapse into full response generation, we incorporate an auto-encoder loss (Kingma & Welling,
2013), where the external decoder model is tasked with inferring the original prompt from the generated context snippet
and perturbed prompt and generate responses according to predicted prompt (see Figure 2). These reconstruction

Our experiments demonstrate that context-aware inference improves performance across multiple safety benchmarks
and base models, including open-weight models and closed models. Our method achieves an average gain of 5.6%

Wildjailbreak dataset (see Table 2). Furthermore, the RL-trained context generator achieves better improvements, with
a 4.21% gain on GPT-4o and a 10.55% gain on Llama-3-8B-Instruct compared to the base models, while using a
smaller 3B model and producing higher quality context snippets (see Figure 4, 5).

Related Works

Early approaches to improve LLM safety largely framed the problem as a variant of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or
reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) on curated safety datasets (Haider et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025).
While effective at suppressing known unsafe behaviors, these methods often collapse diverse responses into a single
“refuse” action, leading to high over-refusal rates on benign queries (Cui et al., 2024; Röttger et al., 2023) and limited
robustness under distribution shifts, or jailbreak-style attacks (Zou et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a).
To strengthen deliberation, prior work has explored auxiliary reasoning steps that encourage models to think before
responding. Simple prompting strategies, such as chain-of-thought (Wei et al., 2022) or deliberative prompting, guide
models to produce intermediate steps without additional training (Guan et al., 2024). However, such prompting alone
has shown limited gains for safety (Jiang et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2024). Supervised approaches extend this idea by
collecting reasoning traces and training models to follow them (Guan et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025). Reinforcement
learning methods (Kim et al., 2025) further optimize reasoning traces using safety-aware reward signals applied to
responses, steering models toward safer intermediate reasoning. More recently, test-time inference methods refine
or re-sample reasoning trajectories dynamically to avoid unsafe generations under adversarial conditions (Zaremba
et al., 2025). Decoding-level alignment techniques additionally monitor or correct harmful continuations on-the-fly,
improving robustness without altering base model parameters (Xu et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2025).
While these techniques improve performance on safety benchmarks, they are computationally costly, remain vulnerable
to ambiguous or adversarial inputs, and tend to optimize reasoning traces primarily for producing correct answers rather
than explicitly surfacing intent or risk factors. As a result, reasoning often collapses into post-hoc justification or a
“means to an end” (Korbak et al., 2025; Mazeika et al., 2025). Recent prompt-driven safeguarding shows that LLMs
internally distinguish harmful intent but safety prompts often push representations toward over-refusal (Zheng et al.,
2024), motivating explicit mechanisms to translate latent risk signals into calibrated behaviors. On the other hand,
modular safeguarding approaches employ external guard models (Inan et al., 2023) or self-introspective classifiers (Wang
et al., 2024b) to gate responses, but such mechanisms largely focus on refusal decisions rather than guiding models
toward safe but helpful alternatives.
In contrast, our work introduces a model-agnostic context generator that operates independently of response generation.
The context distills user intent and potential risks into concise snippets. Unlike refusal-oriented safeguards, our approach
explicitly externalizes latent risk understanding into context, enabling calibrated and interpretable safety control. They
can be incorporated into any LLM at inference time as structured safety signals. This design is compatible with
blackbox state-of-the-art models such as GPT (OpenAI, 2024) and Claude (Anthropic, 2024), reducing unsafe outputs
and over-refusals without requiring additional fine-tuning or changes to the underlying model parameters.

3.2

Context-aware Inference

In non-reasoning models, LLMs generate responses directly based on the given prompts. This approach is efficient,
requiring fewer tokens and minimal computation, but it relies solely on the information contained in the prompt
(Figure 1 (left)). As a result, it has limited capacity to interpret ambiguous requests or anticipate hidden risks. Recent
reasoning-based models address some of these limitations by generating intermediate rationales before producing the
final output (Figure 1 (middle)). The model’s output consists of both a reasoning part (<think>) and a final response
(<response>). Those rationales can implicitly capture aspects of a user intent and task-specific constraints, which have
inspired the use of reasoning-based training for safety tasks. We propose context-aware inference, that disentangles
response model and context generator, e.g., C ONTEXT L ENS (Figure 1 (right)). We provide the output of the context
generator to the desired final inference model. The context (c) is appended to the original prompt (p) to form an enriched
input. This modular approach is similar to the reasoning model, with the additional flexibility that a) context generators
could be trained independently with their own objectives; b) could avoid co-adaptation and ensure transferability of the
context across models.
3.3

Context Generator

A context generator (g) is loosely defined as a model that takes an input prompt (p) and produces tokens (c) describing a
more complete description of the given prompt, including potential intents, risks, or even relevant technical background
knowledge. The assumption is that LLMs can more accurately decide whether to refuse or comply to the user prompt
(or ask for more information) given a more complete description of it. By definition, a context generator is independent
of the model that will consume the context in order to produce the final answer. We explore three approaches for
generating context: a zero-shot prompting method; employing thinking traces from conventional reasoning models; and
directly training context generator with reinforcement learning (RL).
The zero-shot approach applies a fixed prompt template to elicit intent and risks
from the language model without additional training. We construct a fixed prompt template for an off-the-shelf LLM
that instructs it to output five sections: (1) “User Intent” identifying the user’s goal, (2) “Ambiguity” of requests, (3)
“Potential risks” enumerating safety concerns, (4) “Action decision” choosing to direct answer, ask clarification, or
refuse to answer and (5) “Safe response plan”. We adopt a simple zero-shot prompt template that only requires a
single forward pass through a frozen model. Despite being entirely annotation-free, this template provides baseline

We adopt an autoencoder-style architecture with the hope that the context c gets imbued with more information about
the prompt. To regularize the context, decoder rewards are only applied on the tokens in context c as illustrated in
Figure 2 while context reward Safe(rg ) is applied in all generated tokens. However, optimizing this objective function
may result in undesired behaviors such as a model that simply copies the prompt into the generated c. To discourage
such trivial copying, we add a penalty term. Specifically, we randomly split the prompt into two parts and check whether
the larger segment appears in the context snippet, and assign a reward of 0 if it does.
We design two distinct prompt templates to support our training pipeline: one for the context
generator and one for the decoder model. Each template instructs the model to produce outputs wrapped in special tags,
enabling straightforward parsing and reward computation (see Appendix A.2). For the context generator, we instruct
the model to “think step by step” to generate an explicit risk assessment enclosed together with the corresponding
answers, using the same template employed for zero-shot context generation. For the decoder, we adopt a reconstruction
objective: given a generated context snippet (c) and a corrupted prompt (p′ ), the decoder is prompted to recover both
the original user request and its corresponding response.

to test over-refusal and explicit harmful prompts. To evaluate context-aware inference, we employ two small openweights LLMs, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025), one large open-weights LLM,

Baselines.

Our C ONTEXT L ENS model is trained on a 5,000 samples subset of the WildJailbreak training set, with an additional 500 samples used for validation. We optimize the model using GRPO, with a
learning rate of 1e−6 , clipping parameter of 0.2, batch size of 4, KL coefficient 0.001, and generations per sample 8.
Inference uses fixed decoding parameters, temperature = 1.0, top-k = 1, and a maximum length of 1024 tokens. By
default, we employ Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct model for the context generator gθ and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct model
for the decoder model d. For inference, we use the checkpoint that achieved the highest validation accuracy. Training is
early-stopped once the reward on the validation set exceeds 0.9. We conducted all training experiments using a single
NVIDIA A100 (80 GB). For inference, we additionally employed an NVIDIA RTX A6000 (40 GB).
Training and Inference Details.

We employ large language model as a judge (Röttger et al., 2023), GPT-4o-2024-11-20, to
evaluate attack success rate of model responses. The judge receives both the input prompt and the generated response,
and classifies the output into one of three categories: (1) full compliance, (2) full refusal, or (3) partial refusal, as
described in the prompt template in Appendix A.3. In our evaluation, full compliance is counted as a signal for both
attack success rate (ASR) and compliance rate (Comp.). This provides a direct measure of whether the model yields to
unsafe or adversarial prompts. Since there are trade-off characteristics between compliance rate and refusal rate, we
employ the harmonic average as H-Avg = 2×((1−ASR)×Comp.)
(1−ASR)+Comp.) in the results.

Analysis

We perform two ablation studies to better understand the contribution of each component: 1)
removing individual terms from the reward function, and 2) varying the decoder architecture. Table 4 shows that the
auto-encoder structure is essential for producing informative contexts that translate into improved safety performance
(see pink blocks in the w/o Decoder (d) row). The Safe(·) reward primarily enhances performance on explicitly harmful
prompts (orange blocks), indicating its effectiveness in steering the model toward safer behaviors. The reconstruction
loss SIM contributes to generating higher-quality contexts (yellow blocks), although its influence on quantitative
performance metrics appears relatively similar (blue blocks). Since the decoder remains frozen throughout training,
variations in decoder type exhibit minimal impact on downstream performance, as reported in Table 3.

Conclusion

We propose C ONTEXT L ENS, an RL-trained small LLM that generates additional context for user prompts, which
can be equipped to any foundation model to facilitate context-aware inference. We incentivize this model through an
auto-encoding approach that provides explicit rewards on the generated context by reconstructing the original prompt
and its appropriate response. In addition, we demonstrate that context snippets obtained through zero-shot prompting
and thinking traces from a reasoning model can also serve as effective context to boost ASR and compliance rates on
baseline models across multiple datasets. Our approach, C ONTEXT L ENS, achieves an average 5.6% improvement in
ASR on harmful prompts and an average 6.2% improvement in harmonic mean on XSTest and Wildjailbreak across
multiple baseline models. These results point toward an underexplored path for building safer LLMs, and we hope this
work establishes context-aware inference as a new paradigm for safety-critical AI systems.
