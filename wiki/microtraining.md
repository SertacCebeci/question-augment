# Microtraining

> The training architecture that turns "interview the person well" into
> countable units: one behaviourally defined skill per cycle, a manual, a model
> recording, the trainee's own recording beside it, and a recycle — about an
> hour per skill.

## Definition / Mechanism

**Ivey, Normington, Miller, Morrill & Haase (1968)** identified specific[^s1][^s2][^s3]
behaviours of the helping process and built training on **single discrete
behaviours**, on the belief that a trainee who learns one skill and performs it
well is better served than by classroom methods that teach the skills of
therapy "in a somewhat mystical fashion". Ivey's own later account (1973,
ERIC ED073398) gives the paradigm:

1. Videotape a five-minute segment of the interview.
2. Training: (a) a written manual describing **the single skill being taught**;
   (b) video models of an expert or of "good" communication illustrating that
   skill; (c) the trainee views its own recording and compares its performance
   *on that skill* against the manual and the model; (d) a trainer-supervisor
   adds didactic instruction and support.
3. A second five-to-ten-minute session is recorded.
4. Examine it, and recycle the whole procedure if the skill level warrants.

One pass is about an hour; a recycle adds 30–45 minutes. The mechanism Ivey
names is cue discrimination, modelling, and operant reinforcement by the
trainer, who ignores ineffective behaviour and rewards the change.

The load-bearing move is the **behavioural definition**: the basic skill,
*attending behaviour*, is defined as eye contact, physical attention and verbal
following behaviour — which is what makes presence or absence of the skill
decidable by an observer from a recording, and therefore countable.

Ivey (1973) reports variations: audiotape substituted for videotape with
verbally oriented skills, the first session dropped, the model tapes omitted,
with or without a supervisor — "sometimes proves sufficient".

## Why it matters

1. **It makes an answerer's clarifying behaviour an acquirable unit rather than
   a disposition.** [[query-clarification-rate]] counts a behaviour without
   asking where it comes from; this supplies the only installation procedure
   the reference literature actually used, and `interview-skill-transfer` (draft dropped) is
   its one controlled test on asking.
2. **A skill must be defined so that a third party can decide it from a
   record.** That requirement, not the video equipment, is what carries: an
   asking behaviour that cannot be scored from a transcript cannot be trained
   this way and cannot be measured afterwards.
3. **It supplies no selection rule.** The paradigm installs a repertoire one
   element at a time and says nothing about which element an encounter calls
   for — the gap [[clarification-request]] and [[decision-theoretic-grounding]]
   fill at run time.

## Transfer

| | |
|---|---|
| **Home field** | prepracticum counsellor training: a volunteer client, a five-minute recorded session, a supervisor, and a trainee who will conduct long open-ended interviews |
| **Here** | installing a question-asking repertoire in an answerer — human or built — as separately practised, separately scored units rather than as a single competence |
| **What survives** | the single-skill unit; the behavioural definition that makes a skill decidable from a record; the model-versus-own-output comparison; the recycle-to-criterion loop; the cheapness (one hour per skill) that makes the unit worth isolating at all |
| **What does not** | the live client, the video apparatus and the supervisor, whose necessity Ivey (1973) himself reports only from informal observation; and the assumption that the interview decomposes into skills without residue — **Dewdney's (1986)** experiment moved only two of this arm's skills into the work setting (`interview-skill-transfer` (draft dropped)) |

## Limits

- The procedural detail above — the four steps, the one-hour pass and 30–45
  minute recycle, the cue-discrimination/modelling/operant-reinforcement
  mechanism, the three-part definition of attending behaviour, and the list of
  variations — comes from a full-text read of a **scanned, non-machine-readable**
  ERIC PDF (ED073398) that independent verification could not re-check this pass
  [?]. Only the abstract-level description (video-based, five-minute sessions,
  specific single skills) was independently confirmed; the ERIC record itself is
  confirmed.
- The paradigm is described here from Ivey's 1973 ERIC account; the 1968 origin
  study is cited for provenance only and its text was not read.
- What was read establishes *acquisition*, not benefit to the person
  interviewed. This note claims no effect of any microskill on what an answerer
  recovers; the only outcome evidence the wiki has read for an asking
  application is [[satisfaction-ceiling]], a null on the asker's perception.
- The note's own claim — that a skill exercised in isolation is the same
  behaviour when reassembled into a real interview — is an assumption nothing
  read here tests. It is exactly what the transfer result puts in doubt.
- The variations Ivey lists (dropping the model, the supervisor, the video) are
  reported as informal observation, so the paradigm's necessary components are
  not established by this source.
