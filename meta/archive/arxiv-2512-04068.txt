Abstract
To handle ambiguous queries, AI assistants must decide (a) when to guess
the user’s intent and answer directly, (b) when to enumerate and respond
to multiple plausible intents, and (c) when to ask a clarifying question.
No strategy is always superior; the right decision depends on contextual
factors such as user preferences or modality. For example, enumerating
several possible interpretations can be cumbersome on small screens or
voice-based settings, while a more interactive style may be dispreferred
in desktop settings. In this work, we propose training steerable policies for
managing such uncertainty via self-play. Given two agents, one simulating
a user and the other an AI assistant, we generate conversations where the
user issues a potentially ambiguous query, and the assistant must choose
how to respond. The assistant receives as input the numerical cost of asking
a clarification question, and of generating each word, and is trained to
select the conversational action that maximizes its final reward: accuracy
penalized by these costs. We apply Reinforced Self-Training (ReST) to
optimize this objective and show that it yields a steerable policy that adapts
its behavior based on the provided costs, improving both reward and
accuracy. Moreover, the resulting policy generalizes to numerical cost
values unobserved during training.

Introduction

Establishing common ground between a user and an AI assistant is key for successful
communication, but is an area where AI assistants have been shown to struggle (Shaikh
et al., 2024). As a result, assistants may offer responses that fail to match the true intent of an
underspecified user request, or offer overly verbose responses that attempt to accommodate
all possible intents — both failure modes that could be avoided by more interactive policies
for building common ground, e.g., through clarification questions. Recent work (Chen et al.,
2025; Zhang et al., 2025) has proposed to teach models when to ask clarification questions
by using self-play: an episodic learning strategy in which one language model simulates a
user, and another represents the AI assistant. The assistant is then finetuned on successful
“rolled-out” conversations to learn when to answer a query and when to ask for clarification.
However, such prior work assumes that ambiguity should be uniformly handled by requesting clarification, which does not capture the richness of strategies a conversational
participant might take to manage uncertainty. Given an ambiguous utterance, there are
many possible approaches: (a) guess a single probable intent and respond to it; (b) enumerate multiple possible intents and provide a response for each; (c) issue a clarification
question to pinpoint the exact intent. Which strategy is optimal? This depends on whom
you are talking to, and in what context. Some users dislike long outputs and favor a more
conversational approach; others prefer to avoid multi-turn conversations, but will relax
this preference when it is not possible to process long outputs, such as while driving. In
low-stakes settings it may be acceptable to simply make a best guess. A proficient assistant

should therefore take preferences, interaction modality, and other environmental factors
into account when deciding how to respond. Thus, we need steerable policies for conversation
grounding, which let the assistant flexibly adjust its dialogue strategy on the fly.
We propose a new form of self-play for training a single steerable policy that takes as input
a specification of the reward for a conversation, and modifies its grounding behavior to
maximize the provided reward. Figure 1 provides an example for an ambiguous query. The
model is trained to maximize cost-penalized accuracy, where cost depends on the number of
turns and the length of the final answer. This objective is parameterized by scalar cost coefficients, α and β, provided as part of the input. On the left, the cost of additional conversation
turns is low, so the model clarifies uncertainty by asking a follow-up question. In the middle,
the cost of enumerating interpretations is low, so the model provides answers for two probable interpretations. On the right, both costs are high, so the model makes an educated guess
about user intent, without resolving the uncertainty. We focus in this work on training the
assistant for steerability, assuming cost coefficients are given as input. In practice, cost coefficients can be inferred from user preference data or feedback, which we leave for future work.
To finetune our model, we apply Reinforced Self-Training (ReST), a common post-training
strategy for optimizing rewards (e.g., Gulcehre et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). We use
one model as a user simulator and another as our target assistant, sampling different cost
coefficients per conversation. We then apply collaborative self-play. For each question and
cost coefficient setting, we sample multiple conversations, and compute the true reward.
We then finetune the assistant on the highest-reward conversations, teaching it to condition
on the cost coefficients to maximize its reward.
We evaluate our Steerable Grounding Policy (SGP) on two question-answering benchmarks:
A MBIG QA (Min et al., 2020), where the model answers questions using parametric knowledge, and PACIFIC (Deng et al., 2022), where non-parametric context is provided in the form
of a document and table. We find that even strong prompted models fail to condition on
cost coefficients, even when using chain-of-thought prompting. In contrast, models trained
with SGP appropriately condition on the provided cost coefficients; when needed, they can
substantially alter their inference-time behavior, achieving higher accuracy at lower cost in
accordance with the objective specified in the prompt. Moreover, this generalizes to cost
coefficients never observed during training.

Reward. Let nclar (ρ) denote the number of clarification actions and |o T −1 | the number of

R(ρ; a∗ ) = acc( a, a∗ ) − α · nclar (ρ) − β · |o T −1 |
We want the answer to be accurate, to minimize the number of turns, and to get short
answers. Our reward is motivated by Grice’s maxims (Grice, 1975) for effective communication, including the maxim of quality (make your contributions true), quantity (provide
necessary information only), and manner (avoid ambiguity). We measure accuracy, acc( a, a∗ ),
against the gold answer a∗ with token-level F1 (Rajpurkar et al., 2016), in a [0, 100] scale.
The optimal strategy for this reward function depends on whether it is possible to increase
expected accuracy with clarification questions or multi-answers. E.g., if asking a clarification
question can increase expected accuracy by δ, and α < δ, then asking will yield higher
reward compared to answering directly. Similarly, if a multi-answer adds m words to the
final assistant answer but improves expected accuracy by δ, it is preferable when β · m < δ.

We rely on the base model’s instruction-following ability in two ways: (a) given a clarification
question, we instruct the user to answer without revealing the true interpretation and
(b) given the answer rans , we instruct the user simulator to only use rans and its knowledge
of the true interpretation i j to generate the final answer a. The user is instructed to never
answer based on its parametric knowledge. We manually verify that the user’s instructionfollowing behavior is satisfactory. In addition, using parametric knowledge will lead to
lower performance in asymmetric cases where the assistant’s knowledge is superior, e.g.,
when the assistant has access to a private context containing necessary knowledge.
Thoughts. We experiment with models that use chain-of-thought reasoning to generate
actions. Before sampling an action, the model generates a thought that considers all allowed
actions, and estimates which will yield the highest reward (similar in spirit to Dong et al.
(2026)). Our hypothesis is that using a chain-of-thought will help the model better condition
on the input cost coefficients, since it will explicitly reference coefficients to estimate the
reward for each action. We provide the model a few in-context examples with thoughts,
shown in the full prompts in Section I.2.

Experimental Setup

Datasets A MBIG QA is a QA benchmark built from Natural Questions (Kwiatkowski et al.,
2019), where queries are annotated with multiple interpretations and their answers. In
manual inspection, we found noisy annotations (annotated interpretations that are not
plausible). We therefore perform model-based filtering: we ask Gemini 2.0 Flash (Google,
2024a) to determine if a question is ambiguous, and keep only queries where models
agree with human annotations (see Section I.1 for our prompt). Then, we subsample the
unambiguous queries such that the ratio of ambiguous to unambiguous queries is 2:1.
Overall, this leads to 1,776 training examples and 382 development (dev) examples (the
test set is not publicly available), with an average of 2.41 interpretations per query on the
training set and 2.76 on the dev set. An example ambiguous query is “How many basketball
national championships does Louisville have?”, referring to men’s or women’s basketball.
PACIFIC (Deng et al., 2022) is a multi-turn QA task that requires reasoning about contextual
ambiguity grounded in real financial documents and tabular data (see Figure 5). This creates
two differences from A MBIG QA. First, this is an asymmetric setup, where the assistant
5

Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026

provides an answer that can only be derived from an input document, which the user has
no access to. This prevents the user from answering questions using its own knowledge
while ignoring the assistant’s response. Second, the context-grounded and multi-turn
nature of PACIFIC introduce different types of ambiguity. For example, a turn that appears
underspecified may not be ambiguous given previous context (e.g., red text in Figure 6).
Conversely, seemingly precise queries can be ambiguous due to details in the context (e.g.,
blue text in Figure 6). We use 3,744 training examples and 640 dev examples (test set ground
truth is not publicly available), with equal numbers of ambiguous and unambiguous queries.
Models For the main results presented here, we use Gemma 2 9B (Google, 2024b) as our
baseline model for both the user simulator1 and AI assistant. In Appendices G.1 and G.2,
we show additional results with Gemma 2 2B and QWEN 3 4B. We compare several natural
baselines to our model:
• P ROMPTED: queries the baseline model directly with a description of the task, reward
objective, and several examples. This tests whether the model can natively condition on
cost coefficients. We show the prompt in Section I.2.
• P ROMPTED -COT: Similar to P ROMPTED, except that the prompt includes instructions and
examples to generate a thought that explicitly reasons about which action will maximize
reward. See the prompt in Section I.2.
• A NSWER: uses the baseline but constrains it to answer directly without clarification
questions or multi-answers.
• M ULTI - ANSWER (MA): uses the baseline but constrains the assistant to use #MULTI ANS.
• C LARIFY: uses the baseline but constrains the assistant to use #CLARIFY and then #ANSWER.
• C LARIFY- AND -MA: uses the baseline but constrains the assistant to use #CLARIFY and
then #MULTI ANS.
• A NSWER -FT: To teach the base model the right answer format, we sample direct answers
per question and finetune on the highest-reward answer observations.
• SGP: trains a Steerable Grounding Policy (Algorithm 1).
We also include SGP-O RACLE, an oracle that provides an approximate upper bound on
the achievable reward. Using SGP, for every query q and coefficients α, β, we estimate the
expected reward Eρ [ R(s | α, β)] for each action sequence s. We then select the sequence, s∗ ,
that in hindsight attains the highest expected reward. This offers a reference point for the
potential gains from optimal action sequences.
Last, we conduct a few ablations related to the details of our training procedure, which we
describe in Appendix D.
Training and inference details We use nr = 192 rollouts at training time and nr = 64 at test
time. Interpretations are sampled uniformly from the candidates provided in A MBIG QA and
PACIFIC. In each rollout we randomly sample the clarification cost coefficient α ∼ {0, 2, 20}
and the per-token cost coefficient β ∼ {0.1, 0.7, 5.0}. We limit the number of clarification
questions to 1, since typically there is just one ambiguous aspect in each query, which results
in four possible action sequences: answer vs. multi-answer; with and without clarification
questions. We perform T = 3 ReST epochs, following prior work (Eisenstein et al., 2025).
We tune only the learning rate, choosing the checkpoint with lowest dev loss. As explained
in Section 2.2, we discard rollouts with low F1 : < 0.1 for A MBIG QA and < 0.4 for PACIFIC.
We also discard rollouts when the user ignores the assistant, omitting rollouts when the
fraction of tokens in the answer returned by the user that do not appear in the assistant
answer is above half. We provide statistics on how often filtering happens in Appendix E.
Evaluation We use the following metrics: (a) average reward, (b) average F1 , (c) fraction of
queries with a clarification question, (d) fraction of queries that use #MULTI ANS. We compare
models by query type (ambiguous and unambiguous) and across cost coefficients. We
also report metrics for steerability, which we describe in detail below. Last, we measure
steerability generalization by testing coefficients that did not appear during training.

Experimental Results

Table 2 (top) shows results on A MBIG QA. SGP achieves the highest F1 and reward for
ambiguous and unambiguous queries. SGP also dramatically reduces the rate of clarification
questions and multi-answers compared to P ROMPTED. Using chain-of-thought improves
performance and reduces the rate of clarification questions and multi-answers compared
to P ROMPTED, but lags behind SGP. The rate of clarification and multi-answer actions in
SGP is higher for ambiguous queries than for unambiguous queries, as expected. Training
on direct answer observations (A NSWER -FT) leads to almost always returning a direct
answer, but gets higher F1 than A NSWER since it is finetuned to answer in a better format.
Comparing SGP to SGP-O RACLE, SGP does well in choosing high-reward action sequences,
with a similar rate of clarifications and multi-answers. Still, a ∼10 point headroom remains.
Table 2 (bottom) shows results on PACIFIC. SGP outperforms all baselines in terms of F1 and
reward. Unlike A MBIG QA, P ROMPTED and P ROMPTED -COT use clarification questions
and multi-answers more sparingly. Moreover, both methods use more clarifications and
multi-answers on ambiguous queries than unambiguous ones, showing they distinguish
query types when relevant information is in the prompt. SGP increases the clarification rate
on ambiguous queries to 42.86% to improve its performance. P ROMPTED -COT achieves
higher reward than P ROMPTED, but trails SGP by a large margin. C LARIFY performs
well on ambiguous queries, but worse than SGP. As expected, clarification questions

For clarification questions, We check if asking (or removing) a clarification question is
optimal when modifying α1 → α2 , while fixing β. For multi-answers, we check if asking
(or removing) a multi-answer is optimal when β 1 → β 2 , while fixing α. We then compute
recall, precision, and F1 in the following manner. Recall is the fraction of cases where the
optimal action sequence changes when changing cost coefficients (e.g., adding a clarification
question), and our model predicts the correct change. Precision is the fraction of cases
where our model predicts a certain change in the action sequence, and this change is correct
according to the optimal action sequence change. F1 is the usual harmonic average. We
describe the details of this procedure in Section H.
Table 3 reports results when changing the value of α from 0 → 20 and the value of β from
0.1 → 5.0. SGP outperforms baselines across the board. Results are substantially higher for
steering α compared to β, where precision is high but recall is low. We attribute this to the
baseline model producing better training rollouts for asking good clarification questions
compared to multi-answers. Lastly, our results show there remains substantial headroom in
learning policies that choose the optimal action sequence across prompts.
Generalization to new cost coefficients. We test if SGP can generalize to cost coefficients
unobserved at training time. Figure 4 shows the fraction of clarification questions and multianswers for observed and unobserved cost coefficients (see purple stars in the figure) for
A MBIG QA (left) and PACIFIC (right). When varying α, we fix β = 5, and when varying β, we
fix α = 20. SGP generalizes well to unobserved values of both α and β on both A MBIG QA
and PACIFIC, smoothly adjusting the action frequencies between the values corresponding
to neighboring coefficients observed at training time. This shows SGP yields generalizable
steerability, giving users control even at cost coefficients that are unobserved during training.
Results for the length of the final assistant turn are similar and omitted for brevity.
Are Gemini models steerable? We saw that Gemma 2 9B can be trained to respond to
steering signals, but that it is not steerable “out-of-the-box”. A natural followup is whether
stronger models are steerable without fine-tuning. To this end, we compare SGP to Gemini2.5 Flash (Google, 2025a) and Gemini-3 Flash (Google, 2025b) on A MBIG QA.
Table 4 shows the results for this comparison. Gemini models have better parametric
knowledge and consequently higher F1 . Nevertheless, reward is lower for ambiguous
question, and also for unambiguous questions in Gemini-3, due to sub-optimal use of
clarification questions and multi-answers. More importantly, Gemini-2.5 is not steerable,
with the number of clarification questions increasing (instead of decreasing) when α grows,
and no sensitivity to the value of β. Gemini-3 exhibits moderate steerability, but its overuse
of multi-answers reduces its reward. Overall, the performance of even these strong models
is sub-optimal when asked to maximize a prompt-specified objective.

Related Work

Handling ambiguity User queries are rife with ambiguities, consistent with the principle
of least effort (Zipf, 1949). While Grice’s maxim of quantity suggests that a cooperative
speaker provides as much information as necessary (Grice, 1975), users of AI assistants
may assume context that is unavailable to agent, thereby requiring additional grounding.
Consequently, a growing body of work is focused on handling ambiguity in LLMs (Tanjim
et al., 2025)). A natural approach is to ask clarification questions (Dhole, 2020; Ren et al.,
9

Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026

2023; Zhang & Choi, 2025): for example, Chen et al. (2025) and Zhang et al. (2025) use
self-play with user simulators to learn a policy for asking clarification questions, while Kim
et al. (2024) let language models assess their own uncertainty and design a corresponding
clarification policy. Other work generates long responses enumerating multiple plausible
user interpretations and their answers (Stelmakh et al., 2022; Amplayo et al., 2023; Kim et al.,
2023; In et al., 2025).
Recently, Dong et al. (2026) proposed an inference-time method for deciding when to ask
clarification questions that is based on explicit estimation of the expected value of each
possible agent action. Our P ROMPTED -COT baseline takes a similar approach, but makes
less assumptions on the space of possible clarification questions and answers.
Steerable language policies Teaching contextual steerability has been a north star of
language generation (Hovy, 1987; 1988; Chen et al., 2022; Keskar et al., 2019; Sorensen et al.,
2026). Recent work on adapting to uncertainty includes Cole et al. (2023), who teach models
to abstain from answering questions when uncertain about the asker’s intent, and Kuhn
et al. (2022), who prompt models to quantify their uncertainty to determine when to ask
clarification questions. However, these approaches are “steerable” only by the level of
uncertainty, not by other contextual factors. Wang et al. (2024) train conditional language policies (CLPs) for steerable alignment: given multiple reward models, they train to maximize
a linear combination of rewards, where the combination coefficients are sampled during
training and provided as input at test time. We use a similar approach for training steerable
grounding policies under different cost considerations. However, our approach uses
coefficients specified in the prompts, allowing for training a single model, whereas Wang
et al. (2024) train multiple models (with some parameter sharing) to achieve steerability.
Finally, Sun et al. (2025) use RL to train models to ask clarification questions and respond to
prompt-specified user prompts for agentic tasks. In contrast, we focus on training assistants
to maximize a reward function specified by numerical cost coefficients. In addition, we closely
link steerability to the use of clarification, viewing steerability as a knob for controlling the
spectrum of possible grounding strategies. In contrast, Sun et al. (2025) focus on steerability
for stylistic personalization, which they treat as orthogonal to posing clarification questions.
Multi-agent self-play A large body of work explores the use of multi-agent interaction as
part of post-training (in contrast to inference-time multi-agent systems) for improving reasoning (Chen et al., 2024; Subramaniam et al., 2025) and factuality (Khan et al., 2024). Other
lines of research (including this work) focus on self-play for improving the collaborative
and pragmatic skills of assistants (Lin et al., 2024). Beyond ambiguity, past work has also explored self-play for improving uncertainty expression (Stengel-Eskin et al., 2024; Eisenstein
et al., 2025), tool use (Eisenstein et al., 2025), handling of persuasion (Stengel-Eskin et al.,
2025), personalization (Wagner et al., 2024), and general collaborative skills (Wu et al., 2025).
Our work uses multi-agent self play as a training framework, applying it to the objective of
learning a cost-sensitive, dynamically steerable policy for managing uncertainty.

6

Conclusions

Prior work on uncertainty has focused on hedging and clarification in isolation, addressing
single disambiguation strategies. We argue that choosing the right disambiguation strategy
is contextual, and propose training models that select a cost-optimal strategy, based on
numerical steering coefficients. We achieve this through SGP, yielding cost-accuracy tradeoffs that are responsive to steering signals while also being pareto-optimal in aggregate.
Looking forward, a natural next step is to close the loop and infer user preferences over
time, leading to proactive and flexible AI assistants that infer user preferences and adapt
to them on-the-fly. We focused on three disambiguation strategies — guessing, clarifying,
and hedging — but the range of subtle grounding actions, that human speakers use to
signal interpretation difficulties is much wider, often including nonverbal cues. Extending
SGP to such grounding actions is a challenge for future work. Finally, steering models
with numerical values has applications beyond uncertainty management, in areas such as
controlling tool use, test-time compute, and more.

Ablations on the Training Procedure

Our training algorithm is a slight variant of ReST (Gulcehre et al., 2023), as we describe
in Section 2. We conduct two ablations on this training procedure. First, we check whether
grouping of rollouts based on action sequences is necessary by ablating this part and simply
choosing the highest-reward rollout per query without any grouping. Second, we check
if training on observations is beneficial by training on actions only. For both ablations we
conduct a single round of ReST.
Table 5 and Table 6 show the results of these ablations. We observe that in almost all cases
our approach outperforms the ablations, typically by a large margin.

Prompt-level Steerability Metric

Here, we describe the steps for computing the prompt-level steerability metric shown
in Section 4 and Table 3. Let’s assume we are computing prompt-level steerability when
modifying α1 → α2 (β 1 → β 2 is similar).
1. For each query q, cost-coefficients α, β, and action sequence s, generate n rollouts.2
2. For each query q, cost-coefficients α, β find the approximately optimal action sequence
(or sequences) s∗ , with highest average reward across rollouts.
3. For each β compute recall, precision, and F1 :
(a) for each query q, find the optimal action sequences s1∗ and s2∗ for cost coefficients
(α1 , β) and (α2 , β).
(b) label the query with the gold label ‘+C’ if s2∗ has a clarification question but s1∗
does not, ‘-C’, if s2∗ does not have a clarification question but s1∗ does, and ‘N’ if
there is no change in clarification questions.
(c) sample from the model m rollouts3 for both (α1 , β) and (α2 , β) and estimate
the predicted action sequences smodel
and smodel
, by taking the action sequence

1
that was the sampled the most. Similarly label each query q with the predicted
labels ‘+C’, ’-C’, and ’N’ based on the change in the predicted action sequence.
(d) Compute recall as the fraction of all examples with gold-label ‘+C’ or ’-C’ for
which the model predicts the correct label. Compute precision as the fraction
of all examples where the model predicted ‘+C’ or ’-C’ and the prediction is
correct according to the gold label. Compute F1 as the harmonic average.
4. Average recall, precision, and F1 across the values of β.

Filtering prompt

The following prompt was used for filtering A MBIG QA questions, where we verified that

Prompt for verifying ambiguous questions
You are a smart AI assistant whose goal is to answer questions. Questions can be ambiguous, that is, they might mean more
than one thing which could affect the answer.
Given a question, think about all possible interpretations of this question and their answer and determine if different
interpretations lead to a different answer. If this is the case, ask a clarification question that will allow you to answer
the question. If there is just one possible interpretation or if all interpretations lead to the same answer, just answer
the question directly.
Please format your thoughts and answers according to the examples below.
Example 1:
QUESTION: Who won the election?
THOUGHT: The question doesn’t say which election and different elections have different winners so I need to ask a
clarification question.
CLARIFICATION: What elections are you referring to?
Example 2:
QUESTION: On what year did world war 2 start?
THOUGHT: World war 2 is a well-defined event that started in a particular year, 1939, and so I can answer directly.
ANSWER: 1939
QUESTION: <question>

Prompt for AI assistant
You are a helpful agent whose goal is to answer a question provided by another "asker" agent. Sometimes the question
you receive might be ambiguous; if so, it can be good to ask a clarification question. You will be provided with
all past clarification questions that have been asked, and their answers. As a helpful agent, your primary goal is
to be accurate, while balancing asking necessary clarification questions together with providing short and succinct responses.
You can perform one of the following three actions:
1. ***ANSWER** : if you are sufficiently confident that you know the meaning of the question (based on the question and all
past clarification questions and their answers), you can answer it.
2. **CLARIFY** : if you are unsure of what the question means (and that will affect your answer), you can ask the asker
agent for more information by formulating a clarification question.
3. **MULTI ANSWER** : if you are unsure what the question means, but you can still succinctly answer all possible
interpretations of the question, you can provide a "multi-answer", that is, an answer that enumerates each interpretation
and answers it. If it is not possible to enumerate a small set of interpretations, it may be better to ask a clarification
question to be able to refine your answer.
Let’s define a few variables:
‘acc‘ is your accuracy, where 0 is not accurate at all and 100 is perfectly accurate.
‘num clar‘ is the number of clarification questions you had to ask.
‘num words‘ is the number of words in your final ANSWER assuming you chose to **ANSWER** or **MULTI ANSWER**.
Your goal is then to maximize your ‘reward‘ which is defined as ‘acc - ALPHA * num clar - BETA * num words‘ where ‘ALPHA‘
and ‘BETA‘ are constants that you will be given. In words, you want to be accurate, not ask clarification questions when
they are not necessary, and you want to provide short answers. Your behavior should change depending on the particular
balance of ‘ALPHA‘ vs. ‘BETA‘, and your confidence. Specifically, your strategy should be to estimate what action will
lead to the highest reward. For example, if asking a clarification question is likely to lead to a much higher accuracy
(leading to higher reward), then you should choose to ask a clarification question. Similarly, if you can already provide a
multi-answer that will lead to higher accuracy and is relatively short, then you should provide a multi-answer.
**IMPORTANT NOTE**: You should only use **MULTI ANSWER** if you plan to enumerate two interpretations or more and provide
an answer for each interpretation. If you plan to provide just one interpretation and its answer (and optionally ask the
user if they mean something else), please use **ANSWER**.
Here are some examples.
Example 1:
ALPHA : **10.0**
BETA : **1.0**
QUESTION : in what year did world war ii start?
ACTION : **ANSWER** : 1939
Example 2:
ALPHA : **1.0**
BETA : **10.0**
QUESTION : in what year did the first harry potter come out?
CLARIFICATION QUESTION : the book or the movie?
ANSWER TO CLARIFICATION : the book.
CLARIFICATION QUESTION : in what country?
ANSWER TO CLARIFICATION : the uk.
ACTION : **ANSWER** : 1997
Example 3:
ALPHA : **10.0**
BETA : **0.1**
QUESTION : in what year did the first harry potter come out?
CLARIFICATION QUESTION : the book or the movie?
ANSWER TO CLARIFICATION : the book.
ACTION : **MULTI ANSWER** : The book came out in the uk in 1997 and in the us in 1998.
Example 4:
ALPHA : **10.0**
BETA : **0.1**
QUESTION : in what year did the first harry potter come out in the US?
ACTION : **MULTI ANSWER** : The first harry potter book came out in the us in 1998,the first harry potter movie in 2001.
Example 5:
ALPHA : **0.1**
BETA : **1.0**
QUESTION : in what year did the first harry potter come out?
CLARIFICATION QUESTION : the book or the movie?
ANSWER TO CLARIFICATION : the book.
ACTION : **CLARIFY** : in the uk or the us?
ALPHA : {ALPHA VAR}

Prompt for AI assistant with chain-of-thought
You are a helpful agent whose goal is to answer a question provided by another "asker" agent. Sometimes the question
you receive might be ambiguous; if so, it can be good to ask a clarification question. You will be provided with
all past clarification questions that have been asked, and their answers. As a helpful agent, your primary goal is
to be accurate, while balancing asking necessary clarification questions together with providing short and succinct responses.
You can perform one of the following three actions:
1. ***ANSWER** : if you are sufficiently confident that you know the meaning of the question (based on the question and all
past clarification questions and their answers), you can answer it.
2. **CLARIFY** : if you are unsure of what the question means (and that will affect your answer), you can ask the asker
agent for more information by formulating a clarification question.
3. **MULTI ANSWER** : if you are unsure what the question means, but you can still succinctly answer all possible
interpretations of the question, you can provide a "multi-answer", that is, an answer that enumerates each interpretation
and answers it. If it is not possible to enumerate a small set of interpretations, it may be better to ask a clarification
question to be able to refine your answer. You will be provided with a list of ALLOWED ACTIONS, since not all actions are
available in all situations.
Let’s define a few variables:
‘acc‘ is your accuracy, where 0 is not accurate at all and 100 is perfectly accurate.
‘num clar‘ is the number of clarification questions you had to ask.
‘num words‘ is the number of words in your final ANSWER assuming you chose to **ANSWER** or **MULTI ANSWER**.
Your goal is then to maximize your ‘reward‘ which is defined as ‘acc - ALPHA * num clar - BETA * num words‘ where ‘ALPHA‘
and ‘BETA‘ are constants that you will be given. In words, you want to be accurate, not ask clarification questions when
they are not necessary, and you want to provide short answers. Your behavior should change depending on the particular
balance of ‘ALPHA‘ vs. ‘BETA‘, your confidence, and the set of ALLOWED ACTIONS. Specifically, your strategy should be to
estimate which of the ALLOWED ACTIONS will lead to the highest reward. For example, if asking a clarification question is
likely to lead to a much higher accuracy (that will lead to higher reward), then you should choose to ask a clarification
question. Similarly, if you can already provide a multi-answer that will lead to higher accuracy and is relatively short,
then you should choose to provide a multi-answer. To estimate this, you should output a THOUGHT where you try to explicitly
estimate the reward for different actions. After your THOUGHT is done, you should output the ACTION that you will take, as
shown in the examples.
Here are some examples.
Example 1:
ALPHA : **10.0**
BETA : **1.0**
ALLOWED ACTIONS : {‘CLARIFY’, ‘MULTI ANSWER’, ‘ANSWER’}
QUESTION : in what year did world war ii start?
THOUGHT : I am confident that the answer is 1939, and thus I can answer the question correctly with one word. My reward
will be 100.0 - 10.0 * 0 - 1.0 * 1 = 99.0. This is the highest reward I can get, and so I will choose to answer directly.
ACTION : **ANSWER** : 1939
Example 2:
ALPHA : **1.0**
BETA : **10.0**
ALLOWED ACTIONS : {‘MULTI ANSWER’, ‘ANSWER’}
QUESTION : in what year did the first harry potter come out?
CLARIFICATION QUESTION : the book or the movie?
ANSWER TO CLARIFICATION : the book.
CLARIFICATION QUESTION : in what country?
ANSWER TO CLARIFICATION : the uk.
THOUGHT : Given the previous clarification questions and answers, I am confident that the answer is 1997, and thus I can
answer the question correctly with one word. Moreover, I cannot ask additional clarification questions because CLARIFY is
not in the list of ALLOWED ACTIONS. I already asked two clarification questions in previous turns, so my reward will be
100.0 - 1.0 * 2 - 10.0 * 1 = 88.0. This is the highest reward I can get at this point, so I will choose to answer.
ACTION : **ANSWER** : 1997
Example 3:
ALPHA : **10.0**
BETA : **0.1**
ALLOWED ACTIONS : {‘MULTI ANSWER’, ‘ANSWER’}
QUESTION : in what year did the first harry potter come out?
CLARIFICATION QUESTION : the book or the movie?
ANSWER TO CLARIFICATION : the book.
THOUGHT : I know that the question is about the book, however, I am not sure if the question is about the publication date
of the book in the uk or in the us. If I guess one of the two and answer directly, I can answer in one word. My expected
accuracy will be 50.0. Since I already asked one clarification question, my reward will be 50.0 - 10.0 * 1 - 0.1 * 1 = 39.9.
I cannot ask a clarification question because it is not in the list of ALLOWED ACTIONS. Finally, if I provide a multi-answer
that contains the dates for both the uk and the us, my accuracy will be 100.0, but my answer will be long; I expect around
10 words. Since I already asked one clarification question and my response will be 10 words, my reward will be 100.0 10.0 * 1 - 0.1 * 10 = 89.0. This is the best expected score I can get, because the cost of clarification questions is high
relative to the cost of words in my final answer.
ACTION : **MULTI ANSWER** : The book came out in the uk in 1997 and in the us in 1998.
Example 4:
ALPHA : **10.0**
BETA : **0.1**
ALLOWED ACTIONS : {‘CLARIFY’, ‘MULTI ANSWER’, ‘ANSWER’}
QUESTION : in what year did the first harry potter come out in the US?
THOUGHT : I am not sure if the question is about the movie or the book? If I guess one of the two and answer directly,

Prompt for user simulator answering a clarification question
You are a helpful agent whose goal is to clarify a potentially ambiguous question. You have access to a question in two
forms. One is a potentially ambiguous form and the other is an unambiguous form of that question.
You will also be given a clarification question issued by a different agent that only has access to the ambiguous question
and is trying to clarify it. Your only job is to answer the clarification question to the best of your ability. It is
possible that the ambiguous and unambiguous questions are identical.
You are not allowed to directly reveal information about the unambiguous question. You are only allowed to answer the
clarification question based on the unambiguous version of the question. If the clarification question is not answerable
from the unambiguous question, you should answer that you do not know the answer. Again, **NEVER** just repeat the
unambiguous question as an answer. In addition, **NEVER** clarify the question using information that is not inferred from
the unambiguous question. You will get a prize if you manage to complete the task successfully. Always respond in a single
line with the format
’ACTION : **ANSWER CLARIFICATION** : <the answer to the clarification question>’

Potentially ambiguous question: When did the first harry potter come out?
Unambiguous question: When did the first harry potter book come out in the uk?
Clarification question: Did you mean the book or the movie?
ACTION : **ANSWER CLARIFICATION** : the book.

Unambiguous question: When did the first harry potter book come out in the uk?

Prompt for user simulator returning a final answer
You are a helpful agent whose goal is to take a question and an answer to the question that is provided to you by another
agent, and use it to phrase a final answer to the question.
You will get two versions of the question, one is a potentially ambiguous question, and the other is an unambiguous version.
You will also get the answer provided by the other agent. The other agent does not have direct access to the unambiguous
question. Your goal is to answer the question based on the answer provided by the other agent and your privileged knowledge
of the unambiguous question. DO NOT answer the question based on your own knowledge. Only use the answer given by the other
agent, and your knowledge about the unambiguous question.
You will use the action **ANSWER** to pass along the answer.
Importantly, the other agent can give an answer in one of two forms:
1. Just give a single answer to the question, in which case you just need to pass it along as is.
2. The other agent might give you a ‘multi-answer‘ where it specifies possible interpretations to the question and the
answer to each interpretations. Since you have access to the unambiguous version you can select the right interpretation
and provide an answer based on that.
Always respond in a single line with the format ’ACTION : **ANSWER** : <the answer to the question>’
Example 1, where the answer given by the other agent is wrong and is passed as is:
Potentially ambiguous question: in what year did world war i start?
Unambiguous question: in what year did world war i start? FRIEND’s ANSWER: 1939
ACTION : **ANSWER** : 1939
Example 2, where the answer given by the other agent is correct and is passed as is:
Potentially ambiguous question: in what year did world war ii start?
Unambiguous question: in what year did world war ii start?
FRIEND’s ANSWER: 1939
ACTION : **ANSWER** : 1939
Example 3, where the other agent provides a multi-answer:
Potentially ambiguous question: in what year did the first harry potter come out?
Unambiguous question: in what year did the first harry potter book come out in the uk?
FRIEND’s ANSWER: If you mean when the book harry potter came out in the uk, then the answer is 1997, if you mean when did

ACTION : **ANSWER** : 1997

Potentially ambiguous question: in what year did the first harry potter come out?
Unambiguous question: in what year did the first harry potter book come out in the uk?
FRIEND’s ANSWER: I think you mean when the movie came out, and that was in 2001.
ACTION : **ANSWER** : 2001.
