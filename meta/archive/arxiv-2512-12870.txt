Abstract
Active Learning (AL) is commonly used in applications where labeling data is expensive or
time-consuming. In practice, however, labels are often noisy due to varying labeler expertise and annotation uncertainty, especially for complex or ambiguous samples. Learning
from such imperfectly labeled data can degrade classifier performance. We propose an AL
framework that explicitly accounts for label noise by optimally assigning labelers and selecting samples to minimize labeling error. Our approach, called OLAS (Optimal Labeler
Assignment and Sampling), uses a noise model that depends on both labeler accuracy and
model uncertainty to guide these decisions. We develop two tractable optimization formulations: one for assigning samples to labelers to minimize worst-case noise, and another for
selecting samples while controlling overall label noise. Theoretical results provide closedform solutions under mild conditions. Empirical evaluations on benchmark datasets and
a real-world warranty claim classification problem show that OLAS achieves the highest
or near-highest classification accuracy among existing AL strategies across most settings,
using only a single label per sample.
Keywords: Active Learning, Noisy Labels, Labeler Assignment, Sample Selection, Optimization, Classification, Data Quality

1 Introduction
In typical classification problems, machine learning models are trained using a set of labeled
data points, with these labels being assigned through annotation. In numerous applications,
the annotation of samples can be a time-consuming or costly endeavor. For instance, Singh
et al. (2009) explored the challenges of image classification, where annotating images can be
1

labor-intensive. Similarly, in the field of additive manufacturing, acquiring labels has become
increasingly time- and labor-intensive, especially with the integration of advanced sensors
that generate high-dimensional, large datasets (van Houtum and Vlasea 2021). Given the
vast volumes of datasets in many such applications, fully annotating all instances has become
an exceedingly challenging, if not impossible, task. As a result, the training of these models
often relies on a limited set of labeled data.
The limitation in annotating unlabeled samples naturally establishes a labeling budget
defined as the number of unlabeled observations that can be annotated. Consequently,
selecting a subset for annotation becomes a critical task. In general learning problems,
there are typically two strategies for choosing the best samples: one-shot sampling methods
and sequential sampling methods. In the one-shot sampling approach, a fixed number of
observations are chosen based on the available budget. Once the labels for all these samples
are obtained, a model can be trained using this labeled training data (Johnson et al. 1990,
Santner et al. 2018). Conversely, in the sequential sampling design, new observations are
selected over time, informed by data from previously collected samples. At each step, these
accumulated samples are used to update the training model, and the information gained
from the most recently updated model guides the selection of subsequent samples Gahrooei
et al. (2019), Huang et al. (2023). Therefore, while one-shot sampling methods do not utilize
information from earlier observations, sequential sampling methods effectively address this
limitation by iteratively updating the model with the most recent observations.
By employing sequential sampling, the total labeling budget can be distributed across
several cycles. In each cycle, a number of observations are sampled and labeled, based on
the budget allocated for that cycle. Subsequently, the model is updated with this new batch
of labels. In the context of Machine Learning, this procedure is termed active learning (AL).
In AL, the key challenge is selecting samples in each cycle by leveraging insights from the
most recently updated model. A common approach known as uncertainty sampling assumes
that observations with higher uncertainty offer more information about the underlying class
distribution, making them ideal candidates for labeling (Settles 2012). There are various
methods to quantify uncertainty, leading to different uncertainty sampling strategies, including least confident (Settles and Craven 2008), margin sampling (Scheffer et al. 2001),
and entropy sampling (Li et al. 2019). Among these, entropy sampling, which employs the
entropy measure for sampling the observations, is arguably the most widely used method in
AL. Therefore, our focus in this paper will be on entropy sampling.
Annotating data points involves assigning labels to unlabeled samples, typically performed by various labelers (oracles). However, in practical scenarios, these labels can often
be incorrect due to noisy labelers as noted by Gupta et al. (2019). As a result, the labels
obtained might be erroneous, and training a model with such incorrect labels can lead to
an inaccurate prediction model. Abdellatif et al. (2021) investigated the issue in the context
of connected vehicles, where a single road event may be classified into various classes using
multiple vehicles simultaneously. Each vehicle is equipped with a weak prediction model
which can generate noisy labels. A critical aspect of noisy labeling is that observations with
higher uncertainty are more prone to label noise, as discussed by Du and Ling (2010). This
suggests that samples for which the classifier is less confident are also likely to be less certain
for the labelers. This presents a challenge in uncertainty sampling methods, as the tendency
to select uncertain observations can unintentionally increase susceptibility to label noise.
2

Another motivating application of the problem described above is automated claim management (ACM) for warranty claims in manufacturing. Companies receive warranty claims
on a daily basis, and each claim must be categorized into a predefined category. ACM employs machine learning models to categorize all incoming warranty claims. Concurrently,
there is a manual process of binning these claims, performed by technicians who act as
labelers and provide data for updating the classification model. Due to the large volume
of warranty claim data, technicians can only label a subset of the claims. This limitation
motivates the integration of Active Learning (AL) into the claim-binning process, leading to
the development of an AL-based ACM process for the company. In each AL cycle, selected
observations are sent to technicians for labeling, and the received labels are then used to
update the classification model. However, due to differences in skill and experience levels,
technicians may make mistakes during the manual binning process, resulting in noisy labels.
This motivates the need for a systematic approach to handle noisy labelers in active learning
settings.
The main goal of this paper is to propose a novel active learning (AL) framework for
selecting informative samples and assigning them to labelers, considering their skill levels
and labeling noise. We refer to our method as OLAS (Optimal Labeler Assignment and
Sampling for Active Learning). We examine the process of noise generation within the
AL framework and develop a formulation for assigning query points to labelers, aiming
to minimize worst-case noise. Additionally, we adapt the entropy sampling approach to
address the challenge of imperfect labels by reformulating it as an optimization model. We
also introduce an efficient method for solving this model to optimality.
Much prior work addresses noisy annotations from multiple labelers by aggregating or
reconciling labels after they have been collected. The classical Dawid and Skene model
(Dawid and Skene 1979) estimates true labels and labeler reliabilities jointly via expectation maximization. Rodrigues et al. (2013) propose a related probabilistic model that treats
each annotator’s reliability as a latent Bernoulli variable, reducing the number of parameters
relative to full confusion matrix approaches and thereby avoiding overfitting when each annotator labels only a small fraction of the data, a setting we return to directly in Section 4.3
using the same music genre classification data introduced in that paper. In a similar spirit,
Sheng et al. (2008) study how to improve data quality by requesting repeated labels for
the same instance from multiple noisy labelers, and Zhao et al. (2011) propose incremental
relabeling strategies that identify and re-annotate instances whose labels appear inconsistent across the crowd. Kałuża et al. (2023) propose annotation unification algorithms that
estimate annotator reliability and reconcile labels from sparse, largely non-overlapping annotators, requiring little to no intersection between the samples seen by different labelers.
All of these approaches share a common structure: labels are collected, often redundantly
or from overlapping annotators, and quality is improved by aggregating or reconciling them
after acquisition.
Other work brings this same correction after acquisition into the active learning loop
itself, rather than treating it as a separate offline step. Wang et al. (2023) allocate part
of the labeling budget to relabeling instances suspected of being mislabeled, rather than
exclusively acquiring labels for new instances, and Younesian et al. (2021) similarly filter
already-labeled instances into clean and suspected-noisy groups and send the most informative suspected-noisy instances back to an oracle for relabeling. Both approaches improve
3

label quality by revisiting samples after an initial, possibly incorrect, label has already been
obtained. By contrast, Zhang and Chaudhuri (2015) intervene earlier, before any label is
collected for a given instance, by studying active learning with both weak and strong labelers and addressing the trade off between labeler quality and cost; to our knowledge, this
is the closest existing precedent for deciding, proactively, which labeler should annotate a
previously unlabeled instance, though their method selects which instances to query under
label noise without jointly optimizing which specific labeler receives each instance as part
of the same decision.
A closer line of work performs sample and labeler selection jointly. Huang et al. (2017)
propose an active selection criterion that evaluates instance-labeler pairs by cost-effectiveness,
exploiting the fact that a labeler with lower overall quality can still be accurate on instances
within their specific expertise. Fang et al. (2014) transfer knowledge from unlabeled auxiliary domains to estimate labeler expertise, then jointly select the most informative instance
and the labeler with the best expertise for it. Zhao et al. (2014) jointly estimate worker
performance, task difficulty, and annotation reliability within a Bayesian network, using the
resulting risk estimates to guide sample selection. Long et al. (2013) estimate each labeler’s
expertise alongside a Gaussian process classifier and actively select both which instance to
query and which labeler should annotate it. OLAS differs from these four along several
dimensions: all four permit repeated or redundant labeling across labelers, whereas OLAS
restricts each sample to a single label; none models an explicit per-cycle labeler capacity
constraint, whereas OLAS does; each selects a labeler to maximize expected label quality
or minimize expected risk for the queried instance, whereas OLAS minimizes the maximum
noise across all assigned labels, a worst-case rather than average-case criterion; and each
relies on heuristic selection criteria or approximate inference, whereas OLAS admits closedform optimal solutions (Theorems 1 and 2) and a polynomial-time algorithm (Algorithm 1).
OLAS differs from this literature in how it handles noisy annotations. Unlike aggregation,
reconciliation, and relabeling strategies such as Dawid and Skene (Dawid and Skene 1979),
Rodrigues et al. (2013), Sheng et al. (2008), Zhao et al. (2011), Kałuża et al. (2023), Wang
et al. (2023), and Younesian et al. (2021), OLAS never collects more than one label per
sample and never revisits or reconciles a sample’s label once it has been acquired; there is no
redundancy to aggregate and no correction step to design. This distinction matters whenever
repeated, overlapping, or revisited annotation is not merely undesirable but infeasible, for
example when each additional label carries meaningful cost or delay, as in our motivating
warranty claim application in Section 4. Instead, OLAS uses its labeling budget to reduce
the expected noise of the single acquired label for each instance, which requires deciding
sample selection and labeler assignment together, before any label exists, rather than as a
two-stage process of first labeling and then correcting.
Relative to Huang et al. (2017), Fang et al. (2014), Zhao et al. (2014), Long et al. (2013),
and Zhang and Chaudhuri (2015), the closest precedents for joint or proactive labeler assignment, OLAS additionally restricts each sample to a single label under explicit labeler
capacity constraints, and optimizes a worst-case rather than average-case noise objective,
as detailed above. OLAS formulates this proactive, joint decision, of which sample to query
and which single labeler should annotate it, as one optimization problem, for which we
derive closed-form optimal solutions (Theorems 1 and 2) and a polynomial-time algorithm
(Algorithm 1), rather than relying on heuristic or approximate assignment rules. By proac4

2 Overview of the Framework
The AL framework starts with some initially labeled data points. A classifier is then trained
with these initially labeled instances. Due to labeling limitations in an AL paradigm, we can
only label a partial subset of the unlabeled samples. Hence, we train and update the model
sequentially, based on a subset of newly labeled training samples in each cycle or iteration.
Suppose there is a limited budget for labeling only T × B instances, where B is the labeling
budget in each cycle and T is the number of cycles. We denote cycles by t = 1, 2, . . . , T .
At each cycle, a sampling criterion is utilized to choose B samples from the pool of
unlabeled instances. These selected samples are then assigned to labelers according to the
optimization model we propose in the next section, and their labels are queried. The new
labels are added to the training set and the model is updated based on the new training set.
This process will be repeated for T cycles. Figure 1 illustrates the overall AL framework.
Due to noisy oracles/labelers, we may receive incorrect labels in each cycle, which can
degrade the performance of the classifier over time. To maintain an effective active learning
(AL) process, it is essential to account for labeling noise in each cycle, both during sampling
and assignment. To address this, we model the label noise and apply optimization techniques
to propose a sampling strategy, followed by an assignment policy that together form a robust
5

AL framework. We will first study the assignment problem and then propose the optimal
sampling approach based on the findings from the labeler assignment problem.

3 Methodology
An AL problem starts with the query selection where the set of unlabeled instances will be
investigated to select a set of samples called the query set. The selection of the query set
depends on the sampling strategy. For now, assume that we have used a specific sampling
strategy and obtained a query set Qt ⊆ Ut , where Qt and Ut denote the selected query set
and set of unlabeled data points at Cycle t of AL.
After selecting the query set, each sample in the query set should be assigned to a labeler.
We assume we have M labelers where the i-th labeler has an accuracy of ai ∈ [0, 1] and
capacity of ci ∈ N. The accuracy of a labeler is defined as the probability of correctly labeling
a sample. We refer to ai as the labeler’s baseline reliability score, since it characterizes the
labeler independently of any particular sample. The noise actually incurred on a specific label
is given by the noise function ϵ(ai , e) introduced below, which also depends on the entropy
e of the sample being labeled and is therefore generally not equal to 1 − ai ; a highly reliable
labeler (ai close to 1) can still generate substantial noise on a highly ambiguous sample.
We assume these probabilities can be estimated using some historical data, and hence they
are given parameters. The capacity is defined as the number of samples that a labeler can
annotate inP

We also assume that each query point should be labeled only one time using one labeler.
We note that it is, in principle, possible to extend the proposed framework to allow multiple
labels per sample by jointly optimizing the number of annotations and the subset of labelers
assigned to each data point. However, such a formulation introduces additional discrete
decision variables, coupling constraints across labelers, and nontrivial trade-offs between redundancy, aggregation, and budget allocation. This significantly increases the complexity
of the optimization problem and leads to a qualitatively different modeling regime. In this
work, we focus on the single-label-per-sample setting, which captures many practical scenarios where redundant labeling is infeasible due to cost, latency, or limited expert availability,
while leaving multi-label extensions as an important direction for future research.
The single-label constraint is particularly relevant in settings where annotation costs are
high, expert availability is limited, or latency requirements preclude iterative consensus processes. Examples include medical image annotation by specialists, legal document review,
and industrial quality inspection, where each label incurs significant time or monetary cost.
In such scenarios, optimizing the assignment of individual expert labels to maximize information gain under budget constraints is more practical than assuming redundant annotations
are feasible. Our framework directly addresses this problem class by proactively minimizing expected label noise through optimal sample–labeler matching, rather than relying on
post-hoc error correction via aggregation.

3.1 Labeler Assignment and Noise Model
Once a selected query point is assigned to a labeler, the labeler examines the instance
and returns the corresponding class for the point. Due to the existence of noisy oracles,
the label received from the labeler might be incorrect. We assume that the label noise is
measured with a value between 0 and 1, where 0 represents a perfect label (ground truth),
and 1 indicates the highest possible noise level. As a general rule, label noise represents the
probability that the received label from the oracle is incorrect.
Intuitively, the label noise is affected by two factors: the uncertainty of the sample and
the accuracy of the labeler. The uncertainty of a sample is evaluated in each cycle of AL
using the most recently updated classification model, ensuring that the entropy reflects the
model’s current belief about class probabilities. This adaptive calculation avoids the risk
of using outdated uncertainty estimates and leads to more effective sampling as the labeled
dataset evolves.
In this paper, we use the entropy measure to quantify the uncertainty of a data point.

values between 0 and 1 that follows the following conditions is regarded as a valid noise
function: the function should be decreasing in terms of labeler accuracy and the function
should be increasing in terms of sample uncertainty. The first property is straightforward
since by using a high-accuracy labeler, we expect the label noise to decrease. The second
property follows the fact that if a sample point is uncertain for the model, the labeler will
have less confidence labeling the sample, and hence, the resulting label may have higher
noise (Du and Ling 2010). This means that unlabeled data points with higher entropy
are more likely to receive incorrect labels from oracles, and therefore, the noise function
should be an increasing function of the entropy measure. Rather than assuming a specific
parametric form for annotation noise, we only require the noise function ϵ(a, e) to satisfy
two structural properties: it must be monotonically decreasing in the labeler accuracy a
and monotonically increasing in the sample entropy e. These conditions reflect the intuitive
assumptions that more reliable labelers generate fewer errors and that ambiguous samples are
inherently more prone to mislabeling. Any function satisfying these properties is admissible
within our framework, and the optimization results in Section 3.2 hold independently of the
specific functional form. In our experiments, we explored two different valid noise functions.
The proposed noise function ϵ(a, e) captures two fundamental sources of annotation
error in active learning: labeler reliability and sample ambiguity. Labeler accuracy a ∈ [0, 1]
reflects the intrinsic probability that a labeler produces a correct annotation, while the
7

entropy e(x) represents the model’s uncertainty about the class membership of a sample.
Prior work has shown that samples with higher uncertainty are not only harder for classifiers,
but also more difficult for human annotators, leading to increased annotation errors (Du and
Ling 2010). Therefore, modeling label noise as a joint function of labeler accuracy and sample
uncertainty provides a principled mechanism for quantifying the likelihood of incorrect labels
during active learning.
After defining the noise function, the next step is to optimally assign query points to the
available labelers using the noise function. In the next section, we provide an optimization
formulation for the labeler assignment model to find the best possible assignment.

The proposed optimization formulation relies on several modeling assumptions that are
standard in sequential decision-making and active learning settings. First, we assume that
each label acquisition incurs a fixed budget cost and that the total labeling budget is limited, reflecting practical constraints in real annotation workflows. Second, we represent
labeler reliability and sample difficulty through summary quantities that guide assignment
and selection decisions, enabling a tractable optimization model. Third, the ordering and
feasibility conditions in the formulation ensure that the resulting solution defines a valid
sequential assignment policy under the given budget and quality constraints. These assumptions are introduced to obtain an interpretable and computationally efficient policy.
While the theoretical results rely on the stated structure of the optimization model, the
overall framework is flexible and can accommodate alternative utility definitions, additional
operational constraints, or domain-specific budget structures without changing the high-level
approach. Specific modeling choices such as the noise model structure and the single-label
constraint are discussed in detail in their respective subsections.

the sampling strategy, it will result in a more robust sampling to the label noise compared
to the common entropy-based sampling approaches. In the following section, we propose

The parameter β represents the maximum acceptable noise threshold for label acquisition. It controls the trade-off between sample informativeness and label quality: smaller
values of β enforce stricter quality control by rejecting sample–labeler assignments with
high expected noise ϵ(ai , ej ) > β, while larger values permit more aggressive sampling of
uncertain points at the cost of potentially noisier labels. In practice, β can be set based on
domain-specific error tolerance or tuned via cross-validation on a validation set with goldstandard labels (see Section 3.4). This formulation allows OLAS to systematically balance
exploration of high-uncertainty samples against the risk of annotation errors.

The proposed OLAS framework is not limited to a specific application domain. Its design

sample uncertainty (such as entropy), and a labeling capacity constraint. Therefore, the
optimization formulations and assignment strategy can be applied to other classification
tasks with noisy oracles, including crowdsourcing, medical annotation, and human-in-theloop labeling scenarios.
3.4 Estimating Noise Model and Parameter β
In practice, labeler reliability is typically unknown and must be estimated from data. This
can be done using a small set of gold-standard samples, historical labeling records, or online
updates as new labels are acquired. For example, one may maintain an empirical accuracy
estimate for each labeler based on their agreement with gold labels when available, or based
on consistency with the current model predictions on a held-out validation set. These
estimates can be updated sequentially as the active learning process proceeds. Importantly,
the proposed framework does not require perfectly accurate reliability estimates. Because
OLAS updates decisions iteratively over rounds, early estimation errors can be corrected as
more labeled data become available. Moreover, the assignment policy primarily depends on
relative differences in labeler reliability rather than exact values, which reduces sensitivity
to moderate estimation noise. As a result, OLAS is expected to degrade gracefully when
reliability estimates are imperfect, which is consistent with standard practice in adaptive
learning and crowdsourcing settings.
To formulate and solve the proposed model in practice, we need an estimate of the noise
function and the parameter β. To obtain these estimates, we assume access to a dataset
with golden labels, i.e., true underlying labels for the samples. This dataset can be created

for all samples in the golden set, these samples can then be labeled by all available labelers,
allowing us to estimate the underlying noise function by comparing the estimated labels with
the true labels. This problem can be framed as a simple classification task with two features:

In this section, we conduct a series of numerical studies to evaluate the performance of the
proposed OLAS framework. The evaluation proceeds in four parts. Section 4.1 compares
OLAS against seven benchmark methods on five publicly available datasets under two noise
models. Section 4.2 extends this comparison to a larger dataset to assess scalability and
reports a computational cost analysis across all methods and dataset sizes. Section 4.3
moves beyond simulated noise and validates OLAS on real crowdsourced annotations from
a music genre classification dataset. Finally, Section 4.4 applies OLAS to a real-world case
study on labeling automotive warranty claims at Ford Motor Company, demonstrating its
practical effectiveness outside the benchmark setting.

The outcome of the noise functions is compared with a threshold, denoted by α, to
determine whether a label is noisy (incorrect) for a labeled data point. Specifically, for a
data point with entropy measure ej assigned to a labeler with accuracy ai , the label noise
is equal to ϵ(ai , ej ). If ϵ(ai , ej ) ≥ α, the label noise is higher than the threshold, and hence,
the resulting label will be incorrect. Otherwise, the label is correct matching the ground
truth. The parameter α thus serves as an operational decision boundary that converts the
continuous noise output into a binary label correctness outcome for simulation purposes.
In practice, α can be selected based on the acceptable error tolerance of the downstream
application, or estimated from a small gold-standard validation set by identifying the value
that best separates correct from incorrect labels. In our experiments, we fix α = 0.2 across
all datasets and noise models, a value consistent with moderate noise tolerance. To assess
sensitivity, we additionally evaluated α ∈ {0.15, 0.20, 0.25} on the Statlog and Spambase
datasets under Noise Model 1. The relative ranking of all methods remained stable across
α ∈ {0.20, 0.25}, with OLAS consistently outperforming all baselines. At α = 0.15, the
noise threshold becomes excessively strict, converting a large fraction of labels to noise
regardless of labeler quality and creating a degenerate regime that lies outside the practical
operating range of the framework. Within the practically meaningful range of α ≥ 0.20, the
conclusions of our study are robust to the specific threshold choice.

An important observation from Table 3 is that RS+RLA outperforms ES+RLA across all
datasets. This highlights a key failure mode of uncertainty-based sampling under label noise:
entropy sampling prioritizes high-uncertainty samples that are inherently more difficult to
label correctly. When these samples are assigned to labelers at random, the probability of
receiving a noisy label is substantially higher, degrading downstream classifier performance.
Random sampling, by contrast, distributes the labeling budget more uniformly across the
difficulty spectrum, reducing exposure to worst-case noise events when labeler assignment
is not optimized. This observation directly motivates the OLAS design: sample selection
and labeler assignment must be jointly optimized to realize the benefits of uncertainty-based
active learning under label noise. Consistent with this, OLAS combines uncertainty-based
sampling with optimal labeler assignment and outperforms both RS+RLA and ES+RLA
across all evaluated datasets and noise models.

4.3 Validation on Crowdsourced Annotations
The experiments in Sections 4.1 and 4.2 rely on Noise Model 1 and Noise Model 2 to simulate
label noise as a function of labeler accuracy and sample entropy. While this simulation based
approach allows controlled and systematic comparison across methods, it does not by itself
demonstrate that OLAS performs well when labeler reliability and label noise arise from
real human annotators rather than an analytical noise function. We therefore turn to a
dataset with genuine crowdsourced labels and evaluate OLAS and all comparison methods
on the Music Genre Classification dataset of Rodrigues et al. (2013), a publicly available
benchmark built from real crowdsourced annotations collected on Amazon Mechanical Turk.
The dataset consists of 700 thirty second audio clips drawn from ten music genres, each
described by 124 pre extracted tabular audio features, including mel frequency cepstral
coefficients and spectral features. Every clip has a gold standard genre label established
independently of the crowd. In addition, 44 Amazon Mechanical Turk workers each labeled
a subset of the clips, yielding 2,946 individual annotations in total, an average fill rate of
9.6 percent of the full worker by sample annotation matrix. Worker accuracy, measured
against the gold labels, varies widely across the crowd, with a mean of 0.733 and a standard
deviation of 0.242, reflecting the heterogeneous labeler reliability that motivates the OLAS
framework. A held out set of 300 clips, disjoint from the 700 annotated clips, is used as
22

the test set for all reported results. Because the annotation matrix is only 9.6% complete,
worker-sample pairs that were not actually annotated are filled in using the fitted noise
model described in Section 3.4 whenever a method queries them during the active learning
loop; we therefore treat this experiment as a hybrid semi-synthetic evaluation, grounded in
real crowdsourced worker behavior but not a fully observed real-world annotation process.
To validate the estimation pipeline described in Section 3.4 directly on real data, rather
than only describing it, we set aside a gold labeled subset of 140 samples from the 700
annotated clips and use the remaining pool for the active learning experiment. The held
out subset is paired with the corresponding worker annotations to fit the logistic regression
noise function of Section 3.4, using 589 gold subset, annotator pairs. The fitted model
achieves a training accuracy of 0.806 and preserves the monotonicity properties required by
the theory, decreasing in estimated labeler accuracy and increasing in sample entropy. The
same fitted model is used to estimate each of the 44 workers’ individual accuracies directly
from their observed labels; these estimates closely track the true worker accuracies computed
from the full annotation matrix, with an estimated mean accuracy of 0.731 against a true
mean of 0.733. We similarly use the fitted pipeline to select β, obtaining β = 0.332 as the
75th percentile of the fitted noise distribution on the gold subset, and use this estimated,
data driven value of β throughout the experiment below rather than an oracle or hand
tuned choice. Figure 5 shows the resulting fitted surface ϵ̂(a, e), estimated directly from real
Music dataset annotator data, together with the estimated threshold β = 0.332, drawn as a
dashed contour. The fitted surface is visibly decreasing in worker accuracy and increasing in
sample entropy across its full domain, confirming that the monotonicity properties required
by Section 3.4 hold when the pipeline is estimated from real annotator data rather than
assumed analytically.
For the active learning pool of the remaining 560 samples, label outcomes are realized
using a hybrid procedure that combines the real annotation structure with the estimated
noise model: for each queried sample, labeler pair, the estimated worker accuracy and the
fitted noise function are used to determine the probability that the assigned labeler’s response
is corrected, and the observed label is then realized through a Bernoulli draw against that
probability, using the actual worker’s response recorded in the annotation matrix whenever
it is available for that sample. This mechanism allows us to evaluate all eight methods,
including those that require querying labelers who did not happen to annotate a given
sample in the original crowdsourcing collection, while still grounding every probability in
real, estimated worker behavior rather than a fully synthetic noise function.
The experiment follows the same structure as Sections 4.1 and 4.2: T = 10 active
learning cycles, a query budget derived from the same formula used throughout the paper,
and a random forest classifier trained identically across all methods. Results are averaged
over 30 replications. Figure 6 shows the median F1 score for each method across all ten
active learning cycles, and Figure 7 shows the distribution of final cycle F1 scores across the
30 replications.

4.4 Case Study: Automotive Warranty Claims
Having validated OLAS on real crowdsourced annotations in Section 4.3, we now turn to
a second real-world setting in which the labelers are domain experts rather than crowd
workers, and the labeling task is embedded in an industrial production workflow. In this
case study, we address the ACM problem, where daily warranty claims must be categorized
into appropriate classes to assist with root-cause identification. We use warranty claims
data from Ford Motor Company. Due to confidentiality concerns, the data is not publicly
available, and a complete description cannot be provided. The dataset includes categorical
and numerical features explaining the characteristics of the vehicle given in a warranty
claim as well as the details and description of the warranty issue. Each warranty claim
can be categorized into 36 predefined classes. Finding the appropriate label for a given
warranty requires manual labeling, relying on expert technicians who label the claims in
their spare time. Due to time limitations, the team is not able to label all claims. Therefore,
an AL framework is used to identify a subset of claims for labeling in each cycle and to
sequentially update the model. Each technician may have different experience and skill
levels, which results in different labeling accuracy and may lead to incorrect annotations
during the labeling process.
Due to the lack of sufficient historical labeled data with corresponding ground truth, we
simulate labelers with varying levels of accuracy. We employ Noise Model 1, as described
in the previous section, to generate label noise. The experiment is conducted over 30 replications, each consisting of 10 AL cycles. The sampling budget is set to B = 1994 samples
per cycle, with M = 46 labelers each having a capacity of Cap = 44, derived from the
training set size using the same formula applied to the UCI datasets. All eight methods are
trained on a random forest classifier with identical settings in each replication, starting with
16 percent of the training set as the initially labeled dataset.
Figure 8 displays the median F1 score for each approach across 10 cycles. It is observed
that OLAS consistently achieves the highest median F1 score across all cycles, demonstrating
its ability to train a robust model resistant to label noise. Among the baselines, MV achieves
competitive second-place performance by leveraging consensus labels from all available labelers, though it labels only Cap unique samples per cycle compared to the full budget B
labeled by OLAS. RS+OLA and KC+RLA track closely behind MV, while BADGE+RLA
occupies the middle performance band, outperforming RS+RLA, ES+RLA, and ES+OLA
but falling short of OLAS, MV, and RS+OLA throughout most cycles. The consistent
separation between OLAS and all baselines across all 10 cycles demonstrates that the joint
optimization of sample selection and labeler assignment provides sustained benefits over the
entire active learning process.
To better interpret the results, we focus on the box plot in Figure 9, which presents
all 30 replications for the final classifier trained at the end of the last cycle. The results
indicate that OLAS achieves the highest median F1 score at the final cycle, outperforming
all baseline methods. The variation of F1 scores obtained by OLAS is among the smallest of

5 Conclusion
In practice, inaccurate annotation from noisy oracles leads to inaccurate training of machinelearning models. In an active learning framework, highly uncertain data points are desirable
choices for annotation. However, these points are typically associated with higher label noise.
To control noise in the active learning framework, we study the operations within the active
learning procedure. We consider a noise function that depends on labeler accuracy and
sample uncertainty, as quantified by the model’s predictive entropy. It is worth mentioning
27

Figure 9: Box plot of F1 scores over 30 replications for the classifier at the final active
learning cycle on the Ford warranty claim dataset. OLAS achieves the highest
median with low variability across replications.

that accurate estimation of sample uncertainty and labeler accuracy plays an important role
in selecting the best samples and the optimal assignment of samples to labelers. In practice,
measuring the accuracies of labelers might be troublesome, requiring both budget and time.
However, our results demonstrate that achieving accurate assessments for labelers is crucial
and can lead to significant improvements in model performance.
Our experiments show that labeler assignment affects the generated noise, and hence,
using an optimal assignment policy can be effective in reducing the number of incorrect
labels. On the other hand, query selection is another factor that affects label noise. A
formulation was proposed to optimally sample data points at each cycle of AL by maximizing
information gain while bounding the label noise. Experimental results demonstrate the
effectiveness of our sampling approach along with the optimal assignment policy in building
a robust classification model within an AL framework. We note that our framework focuses
on the single-label-per-sample regime, which is appropriate for high-cost or time-sensitive
annotation settings. Extending the model to incorporate redundant labeling and aggregation
strategies under relaxed budget constraints is an interesting direction for future work (see
Section 3).
In our synthetic experiments (Section 4.1), the corruption threshold α and OLAS’s acceptance threshold β are both set to 0.2, reflecting a deployment where the evaluator’s and
the deployer’s noise tolerance coincide. We note that this coupling means OLAS’s acceptance
rule is not fully independent of the corruption rule; decoupling the two in future work, for
example by drawing corruption from a process independent of β, would further strengthen
the comparison.
We also clarify the nature of our evaluation on the Music Genre Classification dataset
in Section 4.3. While it is grounded in real crowdsourced annotations, the underlying an28

notation matrix is only 9.6% complete, and unobserved worker-sample pairs are filled in
using the fitted noise model rather than genuine worker responses. We therefore describe
this experiment as a hybrid semi-synthetic evaluation: real crowdsourced behavior informs
the noise model, but a majority of queried labels during the active learning loop are modelgenerated rather than directly observed. A denser real annotation matrix, or a protocol
restricted to only observed worker-sample pairs, would strengthen this validation and is a
natural direction for future work.
We also note that our noise pipeline (Section 3.4) is validated using training accuracy
and a visual monotonicity check rather than held-out calibration metrics or an enforced
monotonicity constraint; a more rigorous treatment is left to future work.
Although OLAS is presented for pool-based classification with noisy and heterogeneous
labelers, the same optimization structure can be useful in settings where sample selection
and resource assignment must be decided jointly. The method is most appropriate when
labeler reliability varies across annotators, labeling costs are non-negligible, and uncertainty
scores are available. Such settings include medical image annotation, legal document review,
industrial quality inspection, and scientific data curation, where explicitly accounting for
labeler reliability can reduce the risk of assigning highly uncertain samples to low-quality
annotators.
Beyond its operational benefits, systematically assigning samples to labelers based on
estimated reliability raises ethical considerations that deserve explicit discussion. First,
OLAS’s assignment policy, by design, routes the most uncertain samples to the most accurate
labelers, which means less accurate labelers are systematically assigned easier, lower-entropy
samples. Over repeated cycles, this creates differential exposure by construction: some
labelers are never given the opportunity to demonstrate competence on difficult cases, while
others are consistently relied upon for the hardest decisions. In settings where labelers
are also being evaluated, trained, or compensated based on the work they are assigned,
this asymmetry could affect how labeler skill is perceived or rewarded independently of the
quality of their actual judgment.
Second, because OLAS acquires only a single label per sample, there is no redundancy to
catch or correct a mistaken assignment. If the estimated noise function or labeler accuracy
is wrong for a given sample, whether due to poor estimation, a labeler’s reliability changing
over time, or an atypical instance that does not fit the labeler’s usual area of competence,
that error propagates directly into the training set with no opportunity for another labeler’s
judgment to offset it. This risk is more consequential in high-stakes domains such as medical
annotation or safety-critical industrial inspection, where the cost of a single incorrect label
reaching the training set, or downstream model, can be substantial and is not mitigated by
the consensus mechanisms available to multi-label approaches.
Third, the systematic and uneven distribution of easy and hard cases across labelers
raises a fairness concern among the human annotators themselves, distinct from the fairness
of the resulting classifier. A labeler who is judged, formally or informally, on the difficulty
of the cases they are trusted with may be disadvantaged simply by having a lower estimated
accuracy early in the process, regardless of whether that estimate reflects a stable underlying
skill difference or an artifact of limited early data. Because OLAS’s assignment decisions
compound over active learning cycles, an early, possibly noisy, reliability estimate can in29

fluence which labelers continue to receive difficult, and potentially more visible or valued,
work throughout the remainder of the labeling process.
These concerns do not have a complete algorithmic solution within the scope of this
paper, but two properties of the framework and its deployment can partially mitigate them.
First, because labeler accuracy estimates are updated sequentially as more labeled data
become available (Section 3.4), an early and possibly noisy estimate is not permanent: as
evidence accumulates, a labeler’s estimated reliability, and therefore the difficulty of the
samples routed to them, can shift accordingly, which limits how long any single early estimate can dictate a labeler’s assignment history. Second, since OLAS produces an explicit,
auditable record of which sample was assigned to which labeler and why, a deployer can monitor the distribution of sample difficulty across labelers over time and intervene, for example
by periodically reserving a small share of the budget for samples outside a labeler’s usual
assignment profile, without altering the underlying optimization. We view these as practical
safeguards for responsible deployment rather than a resolution of the underlying tension, and
we see a more formal treatment of fairness-aware labeler assignment as a valuable direction
for future work.

Random seeds and reproducibility. The train/test split (below, random_state=42) and
the classifier initializations (random_state=0) use a fixed random state, held constant across
all replications and methods, so these two elements are exactly reproducible. The remaining
stochastic elements of each replication, generating the labeler accuracy pool, sampling the
initial labeled set, selecting query points under random sampling, assigning the specific incorrect class when a label is corrupted, resolving ties in majority voting, and the k-means++
seeding used by BADGE+RLA, draw from the numpy and Python standard-library random
number generators without a fixed seed, and are therefore not deterministically reproducible
across separate executions of the code. We report this explicitly for transparency rather than
describe the pipeline as more reproducible than it is.
This does not affect the validity of the paired significance tests in Appendix C: pairing
there relies on the seven core methods (RS+RLA, RS+OLA, ES+RLA, ES+OLA, OLAS,
MV, KC+RLA) being computed inside the same per-replication function call within a single execution, so that a given replication’s initial labeled set, labeler accuracy pool, and
train/test split are identical across those seven methods, regardless of whether that execution could be exactly reproduced later. BADGE+RLA is run as a separate script execution
and does not share this per-replication state with the other methods, so it is compared using
unpaired tests instead (see Appendix C).
Train/test split. For the five UCI/Covertype datasets, an 80/20 train/test split is used
(shuffle=True, random_state=42), held constant across all replications and methods.
Initialization. For the five UCI/Covertype datasets, the initial labeled set is formed by
stratified sampling of three instances per class (fewer if a class has fewer than three available
instances). For the Ford case study, the initial labeled set is a random 16% of the training
set (Section 4.4).
Preprocessing. Features are standardized (zero mean, unit variance) for use in KC+RLA’s
distance-based sample selection; no additional preprocessing is applied for the other methods
or classifiers.
F1 averaging. Binary F1 is reported for the four two-class UCI datasets (Statlog
Heart, Ionosphere, Connectionist Bench Sonar, Spambase); macro-averaged F1 is reported
for Covertype, the Music dataset, and the Ford case study, all of which have more than two
classes.
Classifier hyperparameters. A random forest classifier (n_estimators=50, random_state=0,
n_jobs=-1; otherwise scikit-learn default settings) is used for all methods on the four
smaller UCI datasets, the Music dataset, and the Ford case study. An extra-trees classifier (n_estimators=20, max_depth=10) is used in place of random forest for Covertype, to
manage computational cost at that scale (Section 4.1).
Hardware and software. All experiments were run on a machine with an Apple Silicon
(arm64) processor, 10 CPU cores, and 16 GB RAM, using Python 3.9.6 with numpy 2.0.2,
scipy 1.13.1, pandas 2.3.3, scikit-learn 1.6.1, and joblib 1.5.3 (used to parallelize replications
across CPU cores).

the noise-estimation pipeline, and the significance tests reported in this paper is publicly available at https://github.com/AhadiPouya/OLAS-Optimal-Labeler-Assignment-and39
