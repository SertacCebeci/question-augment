Benchmark

Abstract
Agentic vision-language models increasingly act through extended interactions, but most evaluations still focus on single-image, single-turn
correctness. We introduce AMIGO (Agentic Multi-Image Grounding
Oracle Benchmark), a long-horizon benchmark for hidden-target identification over galleries of visually similar images. In AMIGO, the oracle
privately selects a target image, and the model must recover it by asking a
sequence of attribute-focused Yes/No questions under a strict protocol that
returns Yes/No/Unsure feedback and penalizes invalid actions with Skip.
This setting stresses (i) question selection under uncertainty, (ii) consistent
constraint tracking across turns, and (iii) fine-grained discrimination as
evidence accumulates. We instantiate AMIGO with the Guess My Preferred Dress task and evaluate open-source VLMs with metrics covering
identification success, evidence verification, efficiency, protocol compliance,
robustness to controlled feedback perturbations, and trajectory-level diagnostics. The benchmarking results show that final-answer accuracy alone
overstates evidence-grounded performance: models can guess correctly
without verification-passing evidence, waste turns through invalid questions, or fail to preserve the upload protocol. Strong AMIGO performance
depends on the combination of visual discrimination, informative question
selection, constraint tracking, efficient stopping, sustained protocol following, and recovery from controlled feedback noise; model scale alone does
not guarantee reliable long-horizon interactive grounding.

Introduction

Most multimodal benchmarks present the relevant visual context upfront and
score a final response. This leaves an important gap: many practical visionlanguage model (VLM) interactions require the model to reduce uncertainty
before answering. In these settings, the model must decide what visual evidence
is missing, ask targeted questions, incorporate user feedback, and delay its final

action until the accumulated constraints are sufficient. These abilities are difficult
to measure in static, short-horizon evaluations [1, 2, 3, 4, 5], where strategy,
memory, and belief revision are mostly invisible.
We study a complementary setting: interactive hidden-target identification
over image collections. A user (or oracle) privately selects a target image from a
gallery of visually similar candidates, and the model must discover the target by
asking constrained questions about observable attributes. Because the target is
hidden from the model but fixed for the oracle, success requires the model to
construct an evidence trail rather than merely recognize a salient object from
a prompt. This formulation surfaces agentic challenges that are often invisible
in single-turn evaluation: choosing questions that efficiently reduce ambiguity,
preserving and applying constraints without drift, and performing cross-image
comparisons that depend on the evolving candidate set rather than any single
image in isolation.
To operationalize this setting, we present AMIGO, an interactive benchmark
centered on a simple but diagnostic loop. The model asks exactly one Yes/No
question per turn, receives Yes/No/Unsure feedback, and iteratively narrows the
feasible candidate set. The interaction is bounded by a fixed question budget,
making efficiency and invalid actions directly comparable across models. If the
model violates protocol rules, the oracle returns Skip, revealing no information.
Because each invalid action consumes a turn while providing no attribute evidence, Skip exposes failures that final-answer metrics miss: models may appear
concise or confident while losing opportunities to gather useful information.
AMIGO’s verification module further separates three outcomes that are often
conflated: incorrect identification, correct final guesses without sufficient supporting evidence, and Verified Correct trajectories that narrow the feasible set
to the hidden target. This distinction is central for evaluating agentic grounding,
where a plausible final answer is less informative than the interaction path that
justifies it.
We implement AMIGO through the Guess My Preferred Dress task, where
each gallery contains 6 to 40+ highly similar dress images. Dresses offer rich
fine-grained variation (e.g., neckline construction, closures, seam placement,
drape, and embellishments), making the task sensitive to careful observation and
long-horizon constraint tracking. The domain is also well suited to controlled
protocol design: highly salient shortcut attributes such as color, pattern, sleeve
length, and garment length can be prohibited, forcing models to rely on subtler
visual evidence.

Related Work

2.1

Multi-image and multi-turn multimodal evaluation

A growing set of benchmarks and datasets extends evaluation beyond singleimage, single-turn QA [3, 2] to multi-image and/or multi-turn settings. Multiimage benchmarks (e.g., MMIU [6], MuirBench [7], mPLUG-Owl3 [8]) probe
cross-image grounding and long visual-context handling. Multi-image instructiontuning datasets (e.g., Mantis [9]) and multi-image multi-turn resources (e.g.,
MMDU [10], MMCR [11]) further study supervision over interleaved images
and dialogue. Separately, multi-turn multimodal conversation benchmarks (e.g.,
MultiVerse [12], ConvBench [13], MMMT-IF [14], MMCoQA [15]) evaluate
contextual coherence and instruction adherence over longer dialogues.
These efforts substantially expand visual context length and dialogue length,
but the model is still typically evaluated as a responder to provided inputs.
AMIGO instead evaluates interactive hidden-target grounding: the model must
choose discriminative visual attributes, update constraints from oracle feedback,
and stop only when the accumulated evidence supports a unique target.

2.2

Multimodal reasoning benchmarks

General multimodal reasoning benchmarks assess whether models can integrate
vision and language for multi-step inference beyond shallow cue matching. For example, EMMA [16] targets “organic” multimodal reasoning across domains, and
MLLM-CompBench [17] emphasizes comparative reasoning under multimodal
inputs. MMMU-Pro [18] further strengthens this line by making evaluation
more resistant to text-only shortcuts and by requiring tighter visual grounding
in challenging multimodal questions.
These benchmarks are valuable for measuring perception-and-inference capability, but the reasoning problem is usually fully specified by the prompt and
visual input. AMIGO makes part of the problem state hidden, so reasoning qual-

3

ity is expressed through the model’s question policy and evidence-accumulation
process, not only through its final answer.

2.3

Interactive Visual Grounding and Target Identification

AMIGO is closely related to interactive settings in which a system must resolve
hidden or underspecified task state through dialogue before making a decision.
Recent work studies this problem in the context of language-model agents: LMRLGym [19] benchmarks reinforcement learning for multi-turn language-model
agents across interactive dialogue and text-game environments, including a
Twenty Questions task that requires strategic questions to identify a hidden entity. This formulation highlights long-horizon decision making, credit assignment,
and action selection that improves eventual task success rather than immediate
response quality. InfoQuest [20] evaluates text-only models that ask clarifying
questions when critical context is hidden. ClariMM [21] extends this idea to
multimodal clarification for underspecified user queries. More broadly, multimodal agent frameworks such as M3Searcher [22] and expert-guided benchmarks
such as MIRAGE [23] follow a “seek-information-then-decide” paradigm, often
with retrieval or tool-mediated evidence acquisition.
A closely related line of earlier work studies visually grounded guessing games
with task-specific dialogue models. GuessWhat [24] introduced a two-player
task in which a questioner identifies a hidden object within an image by asking
a sequence of questions answered by an oracle. Das et al. [25] formulated a
cooperative visual-dialog image-guessing game in which Q-BOT must recover
an unseen image from a candidate lineup through multi-round interaction with
A-BOT, training both dialogue models using deep reinforcement learning. Chattopadhyay et al. [26] adapted this setting into the human–AI GuessWhich game,
evaluating whether a person can identify the secret image by conversing with a
visual-dialog model. Later work developed attentive-memory architectures [27]
and multimodal dialogue-state tracking [28] for this general image-guessing
paradigm.
Interactive visual-grounding benchmarks provide another closely related direction. InViG [29] requires an agent to resolve an ambiguous object reference
by asking clarifying questions and incorporating user feedback. PhotoBook [30]
studies multi-image conversational grounding, repeated reference, and the construction of common ground between interlocutors. Together, these works establish interactive visual identification as a test of grounded language understanding,
dialogue-history modeling, and referent resolution.
Whereas prior guessing-game and interactive-grounding benchmarks focus
primarily on task success, AMIGO makes the interaction policy itself an explicit
object of evaluation. GuessWhat-style tasks identify a hidden object within a
single image, while GuessWhich and related visual-dialog games are typically
framed as recovering or identifying an image through broader free-form dialogue.
InViG focuses on resolving ambiguous object references through clarification,
and PhotoBook studies common-ground construction across repeated visual
references. AMIGO instead fixes a closed gallery of highly similar full-image
4

candidates and restricts the model to one binary, attribute-focused question per
turn. This design exposes failures that are difficult to measure in less constrained
settings: invalid attribute choices, redundant question enumeration, premature
guessing, upload-phase protocol violations, candidate-set stagnation, and correct
final guesses that lack verification-passing evidence. AMIGO scores the path to
the answer directly: whether each question is valid and informative, whether
constraints shrink the candidate set, and whether the final guess is supported
by the accumulated evidence. The resulting logs provide a well-defined action,
observation, and candidate-state record for trajectory analysis.

3

Benchmark

We introduce AMIGO, a benchmark designed to evaluate agentic vision-language
models on hidden-target identification in multi-image, multi-turn settings.
AMIGO operationalizes this paradigm through its primary task, Guess My
Preferred Dress, in which a model must identify a privately selected target
dress from a gallery of highly similar candidates by asking discriminative,
attribute-focused questions under a strict interaction protocol. Beyond this
primary instantiation, the same protocol could extend to other closed-gallery
domains with rich fine-grained variation. Representative examples include
Guess My Preferred Brick-Built Model, Guess My Preferred Wristwatch, and
Guess My Preferred Butterfly Specimen, which respectively emphasize structural,
manufactured, and natural visual evidence; Figure 1 shows example candidate
galleries for these extensions.

3.1

Guess My Preferred Dress

Task. A user uploads one or more batches of dress images that together form
a single gallery, with one image privately selected by the user as the hidden
target. After the signal End of uploading, the model asks at most 20 questions
to narrow the candidate set and finally outputs the 1-indexed position of the
target in the gallery.
Protocol. The interaction is governed by a constrained question-answer protocol. These rules define the protocol-compliance rubric used to classify invalid
actions and interpret Skip feedback:
P1. Question format. Each turn must contain exactly one Yes/No question
about one observable attribute of the target dress.
P2. Oracle response. The question must be answerable with Yes, No, or
Unsure.
P3. Invalid-action feedback. If the model violates any protocol rule, the
oracle returns Skip; a Skip response reveals no attribute information.

5

Figure 1: Candidate galleries for AMIGO task extensions beyond dresses; red
outlines mark selected targets.
P4. Prohibited attributes. The model must not ask about sleeve length,
garment length, piece count, color, prints/patterns, age group, size, shoes,
necklace, hat, bag, background, or the human model.
P5. Redundancy constraint. The model must not enumerate values of
the same attribute type across turns, such as repeatedly cycling through
neckline types (e.g., V-neck, square neck, crewneck).
P6. Index-reference constraint. The model must not reference specific image
indices or inspect images one by one (e.g., “Is it image #1?”).
P7. Stopping rule. The model should not guess until its accumulated constraints narrow the feasible candidate set to exactly one image.
P8. Verification under uncertainty. If constraints appear inconsistent or
uncertain, the model should continue asking verification questions rather
than stopping early.
P9. Final-answer format. When ready, the model must output: My guess
of your favorite dress: #<number>.
P10. Upload phase. The model must not ask questions until the user sends
End of uploading. Echoing the upload-completion phrase is not counted
as a P10 violation unless the output includes a question.

6

P11. Question budget. After the End of uploading signal, the model may
ask at most 20 questions before making a final guess. If the model exhausts
this budget without producing a final guess, the trajectory terminates and
is counted as incorrect. Questions generated during the upload phase are
recorded as premature outputs but do not count toward this budget.
Rationale. The protocol deliberately excludes highly salient cues (e.g., color,
patterns, piece count, sleeve length, and garment length) to encourage reliance
on subtler construction details (e.g., neckline structure, closures, waist shaping,
seam placement, drape, and embellishments). This design emphasizes longhorizon constraint tracking and rewards question sequences that are informative,
non-redundant, and robust to uncertainty.
Example interactions. The example trajectories in Figure 2 illustrate how
visually similar candidates can lead to different outcomes under the same protocol.
The failed trace in Figure 2a contains useful construction-focused questions but
still leaves confusable candidates unresolved, whereas the successful trace in

3.2

Dataset collection

Source and curation. We collect dress images from an online retailer’s fashion
catalog and deduplicate them to ensure each product is unique. Attribute
metadata extracted from the retailer’s historical product database is often
missing, defined inconsistently across brands, and sometimes contains erroneous
or irrelevant values. We therefore prioritize visually verifiable attributes and
build a semi-automatic pipeline to produce standardized labels at scale.
Semi-automatic attribute labeling. Our pipeline has three stages: (1)
Attribute discovery and normalization: we aggregate attribute values from
catalog annotations and use multiple LLMs to normalize synonyms and naming
variants, compile a comprehensive attribute value list, and expand it with as
many visually grounded alternatives as possible; (2) Binary question template
construction: for each normalized attribute value, we generate multiple binary
(Yes/No) question templates (e.g., “Does the dress have a wrap-style front?”,
“Does the dress have a wrap-front design?”, “Does the dress feature a wrapstyle front?”); (3) VLM-based labeling: we apply multiple VLMs to answer
these templates for each image, yielding per-image attribute labels. Figure 3
summarizes this semi-automatic labeling pipeline.
Ensemble VLM labeling and quality control. Given budget constraints,
we adopt open-source VLMs for automated labeling. To improve labeling
reliability, we ensemble multiple VLMs (Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-InstructFP8 [31], Intern-S1 [32], GLM-4.5V [33]) and combine their predictions

Gallery generation and difficulty control. For each target image A, we

Sample image galleries

the intended ambiguity of the task: candidates share coarse visual cues such as

Scale and release. We collect 4,880 unique dress images and use five similarity
thresholds, τ ∈ {0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8}, to control gallery difficulty. Raising τ
keeps only distractors with stronger attribute overlap, so the candidate pool
generally contracts while visual confusability increases; lower thresholds admit
larger, more varied galleries. We obtain 587 galleries at τ = 0.8 because this
strict threshold makes it difficult to find enough highly similar distractors, and we
release 1,000 examples for each of τ = 0.3 through τ = 0.6, yielding 4,587 total
benchmark examples in anc/data.json under the CC-BY-4.0 license. Each
example contains an ID, a target image URL, and a candidate gallery that
includes the target and its similar distractors.

Benchmark framework

Benchmark components. Our framework comprises four modules (Figure 7):
(i) a benchmark model (the evaluated VLM) that generates questions and
13

produces a final guess, (ii) a question-violation detector (LLM-based) that
enforces the protocol, flags invalid questions with Skip, and provides standardized
feedback on protocol adherence, (iii) a user/oracle simulator (VLM agent)
that answers valid questions with Yes/No/Unsure, and (iv) a verification
module that maintains and audits the feasible candidate set implied by the
dialogue.
Given the uploaded gallery and dialogue history, the benchmark model
outputs either the next question or a terminal guess.
To make question-violation detector and oracle feedback more robust, each
newly generated question is paraphrased into two additional variants. Feedback
from both the question-violation detector and the oracle is then determined by
majority vote across the three question variants.
The violation detector checks each question against the benchmark constraints.
It first applies deterministic P1 format checks for empty turns, non-question
responses, multi-question turns, and questions that cannot be answered with
Yes/No/Unsure. It then applies two few-shot LLM-based checks: one flags
prohibited-attribute questions and image-index references, and the other flags
repeated enumeration of an attribute type that has already appeared in previous
valid turns. The prompts for these two checks are provided in the Appendix
(Figures 19 and 20). Any violation returns Skip with no oracle information. We
record the number (and rate) of Skip responses per trajectory as the primary
signal of protocol compliance. Implementing the violation detector as a separate
module ensures that all models receive consistent, standardized feedback on
protocol adherence regardless of their internal architecture or reasoning style.
The user/oracle simulator answers valid questions with Yes/No/Unsure.
Verification module (state tracking and consistency checking). This
separate evaluation module verifies whether a correct final guess is supported by
a coherent process that systematically narrows and ranks the candidate space.
The verification module is intentionally model-relative: it uses the same evaluated
benchmark model to obtain candidate-level responses so that verification reflects
the model’s own visual judgments and reasoning logic, rather than those of
an external verifier. Accordingly, a verification pass measures model-relative
evidence consistency: whether the evaluated model’s candidate-level judgments
support the target as the unique best candidate under the accumulated dialogue
evidence. It should not be interpreted as model-independent proof of visual
correctness.
After each valid question–answer pair, the verification module converts the
interaction into an explicit constraint and applies it to the gallery to update the
feasible candidate set, defined as the subset of images that remain consistent
with all constraints observed so far. Let G denote the full gallery of candidate
images, and let Ft ⊆ G denote the feasible candidate set after the t-th valid
question–answer pair, with F0 = G.

This module serves three purposes: (1) Evidence verification: determining
whether the dialogue makes the target the unique highest-scoring candidate under
the evaluated model’s candidate-level judgments; (2) Trajectory auditing:

checking: detecting contradictions such as an empty feasible candidate set or
conflicting constraints, thereby supporting trajectory-level analysis of whether
the benchmark model recovers conservatively rather than guessing prematurely.
Importantly, the feasible candidate set is updated only after valid, non-Skip
question–answer pairs that eliminate at least one candidate. This design cleanly
separates protocol violations from evidence-based candidate elimination.

Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate models along four complementary axes. Unless noted otherwise, all
constraint application and candidate-set updates are computed by the verification module using only valid (non-Skip) question-answer pairs.
• Identification accuracy: Unverified Overall, Verified Correct,
Random-Guess Correct. Unverified Overall accuracy measures whether
the model’s final guess matches the hidden target. Trajectories that exhaust
16

the 20-question budget without a final guess are treated as incorrect
terminal outcomes. Here, Unverified Overall refers to this final-guess
correctness rate and is partitioned into two mutually exclusive correctoutcome categories: Unverified Overall = Verified Correct + RandomGuess Correct. Verified Correct accuracy excludes random-but-correct
guesses: a trajectory is counted as verified-successful only if (i) the final
guess is correct and (ii) the verification module produces the hidden target
as the unique highest-scoring candidate. This metric captures whether the
model’s own candidate-level judgments support its correct final answer
with a unique top-ranked target. Random-Guess Correct accuracy counts
trajectories where the final guess is correct but the trajectory fails the
verification module because the target is not the unique highest-scoring
remaining candidate. This category captures correctness without sufficient
model-relative, verification-passing evidence.
• Interaction efficiency. We measure interaction cost as the number
of total turns (including Skip) after the End of uploading signal and
before the final guess. We report efficiency separately for Verified Correct,
Random-Guess Correct, incorrect, and overall trajectories to distinguish
evidence-driven efficiency from premature or unproductive stopping.
• Protocol compliance. We quantify instruction following with (i) the
average Skip rate and (ii) the premature-output rate before the End of
uploading signal. Non-question acknowledgments during the upload phase
are not counted as premature outputs. Lower average Skip rates and zero
premature outputs reflect stronger adherence; repeated violations reflect
failures to recover to valid questioning.
• Robustness under controlled noisy feedback. Recovery rate measures
the fraction of perturbed successful trajectories in which the model still
produces the correct final guess. It is conditional on clean verified success,
not all attempted dialogues.

6.6

Robustness under controlled noisy feedback

Robustness under controlled noisy feedback evaluates whether an interactive
model can recover when one piece of oracle feedback is deliberately corrupted
while the rest of the evidence trail remains unchanged. This matters because
AMIGO questions often target fine-grained or subjective visual attributes, where
an oracle response may occasionally be locally ambiguous, inconsistent with later
evidence, or simply wrong. A reliable agent should not treat every individual
response as irrevocable; it should maintain competing hypotheses, use later
questions to repair the candidate set, and still converge when the dialogue
contains a small amount of controlled noise.
We instantiate the noisy feedback with a controlled perturbation: one random
Yes/No oracle response is flipped in each eligible trajectory, the earlier dialogue
prefix is preserved, and the model continues from the contradictory feedback.
We repeat five trials per setting. Unsure and Skip responses are not flipped,
isolating injected binary contradiction rather than protocol noise.
Table 11 reports threshold-level recovery rates and the macro-average recovery
rate under this noisy-feedback setting. The macro-average is the arithmetic
mean of the per-threshold recovery rates. Gemma4-31B is strongest by macro
average, with a 59.1% macro-average recovery rate. Qwen3-VL-235B-A22BInstruct-FP8 has the second-highest macro-average recovery rate at 51.5%,
followed by Qwen3.5-397B-A17B-FP8 at 37.4%, Gemma4-12B at 33.8%,
and Step3-VL-10B at 29.9%.
The per-threshold recovery rate can vary substantially for each model.
Gemma4-31B remains consistently high across thresholds, staying within
54.5–64.6%, which indicates stable repair rather than a single favorable setting.
Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct-FP8 improves from 49.6% at τ = 0.3 to
58.7% at τ = 0.6 but drops to 43.0% at τ = 0.8, where it falls slightly below
Step3-VL-10B. Qwen3.5-397B-A17B-FP8 and Gemma4-12B generally decline as τ increases. Step3-VL-10B shows a different pattern: weak recovery
at low-τ , larger-gallery settings (18.0% at τ = 0.3 and 26.7% at τ = 0.4) pulls
down its macro average, even though its best recovery occurs in the highest-τ ,
smaller-gallery setting (45.3% at τ = 0.8).

Trajectory Structure for Reinforcement Learning

AMIGO also collects interaction trajectories during evaluation for future multimodal reinforcement learning and preference optimization studies. Each interaction naturally defines a sequential decision process: the state includes the image
gallery, dialogue history, and accumulated constraints; the action is a protocolconstrained attribute question or a terminal guess; and the observation is the
oracle response, including Yes, No, Unsure, or Skip. Because the evaluation
records turn-level outcomes and the verification module tracks candidate-set
dynamics, AMIGO captures both terminal outcomes and intermediate reasoning
behavior.
This structure could enable dense, automatically computable reward signals.
Invalid questions can receive negative rewards through Skip; valid questions can
be rewarded according to candidate-set reduction, estimated information gain,
or progress toward a singleton feasible set; and terminal rewards can be assigned
when the model correctly identifies the target with verification-passing evidence.
These signals directly reflect the benchmark’s core requirements: asking valid
discriminative questions, preserving constraints across turns, and stopping only
when the dialogue supports a unique target.
The same structure could support several learning studies. Once rewards

33

are defined and the collected data provide sufficient behavioral coverage, completed traces could support offline reinforcement-learning datasets. Preference
optimization can compare alternative questions for the same candidate state and
favor questions that are valid, non-redundant, and more informative. These uses
build on the state, action, observation, and verification structure.

8

Conclusion

AMIGO reframes multi-image evaluation as an interactive grounding problem
rather than a final-answer recognition problem. By requiring models to identify
a hidden target through constrained Yes/No questions, AMIGO makes question
selection, constraint tracking, evidence accumulation, stopping behavior, protocol
compliance, and feedback recovery directly measurable. The verification module
is central to this framing: it separates correct final guesses from trajectories
whose accumulated evidence uniquely supports the target, exposing failures that
would be hidden by Unverified Overall Accuracy alone.
The experiments show that current open-source VLMs remain far from reliable long-horizon grounding agents. Strong performance does not follow from
model scale or longer reasoning alone; enabling thinking mode does not necessarily increase AMIGO performance unless it improves valid question selection,
evidence use, and stopping decisions. Reliable performance instead requires
the combination of fine-grained visual discrimination, effective questioning and
stopping, constraint maintenance, protocol adherence, and recovery from controlled noisy feedback. Across these axes, AMIGO exposes qualitatively different
failure modes: models may guess correctly without verification-passing evidence,
stop early in low-threshold large-gallery failures, spend many turns on invalid or
redundant questions, exhaust the question budget without converging, violate
the upload-completion boundary, or fail to recover after a single contradictory
feedback signal.
The reliability audits support the benchmark as a measurement tool: oracle
agreement remains high across both a cross-backbone check and a fashion-expert
audit, and the question-violation detector audit indicates that post-upload Skip
rates are conservative estimates of invalid-action frequency. Together with
the released benchmark data, these diagnostics provide a concrete substrate
for future work on protocol-aware multimodal agents. The same protocol can
also be instantiated in the other closed-gallery domains illustrated in Figure 1.
Natural next steps are to broaden the task domains, evaluate closed-source
VLMs, and use the collected trajectories for reinforcement learning or preference
optimization targeted at valid questioning, belief maintenance, conservative
stopping, and recovery from noisy feedback.

Failure Cases

The aggregate results show that current VLMs fail for different reasons: some stop
before collecting verification-passing evidence, some continue asking questions but
repeatedly violate the protocol, and some struggle to integrate valid constraints
into a unique target. We illustrate these behaviors with representative trajectories.
All examples use the same task prompt and protocol; images are uploaded in
batches of five, and the hidden target is selected from the resulting gallery.

A.1

Benchmark Task Prompt

Prompt
Task: I will upload several batches of dress options. Please guess which one is my
favorite dress and provide its index in the image gallery (starting index: 1).
Constraints:
1. Ask questions to gather attribute information about the target dress.
2. Ask exactly one Yes/No question per turn about one distinguishable feature. I
will respond with Yes, No, Unsure (if not confident), or Skip (if you violate the
rules).
3. If you receive Skip, your latest question was invalid and provides no information.
4. Do not ask about sleeve length, garment length, piece count, color, pattern/prints, age group, size, shoes, necklace, hat, bag, background, or the
human model.
5. Do not enumerate attribute values across turns for the same attribute type (e.g.,
square neck, V-neck, crew neck).
6. Do not inspect images one-by-one or reference indices (e.g., “is your favorite
dress the first image?”).
7. Do not guess until you have narrowed the target down to one image.
8. If your confidence is low or you find contradictions, keep asking verification
questions.
9. When ready, output: My guess of your favorite dress: #<number>.
10. Only start generating questions after you receive End of uploading.
11. You may ask at most 20 questions to collect information.

when the model cannot maintain the protocol or synthesize constraints effectively.

print question and then enters a Protocol 5 loop of compound questions that
recombine already-confirmed attributes. These invalid turns return Skip, and
the model exhausts all 20 turns without a final answer. This failure highlights a
distinct reasoning problem: the model retains some local facts from the dialogue,
but fails to use them as a state representation for candidate-set reduction.

before an incorrect guess. Qwen3.5-397B-A17B-FP8 is more persistent, but
often spends its longer traces on Protocol 4 prohibited-attribute violations and
Protocol 5 redundant enumeration. Together, the examples show why AMIGO
evaluates more than final correctness: successful performance requires sustained
protocol compliance, discriminative question selection, constraint tracking, and
evidence-grounded stopping.

B.3

Duplicate Attribute Detection Prompt

Prompt
You are given:
- New question (may ask about one OR multiple dress attributes)
- History questions (a numbered list of previous attribute
questions)
Task:
1) Determine whether ANY fine-grained attribute type mentioned
in the New question has already appeared in the History
questions.
2) Output format:
- If there is a match: output exactly "Yes | <ATTRIBUTE_TYPE>"
where <ATTRIBUTE_TYPE> is ONE matched fine-grained
attribute type.
- If there is no match: output exactly "No"
No other text.
Definition: fine-grained attribute type
A fine-grained attribute type is the most specific attribute
label you can infer from a question.
It is determined by:
(A) the base item being described (e.g., neckline, hem,
skirt, bow tie, zipper, waist)
AND
(B) the specific aspect/property being asked about (e.g.,
shape/type, embellishment, location, presence,
decoration/finish, silhouette, construction, placement)
AND sometimes
(C) a required subtype when the aspect is itself multi-type
(e.g., tiered vs pleated).

46

Matching rule (strict):
- Two questions match ONLY if they ask about the SAME
fine-grained attribute type.
- Do NOT match based on shared keywords alone.
- If you cannot confidently assign or match a fine-grained
attribute type, treat it as NOT a match.
Value rule:
- Different VALUES match ONLY when they are values of the SAME
fine-grained attribute type.
- Some areas require value-level granularity; treat each named
style/construction as its OWN fine-grained type.
Process:
1) Extract all fine-grained attribute types mentioned in the
New question.
2) Extract all fine-grained attribute types mentioned in the
History questions.
3) If ANY overlap exists -> match.
4) If multiple overlaps exist, choose ONE matched attribute
type (any one is fine).
Fine-grained distinctions (non-exhaustive):
- NECKLINE_TYPE (V-neck, square neck, round neck, sweetheart,
etc.)
!= NECKLINE_EMBELLISHMENT (ruffled neckline, lace-trim
neckline, beaded neckline, etc.)
- Bow tie LOCATION ("bow tie at the waist/shoulder")
!= Bow tie PRESENCE/DESIGN ("has bow tie design")
- Zipper LOCATION ("zipper at the back")
!= CLOSURE TYPE ("zipper closure" vs "button closure")
- Hem SHAPE/HEIGHT ("high-low hemline", "asymmetrical hemline")
!= Hem FINISH/DECORATION ("ruffled hem")
- Style OCCASION/USE ("casual", "formal", "party")
!= named CUT/STYLES (wrap-style, fit-and-flare, shift, tunic)
- Named dress CUT/STYLES are each their OWN fine-grained type
(no merging):
* WRAP_STYLE != FIT_AND_FLARE_STYLE != SHIFT_STYLE
!= TUNIC_STYLE
IMPORTANT: Fine-grained skirt rule (do-not-merge)
- "Skirt silhouette" and "skirt construction" are DIFFERENT
fine-grained attribute types.
- Do NOT match a skirt silhouette question with a skirt
construction question.
Skirt silhouette (one fine-grained type: SKIRT_SILHOUETTE):
- Values include: flared skirt, pencil skirt, A-line skirt,
mermaid skirt, etc.

47

- Different silhouette values still match each other.
Skirt construction (fine-grained types, NOT merged):
- Each construction term is its OWN fine-grained attribute type:
* TIERED_SKIRT
* PLEATED_SKIRT
* GATHERED_SKIRT
(and similar)
- Different construction terms do NOT match each other.
Waist fine-grained distinction (do-not-merge)
- WAISTLINE_PLACEMENT/SEAM_TYPE != WAIST_DEFINITION/SHAPING
* WAISTLINE_PLACEMENT/SEAM_TYPE asks WHERE the waistline/seam
sits (empire waist, drop waist, etc.).
* WAIST_DEFINITION/SHAPING asks whether the waist is
emphasized (cinched/defined/nipped-in/belted look).
- These do NOT match each other.
General fine-grained rules (do-not-merge):
1) PRESENCE vs DETAIL
- For any feature X, questions about whether X exists are a
different fine-grained attribute type from questions about
X's subtype/style/shape/material/location.
- Therefore: X_PRESENCE ("Does it have X?") does NOT match
X_DETAIL ("Is X pointed/ruffled/V-shaped/at the waist/etc.?").
Examples (Output = No):
*New: "Does the dress have a collar?"*
*History: 1) "Is the collar pointed style?"*
*Output: No*
*New: "Does your favorite dress feature an asymmetric hemline?"*
*History: 1) "Is there ruffled detailing on the hemline?"*
*Output: No*
2) STRUCTURE/SHAPE vs EMBELLISHMENT/DECORATION
- For any garment area A (neckline/hem/skirt/etc.), questions
about A's structural shape/type are a different fine-grained
attribute type from questions about A's embellishment/trim/
decoration.
- Therefore: A_TYPE/SHAPE (e.g., V-neck, square neck, high-low
hemline) does NOT match A_EMBELLISHMENT (e.g., ruffled
neckline, lace-trim neckline, ruffled hem).
Examples (Output = No):
*New: "Does the dress have a V-neckline?"*
*History: 1) "Does the dress have a ruffled neckline?"*
*Output: No*
3) Exact repeat vs new-detail exception:
- Output "Yes" when the New question repeats an alreadydiscussed fine-grained attribute type/value and adds no new

48

fine-grained detail.
- Output "Yes" when the New question asks the same fine-grained
attribute type again with a different value.
- Output "No" only when the New question repeats an exact or
nearly exact old attribute value as context AND adds a new
fine-grained modifier/detail including decoration, trim, seam
detail, placement detail, material, count, measurement, or
other specification details.
- If the added modifier is only a refinement of the same
fine-grained attribute type, output "Yes".
Examples:
*New: "Does the dress have a tiered skirt?"*
*History: 1) "Does the dress have a tiered skirt?"*
*Output: Yes*
*New: "Does the dress have a tiered skirt with ruffle details
on the seams?"*
*History: 1) "Does the dress have a tiered skirt?"*
*Output: No*
*New: "Does the dress have a V-neckline with lace trim?"*
*History: 1) "Does the dress have a V-neckline?"*
*Output: No*
*New: "Does the tiered skirt have 2 tiers?"*
*History: 1) "Does the dress have a tiered skirt?"*
*Output: No*
HIGH PRIORITY skirt rule (do-not-merge):
- Skirt SILHOUETTE/SHAPE and skirt CONSTRUCTION/LAYERING are
different fine-grained attribute types.
- "flared skirt" is SKIRT_SILHOUETTE (shape).
- "tiered skirt" is TIERED_SKIRT (construction/layering) and
must NOT be treated as a silhouette.
- Therefore: "flared skirt" does NOT match "tiered skirt".
Examples:
*New: "Does the dress have a flared skirt?"*
*History: 1) "Does the dress have a tiered skirt?"*
*Output: No*
*New: "Does the dress have a flared skirt?"*
*History: 1) "Does the dress have a pencil skirt?"*
*Output: Yes*
