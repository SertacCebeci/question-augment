Abstract
Analyzing decision problems under uncertainty commonly relies on idealizing assumptions about
the describability of the world, with the most prominent examples being the closed world and the
small world assumption. Most assumptions are operationalized by introducing states of the world,
conditional on which the decision situation can be analyzed without any remaining uncertainty.
Conversely, most classical decision-theoretic approaches are not applicable if the states of the world
are inaccessible. We propose a decision model that retains the appeal and simplicity of the original theory, but completely overcomes the need to specify the states of the world explicitly. The
main idea of our approach is to address decision problems in a radically empirical way: instead
of specifying states and consequences prior to the decision analysis, we only assume a protocol of
observed act–consequence pairs as model primitives. We show how optimality in such empirical
decision problems can be addressed by using protocol-based empirical choice functions and discuss
three approaches for deriving inferential guarantees: (I) consistent statistical estimation of choice
sets, (II) consistent statistical testing of choice functions with robustness guarantees, and (III)
direct inference for empirical choice functions using credal sets. We illustrate our theory with a
proof-of-concept application comparing different prompting strategies in generative AI models.
Keywords: decision theory; decision making under uncertainty; states of the world; credal set;
choice function; non-parametric statistics; robust testing; small world assumption; closed world
assumption; direct inference; protocol of act-consequence pairs; contamination models

1. Introduction
This article deals with empirical decision problems (or shorthand: EDPs). By this term, we refer
to decision problems under (generalized) uncertainty whose possible states of the world are not a
priori available to the decision maker, but can only be made accessible indirectly via appropriate
sampling strategies (e.g., by collecting the consequences different decisions lead to in past situations
that can be judged to be similar enough to the present one). In other words, rather than assuming to
know all the states of the world and (equally restrictive) all the consequences that different decisions
under a given state would theoretically lead to, our model primitive is a so-called protocol, i.e., an
(observed) set of act-consequence pairs, each specifying what consequence a certain decision led to

1.1. Motivation and Background
The following questions come to mind: Why is a modification of the classical framework of decision
theory under uncertainty2 interesting or even necessary? Hasn’t the classical approach achieved considerable success and produced a mathematically and philosophically beautiful theory? Of course,
this is the case, and this paper does not seek to cast any doubt on that whatsoever. However, we
believe that there are (many) relevant decision-making situations that simply cannot be analyzed
within the traditional framework. Specifically, we will argue that by transitioning from classical
decision problems to empirical ones, we are able to overcome two major challenges of classical decision theory: The small world problem referring to the (potentially) arbitrary set granularity of the
state space under consideration (and the dependence of an optimal decision on this setting), and
the closed world problem, referring directly to the practical lack of ability of specifying an suitable
state space. Even more, we will argue that specifying an appropriate state space is very hard in
many (if not most) practical decision situations, emphasizing that the problems just mentioned are
far from being only theoretical and, thereby, making the possibility to overcome them even more
attractive. Or, in the words of the decision theory pioneer Edi Karni, we can attest that “[...] careful
examination of the concept of state of the world reveals that the depiction of the relevant state-space
is often unintuitive and too complex to be compatible with the decision makers’ perception of choice
problems” (Karni, 2006, p. 325).
1.2. Related Work
Given the generality and importance of the problem of inaccessible state spaces, it hardly surprises
that there can be found many different approaches for tackling it in the literature. In our eyes, the
following among these approaches seem particularly prominent and relevant for our discussion: The
first is Gilboa and Schmeidler’s case-based approach to decision making (see Gilboa and Schmeidler
(1995, 2001); Billot et al. (2008)), where decisions – rather than comparing their expected utilities
– are evaluated by a weighted sum of the utilities they have produced in similar problems in the
agent’s memory. Similarity here is measured by a function assigning a real-valued score to any pair
of problems. While the aforementioned works focus in particular on the axiomatic characterization
of this alternative decision theory, subsequent works such as Ha and Haddawy (1998); Bleichrodt
et al. (2017) have shown how similarity measures can be elicited and thus built a bridge to practical
applications such as Guerdjikova (2004); Lovallo et al. (2012); Grosskopf et al. (2015).

From a technical point of view, the mathematical machinery here is mainly based on the theory of

interesting parallels to literature on human-AI alignment, where the question also arises as to how
well a decision-making algorithm provided by an AI can fulfill the goals articulated by a human.
Some recent works in this directions are Yang et al. (2024); Rodemann et al. (2025).
A third approach is Blume, Easley, and Halpern’s constructive approach to decision making (see
Blume et al. (2021)), where (essentially) decisions (or acts) are replaced by syntactic programs (simple if-else rules) and states are replaced by propositions about the world. These model primitives
are then used to derive (or construct) acts, states, and consequences corresponding to a classic
Savageian setting. More precisely, the authors prove here an existence result stating that if the
preferences of the decision-maker over these programs satisfy certain axioms, then the information
from these preferences can be used to construct a classic decision problem.
Finally, there is a number of technically deep papers that describe rational decision-making axiomatically without having to specify a state space or find ways to do it in a justified manner: In
Dekel et al. (2001), the authors provide an axiomatic characterization of preference structures that
enables the construction of an (essentially) unique subjective state space. The paper by Ghirardato
(2001) introduces a way to formally represent situations where the decision maker cannot list all
possible states, and show how such situations under unforeseen contingencies can be tackled by
using Choquet expected utility rather than classical one. Moreover, while Machina (2003) gives
a critical reflection of the role of states of the world in decision theory and surveys and discusses
different approaches avoiding the use of states, the papers Karni (2006) and Ahn (2008) manage
to axiomatically recover subjective expected utility theory and maximin expected utility theory,
respectively, without ever referring to a state space at all. Finally, in Karni (2022) acts (and their
respective outcomes) are interpreted as experiments that are used to provide evidence about the
validity of different competing theories about which act would lead to which outcome. States are
replaced by such theories in this model.
Interestingly, what almost all of these works have in common is that they propose alternative models
to Savage’s classic model, rather than providing solutions to the challenges within this framework.
More precisely, all these models address the problem of the non-specifiable state space by replacing
it with a constructive, more easily specifiable component, thereby, potentially losing what made
Savage’s framework of comparing acts mapping states to consequences so appealing at first. Against
this background, we should also explicitly stress that we, in particular, aim to maintain the prescriptive character of Savage’s theory, informing us about how a rational agent ought to decide.
Thus, our notion of an empirical decision theory must not be confused with concepts of descriptive
decision theory that, in the tradition of Simon (1955) and Tversky and Kahneman (1974), collect
and systematize empirical evidence on how decisions with their heuristics and biases are concretely
made under bounded rationality.

1.3. Contribution and Outline
In contrast to the alternatives decision theoretic approaches just discussed, we want to take a path
here that retains the appeal and simplicity of the original theory, but avoids the necessity to specify
the states of the world explicitly. The main idea of our approach is to address decision problems
in a radically empirical way: instead of specifying states and consequences a priori to the decision
analysis, we assume our decision problem to consist only of observable components of the form
act Ñ consequence, i.e., of a protocol of act-consequence-pairs storing the information which act
led to which consequence in previous trials of choosing the corresponding act (see Definition 2 of
Section 4). Based on these protocols, we go on defining so-called empirical choice functions (see
Definition 3 of Section 4). Like classical choice functions, these assign to each potential input a
set of decisions that appear to be appropriate choices under that input. The difference lies in
the domain: While classical choice functions operate on a set system over the acts (i.e., require
knowledge of the state space), empirical choice functions refer solely to the available protocol. No
explicit knowledge of the states is required. Note that, while we do need to assume the existence
of a (latent) state space and that every decision option can be identified with a latent act on this
space, our assumption is nevertheless substantially weaker than that of the classical theory, which
presumes that these can be made explicit. In plain terms, our assumption essentially is that the
decision maker believes in a non-deterministic process generating the states of the world.
So, how should the decision sets select by an empirical choice function be interpreted? We provide
two answers: First, we show under which conditions an empirical choice function can converge
towards its population variant (i.e., the choice function whose empirical analog it models). Under
these conditions, an empirical choice function can then be interpreted as a consistent statistical estimator for its population analog (see Theorem 1 of Section 5). Second, we show how to construct
an appropriate statistical test for checking whether some act is contained in the choice set of the
population variant of an empirical choice function under investigation (see Theorem 2 of Section 6).
From this angle, the choice sets of the underlying empirical choice function can then be interpreted
as being statistically significant candidates for the true choice sets under some pre-specified error
level. Further, we show how the proposed statistical test can be modified to remain valid even under
slight deviations from its main underlying assumption, namely the i.i.d. structure of the observed
consequences for each action description belonging to the respective protocol (see Theorem 3 of
Section 6). In Section 7, we propose an alternative way to obtain inferential guarantees for solu4

tions of empirical decision problems that we term direct credal inference. We connect this to the
results obtained in the classical inferential setting (see Corollary 1 of Section 7). In Section 8, we
illustrate our theory with a proof-of-concept application comparing different prompting strategies
in generative AI models. To make a discussion of EDPs and corresponding approaches for their
solution possible, we begin by outlining the classical decision-theoretic setting on which we base
this paper (Section 2) and by providing some details of its limitation (Section 3).
2. The Classical Setting: Decision Problems with Known State Spaces
We now give a short overview of the concepts from decision making with known state spaces which
are relevant for building our theory in the later parts of this article. This section is in parts
influenced by the presentation in Jansen (2018, 2025).
2.1. Decision Making under Uncertainty
The basic formalism of decision making under uncertainty is as simple as it is expressive: the agent
is asked to choose between different available acts X from a known set of acts G. However, the
consequence of choosing an act X is not (necessarily) deterministic, but rather depends on which
state of the world from a known set S of such states turns out to be the true one. Formally, each
act is a mapping X : S Ñ C, where C is the set of all possible consequences. The decision problem
G is then some fixed subset of the set of all possible acts, i.e., the set C S “ tX : S Ñ Cu.
The agent’s goal is to select an optimal (set of) act(s) or, more general, a set of acts for which there
is no evidence that one is in some sense superior to the others. This selection process is commonly
formalized by a choice function ch : 2G Ñ 2G satisfying chpDq Ď D for all D P 2G .3 The sets
chpDq are called choice sets and have a slightly different interpretation depending on the quality
of the information used to construct the choice function: The strong view interprets chpDq as the
set of optimal acts from D. The weak view, on the other hand, interprets chpDq as the set of acts
from D that cannot be rejected based on the information. Usually in this paper we will rather be
concerned with only weakly interpretable choice functions.

3. Small Worlds, Closed Worlds and the Definition of States
As already briefly foreshadowed in the introduction and as is also directly evident by inspecting
the basic decision-theoretic concepts recalled in the previous section, the analysis (and even the
mere definition) of decision problems under uncertainty makes use of (and therefore heavily depends
on) idealizing assumptions about the describability of the world, i.e. about the availability and the
structure of the states of the world S as well as the consequences C. A classic decision problem
cannot be formally described or solved unless the quality of the decisions can be clearly analyzed
conditional on some fixed and well-defined set of hypothetical worlds. Even more, it must also be
possible to clearly state which decision would lead to which consequence in each of these hypothetical worlds. This implies in particular that a decision needs to be completely identifiable with the
random variable describing it on the set of states of the world. Conversely, of course, this means
7

that classical decision-theoretical approaches run into severe trouble if the set of states of nature in
the specific problem under consideration is inaccessible (or not appropriately approximizable) and,
therefore, these implicit assumptions are violated.
We want to distinguish between two (closely related) types of such assumptions:
Closed World Assumption (CWA): It is assumed that a priori – in the formalization step of
the problem – an exhaustive, informative, and mutually exclusive list of all states relevant for the
problem can be given and that the consequence that an act yields under each of the states is clearly
specifiable. Specifically, if a state is not contained in the list, it is assumed to be impossible to occur
and is therefore irrelevant for the decision problem.
Note that CWA originates from the theory of data bases (Reiter, 1978), where it (essentially) states
that what is currently not known about a system is assumed to be false. Beyond its relevance for
classical decision theory (see Gilboa (2009, Chapter 12) for a nice discussion), it is also judged to
play a crucial role in more advanced areas like Markov Decision Processes (e.g., Boutilier et al.
(1995); Dearden and Boutilier (1997)) and, therefore, also appears to be of immense practical relevance for, e.g., modern machine learning.
Small World Assumption (SWA): It is assumed that there is a natural granularity of the states
of the world for the considered decision problem. Any finer or coarser partition of the states of the
world is judged to be accepted as unnatural. Specifically, no matter how complex the true processes
driving the dynamics of the system under consideration, it is assumed that these dynamics can be
precisely enough captured by a moderately large list of states of the world with an intuitive interpretation.
Note that SWA is also closely related (but not identical) to the problem of act-state dependence: In
Savage’s original model (Savage, 1954) for decision making under uncertainty (which, in essence, coincides with the one recalled in Section 2.1), the acts are assumed (by construction) to not influence
the availability and the probability of the states of nature. This, too, is an (indirect) assumption
about the ease with which the world can be described in a way that resolves all uncertainty and,
therefore, can be considered a sub-assumption of SWA.6 The challenges arising from act-state dependence in decision problems have, prominently triggered by Newcomb’s paradox (Nozick, 1969),
established an entire subfield of modern decision theory (e.g., Karni (1985, 2018); Schervish et al.
(1990, 1991); Baccelli (2020); Jansen and Augustin (2022)) with application ranging from insurance
science (e.g., De Donder and Leroux (2021)) to portfolio selection (e.g., Wei et al. (2020)).
But why can these assumptions be problematic? At first glance, it seems obvious to argue that
both assumptions, CWA and SWA, are always trivially satisfiable: In the case of CWA, an artificial state s˚ could always be introduced, which occurs exactly when none of the clearly specifiable
states occurs. However, closer inspection clarifies that CWA also requires that under each state the
consequences of the actions must be clearly specifiable. This is obviously less easily satisfied: What
consequence should one assign to an artificial state s˚ that cannot even be precisely described? In
this light, CWA seems a very strong assumption after all.
This point of view is backed by the fact that the (presumably) most standard approach to circumvent act-state
dependence is to extend the state space from S to AS , i.e., the set of all functions from S to A (Gibbard and Harper,
1981). While, in this way, it is ensured that all act-state dependence disappears from the model, as the extended
state space definition directly accounts for potential dependencies, dealing with the extended state space enforces
dealing with a state space of unnatural granularity, thereby enforcing violations of SWA.

In the case of SWA one could try to argue that a choice function whose choice sets depend on
the specific framing of the decision problem should not be chosen anyway, and thus invariance
under the granularity of the set of states of the world should serve as a quality measure for choice
functions rather than a criticism of SWA. However, the requirement of perfect invariance under
the granularity of the state space (i.e., the way the states are represented as a set) quickly leads
to trivial and non-informative decision criteria: Most often the only choice function satisfying this
kind of invariance property will be the identity map, which is obviously useless for any meaningful
analysis. Thus, invariance under the granularity of the state space is presumably only desirable at
first sight. This point of view is certainly backed by the observation that even the choice function
based on expected utility does not satisfy this requirement. Finally, it is important to note that
the two assumptions – CWA and SWA – are by no means independent of each other: if an artificial
state s˚ is introduced as described above, it is probably only in extremely rare cases possible to
make statements about the naturalness of its granularity. In other words: often, the violation of
one of the assumptions can only be prevented by deliberately violating the other assumption.
Example 2. Consider again the situation of our running Example 1. It is easy to see here that
neither CWA nor SWA are sensibly acceptable. Since we only have exemplary consequences of certain decisions (the different amounts of monetary gains throughout the month) at our disposal, but
no insight into the underlying dynamics, it is difficult in this situation to specify even an approximation of a state space. In such a situation, we clearly leave the scope of application of classical
decision-theoretical methods and therefore need alternative tools to analyze the existing information
(i.e. the observed consequences of action). Any serious alternative theory should, on the one hand,
recognize the empirical nature of the problem but, on the other hand, also provide guarantees of
optimality that go beyond the merely empirical.
4. Empirical Decision Problems
We now come to the main object of investigation of this paper: empirical decision problems (EDPs).
The main motivation here is to build a decision theory exclusively on observable quantities and thus
to completely avoid strong assumptions like SWA and CWA and, with them, the necessity to define
a state space or a set of consequences at all. At the same time, our newly built theory should be
as close as possible to the classical decision-theoretic framework recalled in Section 2, in this way,
still retaining its technical and philosophical maturity. However, the sparseness of assumptions has
to come at a price: Building solely on observables makes the statements about e.g. optimality of
decisions purely empirical ones, not directly generalizing to the underlying true decision problem
(if there is one). This demands to back up these statements with generalizable guarantees.
To this end, we propose three different approaches: In Section 5, we show under which conditions
consistent estimators can be constructed for the choice sets of special classes of choice functions in
an EDP. In Section 6, we derive statistical tests based solely on observed consequences for checking
whether an act lies in the choice set of some choice function of interest. In Section 7, we propose
a direct credal inference approach for robustifying empirically obtained choice functions by using
credal sets for describing contaminated sampling models. A schematic overview of the paper so far
and the concepts still to be discussed can be seen in Figure 1.

4.2. Limitations and Opportunities of Empirical Decision Theory
The usefulness of the analysis of an EDP stands and falls with the interpretation of the protocol
it contains. Put more specifically, if the protocol for each concrete action description ai can be
understood as a random sample from the distribution of Xai , i.e., the act underlying the action
description, then EDPs can be approached using specialized techniques of (non-parametric robust)
frequentist statistics. This will be the main route we will follow in the next few sections of the present
article. In Section 6.2, we will even see that some of the methods developed can be extended to
cases where the classical statistical assumptions are slightly violated.
Nevertheless, a clear limitation of the scope of our theory is already apparent here: To ensure that
EDPs can be analyzed using frequentist statistical methods, even if only potentially, we assume
that each of the generations of the states of the world is happening according to a (generally
unknown) probability distribution—i.e., it stems from the independent repetitions of the same
random experiment. This assumption can, and will be, weakened by considering contamination
models. Even more severe violations, however, would allow almost anything to happen, and analyses
based on protocols or EDPs in general could become highly misleading. An especially severe
example of such a situation is illustrated in the following continuation of our running problem.

7. Direct Credal Inference via Noise-robust ECFs Using Contamination Models
In Section 5, we saw that the empirical variant of the EU-based ECF acts as a consistent approximator of the population variant of the choice function under certain conditions. While this is
certainly reassuring from a purely mathematical point of view—at least in the limit, one is doing
the right thing—it still provides little information about the uncertainty associated with using the
empirical choice function in the finite sample case. This is why we formalized the dependence of
the ECF on the intended contamination of the underlying empirical distributions in Section 6. We
then turned to statistical permutation tests to test the significance of the choice sets obtained by
the EU-induced ECF using appropriate statistical tests. Credal sets proved useful in this endeavor,
as they allowed modeling contaminates samples, see Assumption 1 and Theorem 3. In what follows,
we aim to robustify empirical choice function in a more direct way, but still relying on credal sets.
Empirical decision problems do not hinge on strong population-level assumptions like SWA and
CWA. They allow for what we refer to as direct inference: We do not, as is customary, relate the
sample to the population via common i.i.d.-style assumptions and then draw conclusions in an
indirect way. Instead, we directly approximate the underlying population choice function by the
empirical choice function, see Definitions 3 and 4.
This comes at the price of a stronger dependence on the observed protocols. Through an epistemiological lense, direct inference might be susceptible to what Horkheimer and Adorno (Horkheimer
and Adorno, 2002, page vii) refer in general to as the positivist "myth of that which is the case":
adhering to observations without questioning their origin and relation to the explanandum, see also
Herrmann et al. (2024); Rodemann et al. (2025) in an explicit statistical and machine-learning
context. Surely, this is not desirable. In order to hedge against these risks of blind positivism, we
aim at a nuanced robustification of ECFs. In particular, we want to robustify emiprical variants of
the ECFs towards noise in the observed protocols.

8. Proof of Concept: EDPs for Evaluating Prompting Strategies for ChatGPT
We now come to an (illustrative) application of our theory in the context of comparing prompting
strategies when using ChatGPT. Specifically, we want to use our framework to study whether the
degree of politeness in prompts has an impact on the quality of the responses produced. Here, we
operationalize quality by a two-dimensional metric composed of perplexity (PPL) (Jelinek et al.,
24

2005; Holtzman et al., 2020) that measures how well a probabilistic model predicts a sequence
of words, and coherence (Jelinek et al., 2005) that calculates the log-likelihood of generated text
given its prompt (thus, low perplexity values are desirable while for coherence it’s vice versa).13
It should be noted that although prompt politeness has recently received quite some attention in
connection with the energy consumption of LLMs (Delavande et al., 2025; Raebsamen, 2025), this
section serves more as a proof of concept than a fully developed application. However, we believe
that our example can serve as a blueprint for future studies in this direction.
8.1. Setup of the Study
We want to exemplarily compare three different degrees of politeness in prompting. These correspond exactly to the different action descriptions in the underlying EDP. The common context of
the action descriptions in our EDP is the search for the best possible explanation of the board game
GO with a fixed length. Concretely, we have:
a1 : neutral (“Explain GO in exactly 20 words.”)
a2 : polite (“Explain GO in exactly 20 words, please.”)
a3 : inpolite (“Tell me what GO is in exactly 20 words. No more, no less. Just do it.”)
Our protocol is structured as follows: Each of the three action descriptions was applied to the free
version OpenAI’s ChatGPT model GPT-5 multiple times (a1 eleven times, a2 fourteen times, and
a3 ten times).14 As a consequence of each execution, the vector (PPL,Coh) was calculated from
perplexity and coherence. The corresponding protocol is shown in Tables 3 to 5 in the Appendix.
As the choice function, we select the ECF based on the FSD rule chÁ,π in Equation (2). The set F is
then the set of all uniformly bounded functions that are isotone with respect to the component-wise

Q1 : How does the choice set look like if we apply the empirical variant of (2) as an ECF?
Q2 : Can we use our test to derive inferential guarantees for the choice sets of (2)?

possibility of questioning guarantees if they do not appear robust enough.
9. Summary, Discussion, and Future Research
9.1. Summary
In this article, we introduced empirical decision problems as an alternative to classical decision
theory that is based purely on observational data (protocols) and thus avoids the strong structural
assumptions of the classical theory such as CWA or SWA. We were able to show that the central
decision-making tool of classical decision theory – choice functions – can also be adapted to the
empirical case discussed here. We could see that this adaptation has the advantage that many
concepts can be transferred more or less directly from classical theory to our setting. Examples
include the transfer of common decision-making rules such as expected utility maximization or firstorder stochastic dominance. However, basing our theory purely on observational data naturally has
its price: any statement about optimal actions refers purely to their empirical optimality given the
observations made so far. In other words, we initially make a purely descriptive statement about
the observed protocol, and not about the optimality of the respective action description in the
underlying population.
In order to relate the optimality statements derived from an EDP to the underlying population,
we use statistical learning theory on the one hand and non-parametric statistical methods on the
other: In Theorem 1, we show how, under mild regularity assumptions on the consequence space,
ECFs can be constructed such that they form consistent approximators of underlying population
choice functions. In Theorem 2, we show how, given an EDP, a consistent statistical test can
be constructed that examines whether an action belongs to a population choice set. Finally, in
28

Theorem 3, we demonstrate how the use of ideas from the theory of imprecise probabilities can
be used to make our test more robust to deviations from the i.i.d. assumption. In other words,
our inferential guarantees go far beyond the usual, allowing us to analyze the dependence of their
validity on the assumptions made about the design of the protocols.
The decision-making framework presented here, based solely on observable components, has many
potential areas of application. Specifically, we have focused on a proof-of-concept application in the
field of generative AI model evaluation, the results of which look extremely promising: To the best
of our knowledge, both the inferential guarantees of the evaluations resulting from our framework
and their robustness analyses are rather novel in this currently much-discussed field (an exception
is given by Garces Arias et al. (2025a)).
9.2. Discussion and Future Research
While we are strongly convinced of the usefulness of the new perspective on decision-making under
uncertainty described here, we should not ignore the limitations of our framework and the avenues
for future research that arise from them. The first major limitation has already been discussed and
illustrated in Section 4.2: The analysis of EDPs, or at least the inferential and robustness guarantees
derived from them, only apply in frequentist settings. As soon as we doubt the randomness of the
generation of states and suspect a deterministic, possibly even hostile mechanism behind them, our
theory remains silent about these situations. In such cases, it would be much more appropriate to
first try to learn the deterministic mechanism from a protocol and then use this knowledge to develop
the best decision-making strategies. However interesting this may be, it would cover a completely
different set of questions and thus go far beyond the scope of the results presented in this paper.
Interestingly, these problems could still be discussed—at least in purely formal terms—within the
same framework proposed here. This opens up a interesting avenue for future research.
Another limitation concerns the design of the protocols on which our theory is based. To ensure
the validity of our inferential guarantees (Theorems 1 and 2), we assume that the consequences
under each individual action description can be interpreted as an i.i.d. sample. For various reasons,
this is questionable in practice, to say the least. On the one hand, in real-world problems, the
state space is likely to be dynamic, so that, strictly speaking, each time an action description is
followed, one could formally be faced with a slightly different decision problem. However, even
if we assume a static state space, the assumption that dependencies do not arise when an action
description is repeated multiple times seems rather strong in many situations. To a certain extent,
our framework is able to respond adequately to these challenges: In Theorem 3, we have shown that
at least the inferential guarantees obtained through testing can be extended to protocol designs
that deviate from a perfect i.i.d. structure. However, it should be clearly noted here that our
framework is more suited to handling isolated outliers or sampling processes that deviate only
slightly from an i.i.d. design. Our framework will only very rarely produce interesting statements
about designs substantially differing from i.i.d., as the modified statistical test from Theorem 3 will
rapidly lose much of its power in such cases. It is definitely a very interesting avenue of research
to investigate whether stronger statements can be made here, for example by first attempting to
learn the dependency structures from the protocols.

Proof of Theorem 3
Under Assumption 5, the empirical measures of the (unknown) i.i.d. samples are contained in the
credal sets of the (known) contaminated samples. Since this holds for arbitrary samples, it applies
specifically to every permutation sample. In a permutation test, the statistic is recomputed for each
permutation of the combined sample and compared to its value on the original sample; larger resampled values provide evidence against the null. By Theorem 2, this procedure is (asymptotically)
valid under i.i.d. sampling. In our setting, computing the statistic over all compatible empirical
measures ensures that the resulting set contains the i.i.d. statistic, both for the observed and for
each permuted sample. To guarantee rejection only when fully justified, we adopt a worst-case
33

rule: evidence against the null is counted only if the supremum of the observed statistic remains
below the infimum of the corresponding resampled statistic. This coincides with the test from the
theorem and therefore concludes the proof.
