# Synthetic rubric level inflation

A rubric is a set of self-contained criteria, each given full or zero points, against which an answer is scored. In PanCanBench the criteria were written by people: rubrics for 282 authentic patient questions were designed by oncology fellows, and each criterion is scored all-or-nothing.[^c1][^c2] The same questions were also given synthetic rubrics, generated by an LLM from the rubric design guideline and from sample answers of four AI models, with the instruction to be specific to the question.[^c3][^c4]

Scored against the two rubric sources, the same set of models moved in different ways. Under the AI-generated rubrics the absolute scores rose, by 17.9 points on average.[^c5] The relative order of models was generally kept.[^c6] The rise was not even: top-tier models approached near-saturation.[^c7]

So what the rubric source changes is mostly the level of the score, not the order. A benchmark built on generated rubrics can say which system is better more reliably than it can say how complete a given answer is, because the second depends on the level. The authors draw the same line: AI-generated rubrics "have the potential to serve as a scalable proxy for determining relative model rankings", and the synthetic rubrics "cannot replace humancurated benchmarks".[^c8][^c9]

The paper does not say why the level rises. Both sources saw the same material: the fellows were given "representative responses from four AI models" to help their design, as the generator was.[^c10] So the gap lies in how the criteria were written, which the paper does not separate into writer, prompt or guideline. That is my reading; it is not tested here.

A second, unmeasured case points the same way. ProfBench criticises DeepResearch-Bench because its criteria were generated synthetically and never checked by experts: "there is a pervasive bias towards Gemini-2.5-Pro since the criteria and the reference ‘high-quality’ answer are both generated by this model."[^c13] On that benchmark the same generator is rated "reaching more than 48.5 out of a maximum of 50 on each of the 4 axes, meaning >97%."[^c14] This is the paper's observation about a benchmark it did not rerun under a controlled rubric source, so it shows the pattern without measuring a level shift. It does suggest a candidate cause that the PanCanBench comparison leaves open: when one model writes both the criteria and the reference answer, its own style is graded against itself (my inference, not tested by either paper).

## Limits

- One disease, one patient help line, 282 questions. "this study is limited by its small sample size" is the authors' own caveat.[^c11]
- The expert rubrics serve as the reference for "inflation". They are not ground truth, so inflation means departure from them, not from correctness.
- The ranking half of the claim is qualitative in the text I read ("generally", "similar"). No rank correlation is given there, so it is weaker than the level result.
- The cause of the level difference is not separated (see above). Any account of why generated rubrics are more lenient is a conjecture.
- Scores come from an LLM judge, and the authors note "there is potential for biases inherent in any LLM-based judging".[^c12] The level comparison shares that judge across both rubric sources.
