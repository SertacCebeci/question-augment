# Benchmark drift

A model-based benchmark is fitted once. It has a judge, which turns each generated output into a score, and a set of test items chosen so that they are hard for the models of the day while the judge can still handle them. The paper states the constraint directly: "test prompts must be selected to be challenging for current T2I models but" the judge is not.[^c1] Both parts are frozen at release. The outputs they score are not. The paper audits GenEval, a text-to-image benchmark of 553 prompts in six categories, judged by an object detector and by CLIP for colour.[^c2] At release its judgments matched humans on 83% of the images from three earlier generators.[^c3]

The claim is that this agreement decays as the outputs change. The authors name the phenomenon "benchmark drift." Their account is a distribution shift: "with a distribution shift in images generated by T2I models over time" the evaluation "drifted farther from human judgment."[^c4] What the frozen judge gets wrong is then the part that moves with the new images. (My reading: the paper does not show the judge's error at the level of individual image features.)

The measurement is a human study of 8 models, with three annotators per image, "amounting to 23,226 human annotations."[^c5] The net deviation between reported and human scores "was within ±3% for SD2.1 and SDXL," which were released before GenEval. It then rose with release date, "culminating in a deviation of 17.7% for the latest T2I model, Gemini 2.5 Flash Image."[^c6][^c7] The deviation grows along the time axis. That is what separates drift from a single bad model: the judge is not wrong on average, it is wrong more as the generators move on.

Drift also hides saturation. On human scores, the four current models released in 2024 and 2025 "score 94.8% and above with rewriting techniques," and the authors conclude that "GenEval is effectively saturated today, and has been for over a year."[^c8][^c9] A reported benchmark still shows the gains papers use to rank methods: "papers report gains of 2-3% over previous models."[^c10] So a judge that has drifted can make a benchmark look informative at the moment the models it separates have converged. The reported number and the human number then diverge by more than the gains the papers claim to show.

The direction of the shift is not uniform. Rewriting, a prompt-side technique that several recent generators use, "decreases human alignment for 4 models" but "it increases human alignment for Qwen-Image and Gemini 2.5 Flash Image by 3.3% on average."[^c11][^c12] A correction with one fixed sign would not remove the drift.

The same structure appears in another judge. For VQAScore, "estimates tend to diverge from human judgments over time after the model" is released.[^c13] The paper's own remedy is a different judge design, treated in [[atomwise-soft-verification]].

Beyond text-to-image, the closest analogue is in agent evaluation, where a fixed judge's error can depend on which version produced the output: [[version-dependent-judge-error]] treats that dependence as a differential term in a paired comparison. Benchmark drift is the absolute version of the same worry: the judge is not compared across two versions but tracked against humans as the generators change. (My inference: the two share a fixed judge facing a moving producer, and nothing in the GenEval text tests the link.)

## Limits

- Eight models, ordered by release. The trend is a pattern over eight points. The text does not isolate which feature of the new images causes the drift; the distribution shift is the paper's explanation.
- The human side is also noisy. "of the data points were labeled unanimously by the 3 annotators," so the remainder were settled by majority vote, and the human score is not a ground truth either.[^c14]
- The measured drift is for text-to-image judges on one benchmark. Transfer to other judged domains is my inference, not tested here.
- Drift is defined relative to human judgment. Where the human reference itself moves, the measure cannot separate the judge's drift from a change in what people accept.
- The paper does not claim the cure is permanent: "avoiding benchmark drift is far from guaranteed."[^c15] The replacement benchmark is untested over time.
