# Feedback term selection and weighting

Traditional pseudo-relevance feedback (PRF) updates a bag-of-words query in two phases. The first selects the terms that enter the expanded query; the second weights those terms, together with the original query's terms. Classical PRF takes its candidate terms from top-ranked retrieved documents. An LLM expansion changes only the source of those terms: in HyDE, "traditional PRF methods extract feedback terms from topranked retrieved documents", whereas the feedback terms are generated by the LLM.[^c1] The update step, though, has usually been plain string concatenation of the query and the generated text, which "directly feed[s] the LLMs generated text" into BM25 with minimal processing.[^c2]

The mechanism of the selection step follows the Anserini implementation. Each generated document becomes a term-frequency vector; terms common in the corpus are dropped, "keeping only the terms which occur in less than 10% of the corpus documents"; the rest are ranked and cut to the top k.[^c3] The weighting step then assigns each surviving term a weight. Rocchio does this with two parameters, one scaling the query's term vector and one scaling the feedback vectors, which "instead controls the weight of the query vector and feedback vectors using parameters" rather than giving them equal weight.[^c4] HyDE's own update is the special case in which "it considers the query as an additional feedback document", so the query and the feedback documents are averaged equally.[^c20] RM3 is a third weighting: "This relevance model is then linearly interpolated with the probability of the term given the original query."[^c21]

The comparison isolates the selection step. Against naive concatenation, the average-vector update was better by 3, 2.3 and 3.1 points on three LLMs. The authors place the difference in selection: the main difference between the two "lies in the term selection step".[^c5][^c22] Rocchio was the strongest of the feedback models on every LLM: "Of all the feedback methods, Rocchio demonstrates the strongest effectiveness with HyDE."[^c6] The authors read this as HyDE's own feedback under-weighting the query: "This suggests that the HyDE feedback under emphasizes query terms."[^c7] Across the 14 datasets the reported gain over the strongest concatenation baseline is "by up to 1.4 points (4.2%)", and 2.2 points on the low-resource tasks.[^c13]

Two accounts of why the gain arises are given in the conclusion. The first is that feedback models "implicitly filter out noisy terms, keeping only terms that are meaningful within the corpus" and heavily weighted in the generations.[^c8] The second is a claim about weighting rather than selection: "The second improvement, we hypothesize, comes from a more stable and linear weighting of query terms and terms in feedback documents".[^c9] Concatenation expresses query emphasis through repetition, as in Query2Doc, which uses "with a constant query repeat of five", and MuGI, which "adaptively repeats the query using the following equation".[^c10][^c11] A Rocchio query weight is a single parameter, and the paper notes that it "can be adapted linearly with a single parameter rather than being dependent on other factors such as feedback document length."[^c12]

What transfers from PRF and what does not. The two-phase split and the query-versus-feedback balance transfer unchanged, because they are properties of the vector update, not of where the feedback came from. What does not obviously transfer is the assumption behind classical PRF that the feedback documents are retrieved corpus documents. A generated document is not in the corpus. My reading is that the corpus-frequency filter is what restores corpus grounding to its terms; the paper does not test this directly.

## Limits

- The experiments score BM25 only: the claim is for "BM25 with HyDE".[^c15] Whether selection and weighting help a dense retriever is not shown by this work. The dense case is treated in [[query-anchored-expansion-integration]].
- The parameters are defaults, not tuned per LLM or per dataset: "we stick to the default hyperparameters used in the literature".[^c16] Whether a tuned weight would widen or close the gap is untested.
- The metric is Recall@20: "Main results (Recall@20) across MS MARCO and BEIR."[^c17] Effects on top-10 precision or on nDCG are not reported in the text I read.
- The advantage is an average and depends on the benchmark. On MS MARCO, concatenation is competitive, and Query2Doc and MuGI beat RM3 on every MS MARCO dataset for gpt-oss-20b; on BEIR, "RM3 beats Query2Doc on all 10 datasets".[^c14][^c23] The same paper notes that "while RM3 is less effective than" Rocchio on HyDE documents, the reverse on top-ranked BM25 documents, without a cause given in the text I read.[^c18]
- The two accounts of the gain are a conclusion, not a tested decomposition. The second is explicitly a hypothesis, and no ablation in the text I read separates selection from weighting beyond the average-vector comparison.
- Adaptive weighting is not tried: "An interesting area for future work would be to investigate how to adaptively" reweight the Rocchio parameters.[^c19]
- The 10% corpus-frequency cut and the cap of 128 terms come from the cited implementation and are not varied in the text I read.
- I read the prose and the main-table caption; the numeric tables were not parsed, so the per-dataset figures above are the paper's own summaries.
