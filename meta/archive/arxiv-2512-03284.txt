Abstract
Spatial reasoning in large-scale 3D environments remains
challenging for current vision–language models, which are
typically constrained to room-scale scenarios. We formalize Active House-Scale Spatial Reasoning (AHSR), a new
paradigm in which a model reasons over a pre-built housescale 3D map via virtual spatial tool invocations to answer
spatial questions, without exhaustive scene-wide processing.
To support AHSR research, we introduce H2 U3D (Holistic
House Understanding in 3D), the first benchmark targeting
house-scale 3D scene understanding, featuring environments
with an average aggregate floor area of 250.8 m2 and up to
three floors, together with hierarchical coarse-to-fine visual
representations. Building on H2 U3D, we propose SpatialReasoner, an AHSR framework trained via supervised fine-tuning
with self-correction, followed by reinforcement learning with
a task-aware adaptive exploration reward. SpatialReasoner
achieves state-of-the-art performance on H2 U3D with 64.9%
overall accuracy, outperforming strong baselines including
GPT-5.4 and Gemini-3.5-Flash, and generalizes effectively
to MT-HM3D and HM-EQA. These results demonstrate the
clear advantage of active map-directed exploration over passive scene-wide processing in house-scale 3D understanding.

Introduction
In embodied AI, agents operating over extended periods
continuously build and maintain an internal 3D representation of their surroundings. Such representations are inherently house-scale—spanning multiple floors and dozens of
rooms—and serve as a persistent spatial memory for downstream reasoning tasks. Efficiently answering natural language queries grounded in this house-scale 3D memory remains an open and practically important problem.
Many existing 3D QA methods follow a passive perception paradigm, processing a fixed, query-independent set of
sampled views or a scene-wide representation. Although effective in bounded scenes, maintaining fine-grained coverage becomes increasingly costly as the environment grows,
requiring more observations and introducing more queryirrelevant content. Embodied QA instead acquires evidence
through sequential navigation, typically without access to a
pre-built persistent map. Its observations are therefore constrained by the agent’s current pose and the navigable transitions required to reach a target region.

A key observation motivates our approach: regardless of
scene scale, the region relevant to a given query remains
spatially localized. This suggests that exhaustive scene-wide
processing is unnecessary, and that selectively directing perception toward query-relevant regions is both sufficient and
efficient. We formalize this insight as Active House-Scale
Spatial Reasoning (AHSR): a paradigm in which a model,
equipped with spatial tools and a pre-built house-scale 3D
map, actively localizes and inspects query-relevant regions
to answer spatial questions. The house-scale setting is central
to AHSR—it is precisely because agents operate persistently
in such environments that a maintained map exists, and it is
at this scale that passive perception becomes computationally intractable. AHSR is thus orthogonal to passive perception, which lacks selectivity, and to Embodied QA, which
operates without a prior map. Furthermore, while EQA is
typically tethered to sequential physical exploration, AHSR
enables random access to the environment via virtual spatial tools. By decoupling reasoning from the constraints of
robotic movement, AHSR replaces slow navigation with efficient, query-driven evidence gathering.
To support research on AHSR, we introduce H2 U3D
(Holistic House Understanding in 3D), a benchmark comprising 34,651 QA pairs across 797 HM3D scenes. The
scenes have an average aggregate floor area of 250.8 m2 and
contain up to three floors. Beyond question–answer pairs,
H2 U3D provides hierarchical coarse-to-fine visual representations and recorded spatial-operation annotations. A manually verified subset of the training data further includes
chain-of-thought (CoT) annotations. These resources support
research on spatial question answering, exploration decisionmaking, and tool-use reasoning.
Building on H2 U3D, we propose SpatialReasoner, an
AHSR framework that autonomously invokes spatial tools—
zooming into a region of interest or rendering a first-person
view from a specified viewpoint—to progressively localize
and inspect query-relevant areas within house-scale 3D environments. We adopt a two-stage training strategy: supervised fine-tuning on tool-interleaved trajectories augmented
with self-correction examples to instill the required output
format and spatial operation patterns, followed by reinforcement learning with spatially grounded rewards that adapt
exploration to rollout success, favor query-relevant spatial
operations, and penalize redundant tool invocations.

Related Work
Benchmarks for 3D Question Answering. Room-scale reconstructions such as ScanNet (Dai et al. 2017), ScanNet++
(Yeshwanth et al. 2023), and ARKitScenes (Baruch et al.
2022) have supported 3D question-answering benchmarks
including ScanQA (Azuma et al. 2022) and SQA3D (Ma
et al. 2023). More recent benchmarks built on HM3D (Ramakrishnan et al. 2021), including OpenEQA (Majumdar
et al. 2024), HM-EQA (Ren et al. 2024), and MT-HM3D
(Zhai et al. 2025), extend question answering to house-scale
residential environments. However, their evaluation episodes
are restricted to a single navigable floor and do not require
reasoning over a jointly presented multi-floor map.

In contrast, H2 U3D contains house-scale scenes with up
to three floors and jointly exposes the floor-level representations of each scene. The model must first identify the
query-relevant floor and region before acquiring detailed visual evidence. To the best of our knowledge, H2 U3D is the
first 3D question-answering benchmark designed for active
reasoning in multi-floor environments, with tool-interleaved
annotations that record floor selection, region zooming, and
viewpoint rendering.
Passive 3D Scene Reasoning. Most VLM-based approaches
to 3D scene understanding operate on a query-independent
scene representation constructed before the question is
known. Multi-view methods such as Video3D-LLM (Zheng,
Huang, and Wang 2025) and 3D-LLaVA (Deng et al. 2025)
reason over pre-sampled visual observations, while related
approaches incorporate 3D coordinate information (Zhu et al.
2025a) or bird’s-eye-view representations (Qi et al. 2025) to
improve spatial grounding. Although effective in bounded
environments, these methods process essentially the same
visual evidence for every query. At house scale, maintaining fine-grained scene coverage generally requires more
observations and introduces increasing amounts of queryirrelevant information. Given a pre-built 3D map, we instead
study query-time evidence acquisition: the model uses coarse
global context to identify which regions and viewpoints require fine-grained inspection, avoiding dense processing of
detailed observations across the entire scene.
Active Exploration and Spatial Memory. Active perception methods acquire query-relevant evidence through interaction rather than relying solely on fixed visual inputs. ExploreEQA (Ren et al. 2024) uses answer confidence to guide
exploration, while GraphEQA (Saxena et al. 2025) organizes
observations with 3D semantic scene graphs. MemoryEQA

(Zhai et al. 2025) and 3D-Mem (Yang et al. 2025b) further maintain structured spatial memories to support longhorizon reasoning. These methods primarily formulate evidence acquisition as sequential embodied exploration, in
which the available observations depend on the agent’s current pose and navigable transitions. Struct2D (Zhu et al.
2025b) also employs BEV representations and reasoning annotations for spatial question answering.
AHSR considers a complementary setting in which the
environment has already been reconstructed into a persistent
multi-floor 3D map. The model can therefore access queryrelevant regions through virtual zooming and viewpointrendering operations. SpatialReasoner focuses on learning
a query-conditioned map-access policy, separating the decision of where to inspect from the navigation required to reach
that location.

H2 U3D Dataset
In this section, we present H2 U3D, Holistic House Understanding in 3D, a Visual Question Answering (VQA) dataset
targeting house-scale 3D scene understanding (3DSU).
H2 U3D is built upon the HM3D (Ramakrishnan et al. 2021)
dataset, which provides high-quality 3D reconstructions of
large-scale residential environments. Compared to previous
3D VQA datasets (Azuma et al. 2022; Ma et al. 2023), which
are limited to room-scale, H2 U3D extends the scale to houselevel, including multi-floor and multi-room environments,
making the task significantly more challenging due to the
much larger spatial scale. Specifically, each data sample in
H2 U3D is represented as (S, q, a), where S denotes a multifloor, multi-room, ultra-large indoor scene, and q is a natural
language query related to S; a is the correct answer expected
to be produced by the model.

Task Formulation
We define Active House-Scale Spatial Reasoning (AHSR)
as follows. Given a pre-built house-scale 3D map S, its floorlevel BEVs F , a natural-language query q, and a set of virtual
spatial tools T , a model iteratively invokes tools u1:T to acquire query-relevant visual observations o1:T and produces
an answer â within a tool-call budget B. The model initially
observes only the coarse multi-floor context F ; detailed regional and first-person observations are selected during inference conditioned on the query and the preceding interaction
history.
Unlike passive 3D QA, AHSR does not fix the detailed visual evidence before the query is known. To support the learning of such query-conditioned map-access policies, each
H2 U3D example additionally provides the reference floor,
zoom region, viewpoint configuration, and a tool-interleaved
exploration trajectory. These annotations provide training supervision rather than defining a unique valid exploration path.

Dataset Curation & Annotations
QA Data Generation. For each house-scale scene, we first
use Habitat-Simulator to render a high-resolution BEV of
every floor, providing the generation model with global
context about the multi-floor layout. Conditioned on these

BEVs, Gemini-3.1-Pro (Google DeepMind 2026a) identifies a question-worthy region and invokes zoom_in with a
selected floor index and a two-dimensional bounding box.
After inspecting the returned local BEV, the model selects
a ground-plane position and camera orientation within the
region and invokes render_view to obtain a first-person
observation. Views without sufficient visual information are
discarded.
The model then generates a QA pair conditioned jointly on
the multi-floor BEVs, the zoomed regional view, and the final
first-person observation. The generation prompt requires the
question to be grounded in the explored scene, to identify
its referents unambiguously, and to have exactly one answer
supported by the visual evidence. For every accepted QA
pair, we retain the selected floor, zoom region, viewpoint
position, and camera orientation, together with the complete
tool history.
Quality Control and Human Validation. To reduce generation errors and model-specific bias, we use GPT-5.2 (OpenAI
2026) to screen all QA candidates generated by Gemini-3.1Pro for answerability, ambiguity, and answer correctness. We
then apply deduplication and category filtering and divide the
data into scene-disjoint training, validation, and test sets.
Every QA pair in the validation and test sets is independently reviewed by two annotators using the corresponding
scene evidence. Each annotator judges whether the question
is answerable, whether its interpretation and referents are
unambiguous, and whether the annotated answer is correct.
A sample is retained only when both annotators consider it
valid.
The annotators agreed on 82.37% of the reviewed samples,
and 1493 samples were removed during human validation.
CoT Annotation. We augment a subset of the accepted training QA pairs with tool-interleaved reasoning annotations. For
each selected sample, we provide Gemini-3.1-Pro with the
question and annotated answer, the multi-floor BEVs, the
zoomed regional view, the rendered first-person observation,
and the complete sequence of tool invocations and their parameters. The model is instructed to produce a reasoning
trace aligned with the recorded exploration trajectory.
The annotation describes the purpose of each spatial operation, the visual evidence obtained from its returned observation, and how that evidence informs the subsequent
operation or final prediction. The resulting trajectory interleaves reasoning steps with the corresponding zoom_in
and render_view calls and terminates with an answer
grounded in the collected visual evidence. All generated CoT
annotations are manually inspected before being included in
the training data.

SpatialReasoner
As illustrated in Fig. 1, SpatialReasoner is an active reasoning
framework for house-scale 3D scene understanding. Given a
pre-built 3D map and a textual query, the model starts from
multi-floor BEVs and iteratively invokes region-zooming and
viewpoint-rendering tools to acquire query-relevant visual
evidence. The returned observations are incorporated into
the reasoning context until the model produces a grounded
final answer. We first formalize this tool-interleaved inference
process and then describe its two-stage training procedure,
consisting of supervised fine-tuning with self-correction and
reinforcement learning with spatially grounded rewards.

Formal Definition
Under the AHSR formulation, SpatialReasoner starts from
the floor-level BEVs F of map S and accesses the
map through two spatial tools. zoom_in(fℓ , b) returns
a magnified BEV of region b on floor ℓ, whereas
render_view(p, θ) returns a first-person observation
from ground-plane position p and camera yaw θ on the currently selected floor.
At step t, the model samples

Supervised Fine-Tuning with Self-Correction
SpatialReasoner is initialized from Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct
(Yang et al. 2025a). Although the base model supports
generic tool use, it has not been trained for our spatial tool interface or the associated coarse-to-fine interaction protocol.
We therefore perform supervised fine-tuning on the toolinterleaved CoT subset of H2 U3D to align the model with
the required output format and spatial operation patterns.
Error-Induced Self-Correction Trajectories. Because the
verified CoT trajectories primarily demonstrate successful
exploration, they provide limited supervision for recovering
from ineffective tool calls. We therefore augment 25% of
them with template-based error-and-correction sequences.
Starting from a verified trajectory, we perturb either its zoom
region, ground-plane position, or camera orientation and execute the perturbed operation in the rendering environment.
We then insert the returned observation, a failure-analysis
step, and corrective tool calls that reconnect the sequence to
the original successful trajectory.
For 70% of the augmented trajectories, progressive correction refines the operation through two or three attempts. The
remaining 30% use reset correction, abandoning the ineffective configuration and restarting from an alternative region
or viewpoint. Both strategies eventually recover valid visual
evidence and preserve the original answer.

Reinforcement Learning with Spatial Rewards
Supervised fine-tuning equips the model with the required
output structure, spatial tool-use patterns, and self-correction
behaviors. We initialize reinforcement learning from this
checkpoint and, for each input (F, q), roll out a group of
G tool-interleaved reasoning trajectories {y1 , y2 , . . . , yG }.
During each rollout, every predicted spatial operation is immediately executed in Habitat-Simulator, and the returned
visual observation is appended to the interaction context before the model makes its next decision. Each completed trajectory contains intermediate reasoning, spatial operations,
environment observations, and a final answer. We score these
trajectories using the composite reward defined below and
optimize the policy with Group Relative Policy Optimization
(GRPO) (Shao et al. 2024) based on their relative rewards
within each group.

Experiments
Experimental Setup
Datasets and Metrics. We conduct training and evaluation
on H2 U3D, which is divided at the scene level into 27,674
training questions from 638 scenes, 3,605 validation questions from 80 scenes, and 3,372 test questions from 79 scenes.
The validation set is used for model selection, while all main
results are reported on the scene-disjoint test set. We report
multiple-choice accuracy for six question categories: Position, Attribute, Counting, Material, State, and Shape. Overall denotes the accuracy computed over all test questions.
We additionally evaluate cross-benchmark generalization on
MT-HM3D and HM-EQA, as well as two real-world environments reconstructed using 3D Gaussian Splatting.
Implementation Details. SpatialReasoner is initialized from
Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Yang et al. 2025a). During supervised fine-tuning, we use 5,626 manually verified, toolinterleaved CoT examples from the training split, of which
25% are augmented with error-induced self-correction trajectories. We use AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter 2019) and
train for 3 epochs with a global batch size of 16, a cosine
learning-rate schedule, and a peak learning rate of 10−6 . For
reinforcement learning, we use the remaining 22,048 training
questions that are not included in the SFT subset. For each
question, we sample 8 trajectories with a temperature of 1.0
and allow at most 8 tool calls per trajectory. GRPO training
is performed for 1 epoch with a learning rate of 10−6 .
Baselines. We compare SpatialReasoner with three groups
of baselines. Tool-free MLLMs include Gemini-3.5-Flash
(Google DeepMind 2026b), GPT-5.4 (OpenAI 2026),
Qwen3-VL-8B, Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B, and Qwen3-VL235B (Yang et al. 2025a).
These models receive the question, answer choices, and the
BEV image of each floor. Passive 3D models include Video3D-LLM (Zheng, Huang, and Wang 2025), LLaVA-3D (Zhu
et al. 2025a), VG-LLM (Zheng et al. 2025), and Ross3D
(Wang et al. 2025). Following their respective input protocols, Video-3D-LLM, VG-LLM, and Ross3D receive 32
sampled frames, while LLaVA-3D receives 24. Active perception baselines include ExploreEQA (Prismatic-7B) (Ren
et al. 2024) and MemoryEQA (Qwen3-VL-8B) (Zhai et al.
2025). We further equip Qwen3-VL-8B, Qwen3-VL-235B,
and GPT-5.4 with our rendering interface as direct tool-use
baselines. For a controlled comparison, these models receive
the same initial multi-floor BEVs, access the same regionzooming and viewpoint-rendering operations, and share a
maximum budget of 8 tool calls per question.

Experimental Results
We report the main results on the H2 U3D test set in Table 1. SpatialReasoner achieves the highest overall accuracy
of 64.9%, outperforming the strongest tool-enabled baseline, GPT-5.4 w/ Tool, by 4.8 percentage points (64.9% vs.
60.1%). It also exceeds the strongest tool-free model, GPT5.4, by 7.8 percentage points.

The strongest passive 3D baseline, Ross3D, trails SpatialReasoner by 8.6 percentage points overall (56.3% vs. 64.9%),
further demonstrating the advantage of query-directed exploration over fixed sampled views in house-scale environments.
Direct tool access improves all three evaluated VLMs.
Qwen3-VL-8B, Qwen3-VL-235B, and GPT-5.4 gain 2.0, 8.9,
and 3.0 percentage points, respectively, compared with their
tool-free counterparts. However, tool access alone does not
ensure effective spatial exploration. More importantly, under
the same Qwen3-VL-8B backbone and tool interface, SpatialReasoner improves over the directly prompted Qwen3VL-8B w/ Tool baseline by 17.1 percentage points (64.9%
vs. 47.8%). This controlled comparison demonstrates the importance of supervised tool-use alignment and reinforcement
post-training for learning effective exploration strategies.
We further evaluate SpatialReasoner on MT-HM3D (Zhai
et al. 2025) and HM-EQA (Ren et al. 2024), as shown
in Table 2. SpatialReasoner achieves accuracies of 47.9%
and 66.8%, outperforming the strongest compared baselines
by 4.8 percentage points on MT-HM3D and 3.3 percentage
points on HM-EQA, respectively. These results demonstrate
consistent improvements across other established HM3Dbased question-answering settings.

Real-World Reconstruction Evaluation
To evaluate SpatialReasoner beyond existing reconstructedscene benchmarks, we conduct an additional evaluation on
two real-world indoor environments: an office and a laboratory. We reconstruct each environment using 3D Gaussian
Splatting (Kerbl et al. 2023) and integrate the resulting scene
representation into the same hierarchical rendering interface
used by SpatialReasoner.
For each reconstructed scene, we manually create 30 questions, resulting in 60 questions in total. These questions are
authored independently of the models being evaluated and
are verified against the corresponding physical environments.
We compare SpatialReasoner with GPT-5.4 under two settings: a tool-free setting using only the initial scene observations, and a tool-enabled setting in which GPT-5.4 has
access to the same region-zooming and viewpoint-rendering
operations as SpatialReasoner.
SpatialReasoner achieves the best accuracy in both reconstructed environments, reaching 66.7% overall accuracy
compared with 61.7% for GPT-5.4 w/ Tool and 50.0% for
tool-free GPT-5.4. Providing GPT-5.4 with spatial tools improves its accuracy by 11.7 percentage points, showing the
benefit of targeted viewpoint acquisition. SpatialReasoner’s
additional 5.0-point improvement over the tool-enabled baseline provides complementary evidence that its learned exploration behavior remains effective on real-world scenes
reconstructed using 3D Gaussian Splatting.

Conclusion
In this work, we formalize Active House-Scale Spatial Reasoning (AHSR), in which a model answers spatial questions
by querying a pre-built house-scale 3D map through virtual
spatial tools. To support this setting, we introduce H2 U3D,
comprising 34,651 QA pairs across 797 HM3D scenes, together with hierarchical visual representations and a manually verified, tool-interleaved CoT subset. We further propose SpatialReasoner, trained through supervised fine-tuning
with self-correction followed by reinforcement learning with
adaptive, spatially grounded rewards.
SpatialReasoner achieves the best overall accuracy of
64.9% among the evaluated methods, outperforming GPT5.4 w/ Tool by 4.8 percentage points and the directly
prompted Qwen3-VL-8B tool-use baseline by 17.1 percentage points. It also achieves consistent improvements on MTHM3D and HM-EQA and shows promising results on two
real-world 3D reconstructions. Together, these results support query-directed evidence acquisition as an effective alternative to fixed scene-wide processing for house-scale 3D
understanding.

A.1

Additional Task and Dataset Details
Task Definition

SpatialReasoner performs active, query-conditioned perception over a pre-built house-scale 3D map. Given a multi-floor
scene S, its floor-level BEVs, and a natural-language query q,
the model iteratively invokes spatial tools to acquire relevant
visual observations and produce an answer a. As illustrated
in Figure 4, the model starts from multi-floor BEVs, identifies a query-relevant region, and renders first-person views
for fine-grained visual evidence. Unlike passive approaches
that process a fixed set of query-independent visual inputs,
SpatialReasoner selectively acquires observations according
to the current query.
Spatial Operation Protocols As illustrated in Figure 5,
our SpatialReasoner employs two fundamental spatial operations to enable active exploration and perception in largescale 3D environments. These operations are designed to facilitate hierarchical scene understanding through systematic
visual information gathering.
Region Zooming (zoom_in). Given a floor index and
a two-dimensional bounding box, this operation returns a
magnified BEV of the specified region on that floor:
zoom_in(fℓ , b) → Izoom

(7)

Here, fℓ is the 0-based floor index and b =
[xmin , ymin , xmax , ymax ] is an axis-aligned bounding box
expressed relative to the full-floor BEV. Following the
resolution-independent coordinate design of Qwen3-VL (Bai
et al. 2025), the model-facing tool interface represents each
coordinate as an integer on a [0, 1000] scale, independent of
the original BEV resolution. For the released reference annotations and spatial-reward computation, these coordinates are
converted to [0, 1]. The returned regional BEV is appended
to the interaction context, and floor ℓ becomes the currently
selected floor for subsequent viewpoint rendering.
Viewpoint Rendering (render_view). This operation
generates first-person perspective views from specified locations and orientations within the 3D scene. The operation is
formally expressed as:
render_view(p2D , θ, fi ) → Irendered

(8)

where p2D = [x, y] represents the 2D viewpoint position
in the active topdown coordinate frame. As in zoom_in,
each model-facing coordinate is an integer on the [0, 1000]
scale. When the optional floor index fi is specified, the coordinates are relative to the full-floor BEV; when fi is omitted,
they are relative to the current zoomed regional BEV. The
camera angle θ ∈ [0, 360) is measured clockwise from north
(the upward direction of the image), with 0◦ , 90◦ , 180◦ , and
270◦ corresponding to north, east, south, and west, respectively.
The operation supports two distinct rendering modes:
Global Render mode, activated when the floor index fi is
explicitly specified, operates on the original full-scale topdown view of the entire floor, enabling viewpoint positioning
across the complete spatial extent of the scene; and Regional
Render mode, engaged when fi is omitted, operates within

the boundaries of the current zoomed region established by
previous zoom_in operations, allowing for precise viewpoint
control within focused areas of interest.
These two complementary operations enable a coarse-tofine exploration strategy: the zoom_in operation provides
regional focus capabilities for identifying areas of interest
through overhead repositioning, while the render_view operation delivers detailed ground-level observations necessary
for fine-grained spatial reasoning and object identification
through both global and regional rendering modes.

Dataset Statistics

2

H U3D contains 34,651 multiple-choice question–answer
pairs collected from 797 HM3D scenes. We split the dataset
at the scene level into 27,674 training questions from 638
scenes, 3,605 validation questions from 80 scenes, and 3,372
test questions from 79 scenes, with no scene overlap across
the three splits. Among the training questions, 5,626 samples covering 635 training scenes are further augmented with
manually verified, tool-interleaved Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
annotations.
The questions span six categories: Position (9,838;
28.4%), Attribute (9,416; 27.2%), Counting (5,147; 14.9%),
Material (4,666; 13.5%), State (2,846; 8.2%), and Shape
(2,738; 7.9%). On average, each scene has an aggregate floor
area of 250.8 m2 and contains 1.73 floors, with up to three
floors per scene.
Example Data Format Each question–answer pair in
H2 U3D follows a standardized JSON template containing the
question, answer choices, category, scene identifiers, and reference spatial-operation annotations. The reference annotations provide supervision for spatial tool use but do not define
a unique valid exploration path. Tool-interleaved CoT annotations are provided separately for the 5,626-sample training
subset described above.
Template Structure. The QA template contains the following key components:
• question: A natural-language question grounded in the
corresponding house-scale scene and assigned to one of
the six categories described above.
• scene_name: The identifier of the corresponding
HM3D scene, enabling direct mapping to the 3D reconstruction data and geometric information.
• floor_idx: The 0-based reference floor containing the
visual evidence associated with the question. At inference
time, the model initially observes the BEVs of all floors
and must identify the query-relevant floor through its own
reasoning.
• choices: A list of four multiple-choice options (A, B,
C, D) providing candidate answers. This format enables
objective evaluation and reduces ambiguity in answer assessment.
• correct_answer: The ground-truth answer choice
(A, B, C, or D) corresponding to the correct option from
the choices list.
• category: One of the six question categories: Position,
Attribute, Counting, Material, State, or Shape.

Detailed QA Generation Process. As illustrated in Figure 7,

returns a local BEV of the selected region. After examining
this higher-resolution view, the model selects a ground-plane
position p2D = [x, y] and camera orientation θ, and invokes
render_view to obtain a first-person observation.

Quality Control and Human Validation. To reduce generation errors and model-specific bias, we first use GPT5.2 (OpenAI 2025) to screen all QA candidates generated by
Gemini-3.1-Pro for answerability, referential ambiguity, and
answer correctness. We then apply deduplication and category filtering before constructing scene-disjoint training,
validation, and test splits. For the validation and test sets,
two annotators independently review each candidate against
the corresponding scene evidence. A candidate is retained
only when both annotators judge it answerable, unambiguous, and correct. Among 8,470 reviewed candidates, 6,977
(82.37%) are retained, while the remaining 1,493 receive at
least one invalid judgment and are removed.
Chain-of-Thought Annotation Generation. Following the
initial QA generation, we construct detailed reasoning traces
to accompany each question-answer pair. The generated
questions, along with the complete exploration history including all visual inputs (BEV images, zoomed views, and
rendered perspectives) and the sequential tool invocation
records, are reintroduced to Gemini-3.1-Pro in a second processing stage. This comprehensive context enables the model

to reconstruct and articulate the step-by-step reasoning process that leads from the initial spatial query to the final answer, the complete prompt used for CoT generation is detailed
in F. The resulting Chain-of-Thought (CoT) annotations provide explicit reasoning chains that detail the purpose of each
spatial operation, the visual information extracted at each
step, and the logical connections between observations and
conclusions. This two-stage generation approach ensures that
our dataset not only contains accurate question-answer pairs
but also provides rich supervisory signals for training models to perform structured spatial reasoning with interpretable
decision-making processes. All generated CoT annotations
are manually inspected before being included in the training
data.
Self-correction Data Synthesis Details. To enhance the robustness of SpatialReasoner, we develop a systematic error
injection mechanism that simulates common spatial reasoning mistakes and their corresponding correction strategies.
We inject template-generated erroneous exploration paths
into 25% of the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) data to create selfcorrection training examples.
As shown in Figure 8, we identify three primary categories
of spatial operation errors:
• Wrong Zoom-in Operations: We inject errors by modifying the bounding box coordinates b2D to focus on
irrelevant regions that do not contain target objects or

Implementation Details

Simulator Integration for Training. We integrate HabitatSim as the rendering engine to generate hierarchical visual
representations during training and inference. The simulator
supports dynamic scene exploration through adaptive camera
configuration, where we manipulate the near and far clipping
planes to achieve coarse-to-fine rendering, positioning cameras at varying heights for BEV images and optimizing clipping planes for close-view rendering. The BEV and regionalzoom cameras use a 90◦ horizontal field of view, whereas
render_view uses a 100◦ horizontal field of view with
the camera placed 1.5 m above the floor.
Fixed-View SFT Baseline. We initialize Qwen3-VL-8BSFT from Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct and fine-tune it on all
27,674 question–answer pairs in the H2 U3D training split.
For each HM3D scene, we use Habitat-Sim to pre-render a
scene-level pool of first-person RGB views. After sorting the
rendered frames by filename, we uniformly select 32 frames
using ik = ⌊k(N − 1)/31⌋, where N is the number of
available frames and k = 0, . . . , 31. The same 32 views are
shared by all questions associated with a scene, making the
visual input scene-dependent but query-independent. During both training and evaluation, the model receives these
views together with the question and four answer choices
in a single turn. It has no access to BEVs, zoom_in,
or render_view, and cannot acquire additional observations. The same view-selection procedure is applied to the
test scenes.

Additional Experiment Details

To further validate the generalizability of our approach,
we evaluate SpatialReasoner on two existing HM3D-based
benchmarks, MT-HM3D (Zhai et al. 2025) and HM-EQA
(Ren et al. 2024). Since these benchmarks contain singlefloor scenarios, we adapt our method by providing only the
bird’s-eye view of the relevant floor to ensure answer uniqueness and fair comparison.
SpatialReasoner achieves accuracies of 0.479 on MTHM3D and 0.668 on HM-EQA, outperforming the strongest
compared baselines by 4.8 and 3.3 percentage points, respectively. These results demonstrate the generalizability of our
coarse-to-fine active exploration paradigm across established
HM3D-based question-answering settings.

E.1

Real-World Reconstruction Details

To evaluate SpatialReasoner beyond simulated HM3D environments, we reconstruct two physical indoor spaces—an
office and a laboratory—each covering more than 100 m2 .
We capture the environments as videos using a GoPro camera
and extract RGB frames for reconstruction. Camera poses are

estimated using COLMAP (Schonberger and Frahm 2016),
after which separate 3D Gaussian Splatting models are optimized using gsplat (Ye et al. 2024). The reconstructed
scenes support novel-view synthesis from arbitrary camera
poses. We generate the initial BEV representation by rendering each reconstruction from an overhead camera and expose
the resulting maps through the same hierarchical spatial-tool
interface used for HM3D.

Prompt for QA Generation
You are an expert in generating questions about details in a house. You will be given multiple top-down views of the
house. Each of them indicates a floor; the floor index starts from 0.
## Your Task
You should explore the house using a TWO-STEP process:
1. **First**: Use ‘zoom_in‘ to focus on a region of interest (a room or specific area)
2. **Second**: Use ‘render_view‘ to obtain a first-person view from within that zoomed region
3. **Finally**: Generate a question about object details based on BOTH the zoomed topdown view and the first-person
rendered view
## Tool Usage Rules
### zoom_in Tool
- **Purpose**: Focus on a specific region of the floor (e.g., a room, a corner, a functional area)
- **REQUIRED parameter**: You MUST specify ‘floor_idx‘ to indicate which floor to zoom into
- **Can be used multiple times**: You can zoom into different regions or floors as needed
- **When to use**: Use this FIRST before calling render_view
### render_view Tool
- **Purpose**: Get a first-person view from a specific position and camera angle
- **Coordinates**: Position is relative to the CURRENT zoomed topdown view (after zoom_in)
- **DO NOT specify floor_idx**: The view will automatically be rendered within the current zoomed region
- **Can be used multiple times**: You can render from different positions/angles if needed
- **When to use**: Use this AFTER you have zoomed into a region with zoom_in
### IMPORTANT CONSTRAINT
- You MUST call ‘zoom_in‘ at least once BEFORE calling ‘render_view‘ for the first time. This ensures you are
focusing on a valuable region before exploring it from first-person perspective.
## Exploration Strategy
**Step 1 - Choose a Region**:
- Look at the topdown view(s) and identify an interesting area
- Consider rooms with furniture, objects, or unique layouts
- Choose a region that likely contains details worth asking about
**Step 2 - Zoom In**:

**Step 3 - Render First-Person View**:

## Question Generation Guidelines
Your questions should focus on **details and object characteristics**:

### ENCOURAGED Question Types:
- **Object details**: Colors, materials, textures, states
- **Small objects**: Items that might be overlooked or recently moved
- **Object positions**: Where specific items are located
- **Misplaced objects**: Items in unusual or temporary locations
- **Counting**: Number of specific items (chairs, bottles, etc.)
- **Spatial relationships**: What’s next to, above, or near something else
### Quality Requirements:
**(1) Avoid ambiguity**:
- If asking about "the bed", ensure there’s only one bed visible
- Or specify which bed (e.g., "the bed against the north wall")
**(2) Focus on observable details**:
- Questions should be answerable from the rendered images
- Emphasize small objects, colors, materials, positions
- Look for items the homeowner might have recently moved or placed
**(3) Provide context**:
- Help ground the question in a specific location
**(4) Verify correctness**:
- Confirm the question is clear
- Ensure the correct answer is unambiguous
- Check that all choices make sense
**(5) Use environmental descriptions instead of room names**:
- **AVOID** directly mentioning room names (kitchen, bedroom, bathroom, garage, etc.)
- **INSTEAD** describe the environment and surrounding objects to locate the target
- Examples:
- × "In the kitchen, what’s on the microwave?"
- ✓ "What’s on the microwave near the refrigerator and stove?"

- ✓ "How many wooden chairs are in the area with tools and a parked vehicle?"
