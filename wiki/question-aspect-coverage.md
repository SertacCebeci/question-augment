# Question-aspect coverage

A reference-free way to ask whether the retrieved evidence, and separately the answer, reach what the question needs. First a short list of atomic aspects is derived from the question text alone. Then each aspect is checked twice: against the retrieved set, and against the answer. The same list is reused for both checks, so retrieval and generation are scored on one denominator.[^c1]

## Mechanism

Retrieval coverage is the share of aspects that are supported by at least one retrieved document. Answer completeness is the share of the same aspects that the answer explicitly addresses, optionally with a short supporting snippet.[^c2] Both are counts divided by the size of the aspect list. The aspect list is what makes them comparable: the retrieval side and the answer side are asked about the same units, and neither needs a gold passage set or a gold answer to be computed. The authors state that the retrieval measure "operates directly on the question and retrieved documents, making it usable even when references are unavailable."[^c3]

Read together, the two shares localize a gap. If retrieval covers an aspect that the answer omits, the shortfall sits on the generation side (my inference from the two definitions, not a result the paper tests). If neither covers it, the shortfall sits in retrieval. The answer-side measure is described as capturing "how thoroughly the answer resolves the information needs expressed by the question."[^c4]

The relevance side of the same family is a different quantity. Topical relevance does not track whether an answer is complete, which is why an aspect-level check is needed at all (see [[relevance-completeness-divergence]]). Pre-written criteria, as in [[threshold-gated-rubric-coverage]], fix the aspect list in advance by an expert; here the list is produced at scoring time from the question, so the derivation step carries the whole burden. [[dependency-aware-checklist-scoring]] takes a third route, refusing credit for a conclusion whose prerequisites fail.

## What was measured

The authors build 100 synthetic question, context and answer triplets from StrategyQA.[^c5] Each question has five answer variants, ideal, partial, unclear, off-topic and hallucinated, so the set spans known failure modes. On this set the mean retrieval coverage is about 0.50,[^c7] and the mean answer completeness is low, 0.12.[^c6] These are descriptive averages over a constructed set. They show that the measure produces spread, not that it is correct.

## Limits

- The aspect list is generated by a model from the question. The paper calls it "a small set of atomic aspects" but reports no check that the list is complete, stable across runs, or matches what a human would name. The run-to-run noise documented in [[llm-judge-score-instability]] would apply to the derivation as well, and nothing read here measures it.
- Coverage is binary per aspect: an aspect counts if it is "supported by at least one retrieved document." One fragile passage and a well-corroborated one count the same, and redundancy is invisible.
- The aspect count is not reported in the parts read. With a short list each aspect is a large share of the score, so a single disputed aspect can move the result a lot (my inference).
- No human completeness labels appear in the parts read, so the two shares have no external reference against which their values are checked.
- The data are synthetic, with answer quality injected by construction. Behaviour on organic deployed answers is not shown.
