Abstract
Large language models demonstrate strong reasoning capabilities through chain-of-thought
prompting, but whether this reasoning quality transfers across languages remains underexplored. We introduce a human-validated framework to evaluate whether model-generated reasoning traces logically support their conclusions across languages. Analyzing 65k reasoning traces from GlobalMMLU questions across
6 languages and 6 frontier models, we uncover
a critical blind spot: while models achieve high
task accuracy, their reasoning can fail to support their conclusions. Reasoning traces in nonLatin scripts show at least twice as much misalignment between their reasoning and conclusions than those in Latin scripts. We develop
an error taxonomy through human annotation
to characterize these failures, finding they stem
primarily from evidential errors (unsupported
claims, ambiguous facts) followed by illogical reasoning steps. Our findings demonstrate
that current multilingual evaluation practices
provide an incomplete picture of model reasoning capabilities and highlight the need for
reasoning-aware evaluation frameworks.

Introduction

Reasoning models have demonstrated remarkable
reasoning capabilities through the use of Chain-ofThought (CoT) prompting, achieving strong performance across several tasks (Arcuschin et al.,
2025; Lai and Nissim, 2024; Barua et al., 2025).
However, these reasoning capabilities exhibit significant disparities across languages, with models
performing best for high resource languages(Shi
et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2023; Lai and Nissim, 2024).
While multilingual evaluation typically focuses on
answer accuracy, we argue this provides an incomplete picture of model capabilities. A model might
produce correct answers through flawed reasoning

Understanding this distinction is crucial for assessing whether models truly transfer reasoning skills
across languages or simply memorize.
To address this, we propose a framework
that evaluates whether model-generated reasoning traces logically support their conclusions
across languages, measuring crosslingual reasoning–answer alignment. We hypothesize that models exhibit different reasoning processes depending
on language resource level and script type, with differences in how they access and use factual knowledge to support their conclusions. We isolate reasoning traces from their context: evaluators assess
whether reasoning steps alone provide sufficient
justification to reach the model’s conclusion, without seeing the model’s final answer. Through this
human annotation process, we develop an error
taxonomy that captures common reasoning failures, which then informs our automated evaluation
approach that we validate against the human judgments. We apply this framework to analyze reasoning traces across five languages with different

resource levels and scripts using the Global MMLU
dataset. We address three key research questions:
(RQ1) How does reasoning-answer misalignment
vary between high/low resource (or Latin vs. nonLatin script languages)? (RQ2) Can automatic evaluation reliably assess reasoning trace quality compared to human judgment? (RQ3) What are the
primary failure modes in multilingual reasoning
traces?
Our evaluation framework reveals a interesting finding: While answer accuracy predictably
dips from high to low-resource languages (80% ->
70%), reasoning-answer alignment disproportionately drops over two-fold. In other words, models
on average, produced LRL reasoning traces that
logically support those conclusions—getting the
right answer for the wrong reasons. We found that
lower-resourced languages were able can reach
correct conclusions through reasoning that does
not logically support those conclusions, getting the
right answer for the wrong reasons. We validate
that automated evaluation correlates strongly with
human judgment (RQ2), enabling systematic analysis of reasoning failures. Critically, this misalignment occurs both when models answer correctly
and incorrectly, and varies significantly between
Latin and non-Latin script languages (RQ1), indicating systematic issues with reasoning processes
rather than simple knowledge gaps.
Our key contributions are: (1) evidence of systematic reasoning-answer misalignment in multilingual settings, with alignment dropping twice as
much for non-latin scripts than latin-scripts; (2)
human-validated evaluation framework for evaluating reasoning coherence at scale; (3) analysis
showing misalignment occurs independently of answer correctness; and (4) characterization of how
reasoning failures are distributed across language
scripts and subjects.
These findings reveal that answer accuracy provides an incomplete picture of multilingual model
capabilities. The reasoning-answer misalignment
we identify suggests that models may be learning different problem-solving strategies across languages rather than a learning a uniform and coherent reasoning process. Our results indicate that
reasoning quality assessment requires separate evaluation from answer correctness in multilingual settings.

Related Works

Multilingual reasoning evaluation Benchmarking multilingual reasoning has largely centered
on mathematical domains, with datasets such as
MGSM (Shi et al., 2022), MathOctopus (Chen
et al., 2024), XCOT (Chai et al., 2025), PolyMath
(Wang et al., 2025), and MMATH (Luo et al., 2025),
where step-by-step solutions can be formally validated. While mathematical reasoning benefits from
clear symbolic representations and verifiable intermediate steps, other domains lack established
frameworks for assessing reasoning quality across
languages. Broader multilingual evaluation such as
SeaEval (Wang et al., 2024), M3Exam (Zhang et al.,
2023), and forms of multilingual value alignment
(Xu et al., 2024; Rystrøm et al., 2025) evaluate general knowledge and culturally situated reasoning.
Yet, these works largely equate multilingual reasoning evaluation with performance on reasoningintensive tasks, judged by whether models reach
correct answers, rather than by whether their reasoning processes actually support stated conclusions.

Consistency and faithfulness in LLM reasoning
Recent work has highlighted persistent issues with
the faithfulness of LLM reasoning, particularly for
English inputs. Studies show that chain-of-thought
traces often diverge from genuine problem-solving
processes (Turpin et al., 2023), with models producing post-hoc rationalizations rather than faithful reasoning explanations (Zhang et al., 2024).
These shortcomings manifest both in controlled
counterfactual settings (Lanham et al., 2023) and
in real-world scenarios (Arcuschin et al., 2025).
To address consistency, Wan et al. (2024) propose
the RASC framework for evaluating reasoning coherence and guiding early stopping and rationale
selection, while Liu et al. (2024) analyze step-level
self-contradictions on ambiguous questions. In contrast, our focus lies on reasoning trace inference:
whether model-generated traces logically support
their conclusions in non-ambiguous settings, and
crucially, how this differs between non-English languages and language resource level. Moreover,
research that enables automatic prediction of reasoning process failure modes remains understudied.

3

Methodology

We introduce a framework for systematically evaluating reasoning-answer alignment across languages
without requiring gold standard reasoning traces.
Our approach centers on a key insight: by isolating
reasoning content from conclusions, we can assess
whether a model’s reasoning actually supports its
stated answer, which is crucial for developing trustworthy multilingual models.
3.1

Evaluation Framework

Given a model that generates reasoning trace T in
language ℓ concluding with answer amodel , we first
back-translate non-English responses to English
using a top-performing translator for our target languages.0 We then create a truncated trace T ′ by
removing the final answer, allowing us to evaluate what conclusion the reasoning itself supports.
Human annotators or automated evaluators assess
these truncated traces, selecting from the original
multiple-choice options or marking cases as “inconclusive” when the reasoning provides insufficient
evidence.
We evaluate each response along two dimensions: (1) Correctness: Whether the model’s final
answer matches the gold label, and (2) Consistency: Whether the reasoning trace logically supports the model’s stated conclusion. These dimensions can be broken down into the cases detailed in
Table 1
Our primary metric is the Trace Inconsistency
Rate (TIR), the proportion of cases where reasoning fails to support the model’s conclusion, regardless of answer correctness. Additionally, our human evaluation protocol assesses reasoning quality
through supplementary measures including logical
coherence and information sufficiency.

3.2

Experimental Setup

Data and Languages We conduct our evaluation
on GlobalMMLU (Singh et al., 2024), focusing on
7 fact-retrieval centric domains: global facts, high
school history (European, US, World), government
and politics, and prehistory.
We select languages of varying resource levels
and scripts: English, Spanish, Hindi, and Arabic
(higher-resourced, HRL), and Ukrainian and Korean (lower-resourced, LRL). For script-based analysis, English and Spanish use Latin script (LS),
while Hindi, Arabic, Ukrainian, and Korean use
non-Latin scripts (NLS).
Models and Prompting We evaluate six
state-of-the-art instruction-tuned models from two
model families: Llama-4-Scout-Instruct,
Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Qwen3-32B,
Qwen3-32B-thinking, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct,
and Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct.
All models use chain-of-thought prompting (i.e.,
“Let’s think step by step”) with structured answer
extraction via <answer></answer> tags to ensure
consistent outputs across languages and models.
More details on the extraction pipeline can be
found in the appendix §C.1.
Translation and Backtranslation Quality Control To enable systematic cross-language comparison, we back-translate all non-English model
responses to English using Command-R+, which
demonstrates top performance on WMT benchmarks for our target languages (Kocmi et al., 2024).
We also manually validate translation quality on a
small sample of translations, with more details in
the appendix §C.2.

4

Evaluation Validation

We validate our evaluation approach through systematic comparison of human and automated assessment across languages and reasoning types.

4.1

Human Evaluation Results

Our human evaluation establishes both the reliability of our annotation framework and reveals systematic patterns in reasoning inconsistencies across
languages. We achieve substantial inter-annotator
agreement on answer selection (κ = 0.7, 80%
agreement), with fair consistency on logical coherence assessment (κ = 0.39, 69.7% agreement)
and moderate-to-strong agreement on information
sufficiency (κ = 0.53, 79% agreement). These
results validate that our evaluation protocol captures consistent human judgments about reasoning
quality.
Through thematic analysis of the 72 annotated
instances, we identify four primary categories of
reasoning inconsistencies that emerge systematically across languages and models (Table 2). These
failure modes, derived inductively from human observation, reveal how models systematically fail to
align their reasoning processes with stated conclusions.

5

Results

As shown in Table 4, both accuracy and trace inconsistency rates reveal considerable cross-lingual
variation, but capture distinct aspects of reasoning quality. Accuracy ranges substantially across
languages, where English peaks at 87-89% while
Hindi and Korean drop (70-81%), and seem consistent with language hierarchies reflective of current
literature on language resourcing: English > Spanish > Ukrainian/Arabic > Hindi/Korean (CostaJussà et al., 2022). However, TIR patterns diverge
from accuracy trends, exposing reasoning breakdowns that final answer correctness can obscure.
For instance, while Qwen3-32B maintains reasonable accuracy in Korean (78.4%), its TIR jumps
to 9.81%, which is over 4× higher than English
(2.16%). Similarly, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct exhibits
13.3% Korean TIR despite 77% accuracy, meaning

1-in-8 responses contain reasoning that contradicts
its final answer. Interestingly though, Qwen3-32Bthinking consistently outperforms its non-thinking
version across languages, achieving 0.91% English
TIR versus 2.16% for the base model, reflecting
clear gains of extended reasoning.
Latin script languages (LS) outperform nonLatin script languages (NLS) by 4-6 percentage
points in both accuracy and TIR, twice the gap observed between high and low-resource languages!
This pattern seems to hold across models, indicating systematic script-based biases in reasoning
quality are stronger than resource availability effects in this study. We detail explicit examples of
reasoning trace failures detected by our evaluating
framework in section C.2 and section C.2. Furthermore, we provide a subject-level analysis of TIR in
Table 10 and Table 9.

5.2

Analysis of Automated Human-Derived
Error Taxonomy

Our automated evaluation framework, employing
the human-derived error taxonomy described in
§3.3, reveals systematic differences in error patterns across language resource levels (Figure 2).
Unsupported claims and ambiguous facts constitute the majority of detected errors across all evaluated models (23% and 24%respectively), while
illogical leaps account for 18% of flagged instances.
Qwen3-32B-thinking demonstrates the largest resource gap, with 27% of responses flagged for unsupported claims in LRL settings compared to 18%
in HRL settings.
The prevalence of evidential errors (unsupported
claims, ambiguous facts) over logical contradictions seems to indicate that models struggle more
with grounding their reasoning in verifiable information than with maintaining internal logical consistency.
Expanding into long-CoT paradigms with thinking models, while they show better consistency
overall (as seen in section 5), the substantial jump
in unsupported claims for low-resource languages
may hint at potential trade-offs in extended reasoning traces, where while beneficial for complex
problems, may drift from well-supported claims
when operating with limited linguistic knowledge.
Moving forward, we encourage further evaluation
on thinking models in this setting.

6

Conclusion

In this work we introduced an automatic evaluation framwork to assess reasoning quality through
misalignment between trace and answer in a multilingual setting. We find that models, even when
seemingly similar in accuracy, reflect a varying

degree of reasoning trace fidelity. While lowerresourced languages are expected to perform worse
than higher-resourced languages, accuracy on its
own underestimates reasoning trace quality, with
languages of non-latin script reflecting twice as
many reasoning contradictions than their latin counterparts. These findings also motivate a closer look
at thinking models, namely, to what extent they
can maintain factual grounding in more resourceconstrained language settings. Moving forward,
our evaluation framework can serve to help diagnose reasoning trace errors in order to ultimately
improve multilingual reasoning capabilities.

Limitations
While our evaluation framework can be employed
across other datasets that requiring reasoning, we
only test over global mmlu and small sample of
boolean comparative questions (Arcuschin et al.,
2025). We conduct human annotations over a limited number of samples, which can be scaled up
in a future iteration. Furthermore, we only used
one judge to assess reasoning traces, where moving forward we can move to using majority voting
over 3 judges for robust outputs. Evaluation can
also take place over a wider set of languages that
are even lower-resourced than those initially selected, though our initial set serves for script diversity. Translators can also be updated to models like
gpt-4o and double-checked by additional native
speakers across selected languages.

All models generate 4096 tokens to respond to each
question in global mmlu. We follow each model’s
recommended sampling parameters. If none is
available, we proceed with nucleus sampling with
top-p=0.95. To account for differences in tokenization across languages, we allot a larger set of tokens
for the translator during backtranslation from the
original language to english, 8192. Backtranslation
is done with temperature set to 0 and top-p set to 1.
Finally, the judge will generates 8192 tokens, and
we keep a large window size of 32768 to account
for the detailed ai evaluator prompt and the reasoning trace to evaluate. To encourage deterministic
output was also set temperature set to 0 and top-p
set to 1.
C.2

Translation Quality Check

We run a small annotation to evaluate the command r plus quality of backtranslation. We employ
a native spanish speaker to annotate 30 spanish examples backtranslated to english, and find that only
2/30 examples were marked for errors in translation.
