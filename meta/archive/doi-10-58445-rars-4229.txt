Abstract
Intelligent tutoring systems automate the adaptation of mathematics instruction for learners. This
paper introduces BR-Tutor, a multi-agent mathematics tutoring architecture designed to ground
a Tutor Agent in authentic pedagogical discourse rather than static textbook content. While large
language models excel at mathematical problem-solving, their default disposition is to supply
answers prematurely, which undermines the Socratic guided-discovery process essential to
effective tutoring. To bridge this gap, the proposed system enhances an existing
LangGraph-based architecture with a threshold-gated behavioral retrieval module. This module
indexes 3,375 talk-move-annotated tutor-student exchanges from the Eedi QATD-2k corpus and
injects the closest matching historical tutor response into the agent's prompt to model
appropriate pedagogical strategies. Deployed on open-weight language models via the Groq
inference platform, BR-Tutor was evaluated on a 50-problem sample from the standard
MathDial benchmark. The system achieved a 94% task-success rate (Success@5) alongside a
46% answer disclosure rate (Telling@5), with both metrics rapidly saturating by the second
conversational turn. Ultimately, this research highlights a persistent dissociation between raw
mathematical correctness and necessary pedagogical restraint in open-weight models,
emphasizing the critical role of behavioral grounding in intelligent tutoring systems.
Keywords: Intelligent tutoring systems, LLMs, multi-agent systems, RAG, dialogue tutoring, talk
moves
I. Introduction
Mathematics instruction depends on adaptation to individual learners, and intelligent tutoring
systems have long sought to automate that adaptation. Large language models (LLMs) make
open-ended pedagogical dialogue tractable, but they also sharpen a design problem that
predates them. A model optimized to be helpful supplies the answer by default, while
guided-discovery tutoring including Socratic questioning, scaffolding, and hinting, requires the
opposite disposition. This gap is not closed by scale alone.
The stakes of this design problem are well established. Bloom reported that one-to-one tutoring
produced gains of roughly two standard deviations over conventional classroom instruction [1],
1

and although later meta-analysis places the effect of human tutoring closer to 0.8 standard
deviations, it attributes much of that effect to the fine-grained interactivity of tutorial dialogue
rather than to the delivery of answers [2]. Scaffolding, in which the tutor controls the elements of
a task that exceed the learner's current capacity while leaving the rest to the learner [3], and
student self-explanation elicited by tutor questioning [4] are central to that interactivity, and a
period of productive struggle before instruction can itself deepen learning [5]. Recent field
evidence shows the cost of abandoning these principles: in a randomized study with nearly a
thousand high-school mathematics students, unconstrained access to GPT-4 improved practice
performance but reduced subsequent unassisted performance, while a tutor designed with
safeguards largely avoided this harm [6]. The disposition toward answer disclosure is partly a
by-product of instruction tuning, which optimizes models to satisfy user requests directly [7], and
surveys of LLM use in education identify it, together with student over-reliance, as a central risk
[8].
Benchmarks for dialogue tutoring operationalize this tension through two complementary
metrics. The first metric, Success@K, is the proportion of simulated dialogues in which the
student reaches the correct answer within K turns, and the second, Telling@K, is the proportion
in which the tutor discloses the answer within K turns [9]. An effective guided tutor maximizes
the former while minimizing the latter. The metrics are not redundant, since the trivial strategy of
stating the answer immediately maximizes both at once.
Chudziak and Kostka [10] address this problem with a multi-agent architecture built on
LangGraph, comprising a Tutor Agent orchestrating specialist components through a
ReAct-style loop [11], a dual-memory framework separating persistent student modeling from
session context, a GraphRAG [12] retrieval layer over textbook material, and a pipeline of
agents that assemble directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) for course planning. Their evaluation
shows that a pedagogically-informed “Tutor Prompt” substantially outperforms a naive baseline
prompt on both metrics, for both GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini.
The architecture grounds the tutor in what is true, the curricular content of a textbook, but
carries no information about how an effective tutor speaks. The discourse moves that
distinguish skilled human tutoring, such as pressing a student for reasoning or revoicing a
partially formed idea, are not recoverable from expository prose. This paper extends the
architecture with behavioral retrieval. The textbook knowledge graph is replaced with an index
over real, talk-move-annotated tutor–student exchanges drawn from the Eedi QATD-2k corpus
[13], so that retrieval supplies pedagogical form rather than propositional content alone.

Rather than answering the student's implicit request directly, the tutor turn presses the student

interaction. Following are the contributions of the proposed work:
●​ An extension of the Chudziak–Kostka multi-agent architecture, adapted to open-weight
models served through the Groq inference platform, together with a characterization of
the structured-output failures this adaptation exposes and the prompt-level remedies that
resolve them.
●​ Behavioral retrieval: A FAISS-indexed [14], threshold-gated retrieval module built over
sentence embeddings [15] of annotated tutor–student dialogue, which injects real tutor
responses and their talk-move labels as few-shot context into the Tutor Agent.
●​ An evaluation on a standard dialogue-tutoring benchmark, showing a Success@5 of 94%
alongside a Telling@5 of 46% — a profile compared directly against the original
architecture and against other reported baselines on the same benchmark.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section II reviews related work on
intelligent tutoring systems, LLMs in education, multi-agent architectures, RAG, and discourse
analysis of tutoring dialogue. Section III presents the proposed mechanism, BR-Tutor, including
its architecture, its behavioral-retrieval extension, datasets, and the metrics used to evaluate it.
Section IV reports the experimental setup, results, a comparison against existing mechanisms,
and a discussion of the findings. Section V concludes the paper and outlines directions for future
work.
II. Related Work
In this section, we review the bodies of work that motivate the proposed mechanism, namely
foundational and human-modeled intelligent tutoring systems, LLMs applied to education,
multi-agent architectures, RAG, mathematical reasoning in LLMs, exemplar retrieval for
in-context learning, and the use of dialogue and discourse data to directly study tutoring
behavior.
3

A. Foundational and Human-Modeled Intelligent Tutoring Systems
AutoTutor [16] simulates a human tutor through scripted natural-language dialogue built around
a curriculum of deep-reasoning questions, and has produced measurable learning gains across
more than a decade of controlled trials.
ASSISTments [17] takes a different approach, pairing an online homework and
formative-assessment platform with a testbed for randomized controlled experiments run at the
scale of thousands of students. Both systems demonstrate the value of structured tutorial
interaction, but neither incorporates a large language model: their pedagogical content is
authored in advance rather than generated or retrieved dynamically.
Motivating the broader case for adaptive pedagogy, van Hoeve et al. [18] study how a
growth-mindset framing in secondary mathematics classrooms affects student engagement,
underscoring that the manner of instructional feedback, not only its correctness, shapes learning
outcomes. CLASS [19] moves closer to the LLM era, fine-tuning a language model on curated
scaffolding and conversational datasets to produce SPOCK, a proof-of-concept biology tutor
evaluated through expert qualitative review of its responses.
This line of work also established the student-modeling machinery that the architecture
extended here inherits. Cognitive Tutors combined production-rule models of domain skill with
model tracing [20] and demonstrated measurable gains in urban classrooms [21]; knowledge
tracing formalized the estimation of skill mastery from observed responses [22], and was later
recast with recurrent neural networks [23]. On the dialogue side, analyses of naturalistic
one-to-one tutoring identified a recurring five-step frame of tutor question, student answer, tutor
feedback, collaborative improvement of the answer, and a check of understanding [24], which
AutoTutor then operationalized across nearly two decades of development [25]. The long-term
memory component used in this paper plays the role that these student models played in
classical ITS, although it stores learner attributes as natural-language records rather than as
probabilistic skill estimates.
B. Large Language Models (LLMs) for Education
Several surveys chart how LLMs have been adopted across educational settings. Kumar [26]
reviews LLM architectures and deployment frameworks with an eye toward emerging application
domains, and Kasneci et al. [8] survey applications of LLMs specifically in education, cataloging
their use for content generation, assessment, and tutoring.
Wang et al. [27] complement this with a thematic analysis of undergraduates' own perceptions
of AI in personalized learning environments, and Mishra et al. [28] document adoption patterns
among global clinical researchers, illustrating how quickly LLMs have entered research and
academic workflows beyond the classroom.

Closer to tutoring specifically, Chen et al. [29] chain multiple LLMs together to deliver private
tutoring support, and Jill Watson [30] deploys a ChatGPT-backed virtual teaching assistant at
4

scale, using retrieval over course materials and a skill-classifier architecture to answer real
student questions in production classrooms.
Park et al. [31] present a conversation-based tutoring system that builds an explicit model of the
student across a session, and Liu et al. [32] extend tutoring beyond mathematics with a
multi-modal scaffolding system for language learning, both reinforcing that session-level
personalization is a recurring design target across subject domains.
Several studies probe the pedagogical quality of LLM tutors directly. Tack and Piech [33]
compare model-generated and human teacher replies in educational dialogue and find that the
models trail human teachers in helpfulness to the student, and Wang et al. [34] show that
eliciting expert decision-making, namely identifying the error, choosing a remediation strategy,
and stating an intention, improves LLM responses to student mathematics mistakes. Benchmark
evaluation reaches a similar conclusion at scale: across fourteen leading models,
EduGuardBench finds that pedagogical incompetence is the dominant failure mode of LLMs
acting as teachers [35]. Deployment studies extend this picture beyond the laboratory: Jill
Watson has been scaled across online classrooms [36], and the randomized study of Bastani et
al. [6] shows that a guardrailed tutor preserves learning that an unconstrained one erodes.
Training-based approaches pursue the same goal from another direction: multi-horizon
preference optimization refines tutor agents in a simulated teacher–student environment,
rewarding both turn-level pedagogical quality and trajectory-level effectiveness [37]. Together,
these results motivate treating answer restraint as a primary design objective rather than a
secondary matter of style.
C. Multi-Agent Architectures
Multi-agent decomposition has emerged as a way to assign specialized reasoning to distinct
components rather than overloading a single prompt. Chudziak and Kostka [10], the architecture
extended in this paper, coordinate tutoring, memory, task creation, and course planning as
distinct agents in a LangGraph state machine. The same coordination pattern recurs outside
education: ElliottAgents [38] applies a multi-agent, natural-language-driven pipeline to stock
market analysis, and a related agile-software-engineering system [39] distributes planning and
coding responsibilities across LLM-augmented agents. Kostka and Chudziak [40] examine how
logical reasoning, long-term memory, and inter-agent collaboration interact within multi-agent
LLM systems more generally, work that directly informs the dual-memory design reused here.
Viswanathan et al. [41] apply a multi-agent approach to online education specifically, showing
that agent decomposition is a recurring, cross-domain strategy for managing the complexity of
adaptive, multi-component systems rather than a design choice specific to tutoring.
Beyond education, generative agents equipped with memory, reflection, and planning produce
believable long-horizon behavior [42], and role-specialized agent teams structure software
development through standardized operating procedures [43] and chat-chain communication
between agents [44]. These systems share with the tutoring architecture a reliance on
structured inter-agent messages, which makes them sensitive to the reliability of structured
output discussed in Section III-B.

5

D. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and Knowledge Grounding
GraphRAG [12] represents a text corpus as a knowledge graph, pre-summarizing communities
of related entities so that global, corpus-level questions can be answered without exhaustively
searching every passage; the original tutoring architecture adopts this approach for textbook
grounding.
Dense retrieval over learned embeddings [45] and retrieval-augmented generation [46]
established the now-standard pattern of conditioning a generator on passages selected by
vector similarity; GraphRAG extends this pattern with graph structure aimed at global,
corpus-level questions rather than narrow fact lookup. Li et al. [47] apply retrieval-augmented
generation to education directly, using textbook content as context to link diagnostic
assessments to curriculum goals. Across this body of work, retrieval supplies factual or
curricular content; none of it addresses the separate question of which discourse strategy a tutor
should use once the relevant content has been retrieved, which is the gap the present extension
targets.
E. Mathematical Reasoning in Large Language Models
A tutor's ability to guide a student presupposes that the underlying model can solve the problem
itself. Guan et al. [48] show that small language models can achieve strong mathematical
reasoning through self-evolved deep thinking, and Trinh et al. [49] demonstrate olympiad-level
geometry problem solving without human demonstrations. Neither system is a tutor, but both
establish that open-weight and smaller models are increasingly viable for the mathematical
reasoning a tutoring system depends on, which motivates evaluating open-weight models in the
tutoring role directly rather than assuming only the largest closed models are competent
problem solvers.
F. Exemplar Retrieval for In-Context Learning
Behavioral retrieval is, mechanically, a form of demonstration selection for in-context learning.
Large language models can adapt to a task from a handful of demonstrations supplied in the
prompt [50], and demonstrations of intermediate reasoning can further shape how a model
approaches a problem [51]. Which demonstrations are supplied matters: retrievers that select
demonstrations resembling the current input improve over random selection, and a retriever
trained for this purpose improves further [52]. At the same time, analyses of in-context learning
indicate that models draw heavily on the format, input distribution, and label space of
demonstrations rather than on their exact input-output mapping [53]. The latter finding is directly
relevant here, because the purpose of an injected tutor exemplar is to convey the form of a
pedagogical move rather than a specific answer. It also implies a risk that the paper returns to in
Section IV: a single demonstration may be too weak a signal to override a model's default
response style.
G. Tutor Discourse, Talk Moves, and Dialogue Benchmarks
MathDial [9] grounds tutoring dialogues in math word problems and supplies the Success@K
and Telling@K protocol used throughout this paper; Macina et al. [54] separately survey the
6

broader opportunities and challenges facing neural dialog tutoring. A parallel line of work studies
classroom and tutoring discourse directly.
Suresh et al. [55] introduce the TalkMoves dataset, annotating whole-classroom lesson
transcripts for ten discursive moves based on accountable-talk theory; because that corpus
captures one-to-many classroom lectures rather than one-to-one tutoring, it is better suited to
studying teacher discourse at the class level than to grounding a one-on-one tutoring agent. The
Eedi QATD-2k corpus [13] instead annotates real, one-to-one, chat-based tutor–student
exchanges anchored to individual mathematics problems with talk-move labels, which is the
closer match to the one-on-one dialogue setting a tutoring agent operates in and is the corpus
this paper builds on.
The talk-move framework itself originates in accountable-talk theory, which characterizes
productive classroom discourse by its accountability to the learning community, to accurate
knowledge, and to accepted standards of reasoning [56]. Computational work has since
operationalized related constructs at scale, for example by measuring conversational uptake,
the extent to which a teacher builds on a student's contribution, in mathematics classroom
transcripts [57]. These measures motivate using talk-move labels as a retrieval key, because
they describe what a tutor turn does for the student rather than what it asserts.

III. Proposed Mechanism, BR-Tutor
This section presents BR-Tutor, the mechanism proposed in this paper. It builds directly on the
multi-agent architecture of Chudziak and Kostka [10], extends it with a behavioral-retrieval
module, and adapts it to run on open-weight models. This section covers the architecture, the
infrastructure adaptation, the behavioral-retrieval extension, the overall methodology as a
flowchart, and the datasets and metrics used to evaluate the resulting system.
A. Architecture Overview
BR-Tutor is a LangGraph state graph over five functional components. The TutorAgent is the
student-facing conversational agent and the entry point for each turn. The MemoryAgent is
implemented as a single parameterized class serving two roles: long-term memory (LTM),
holding persistent learner attributes such as prior knowledge, recurring misconceptions, and
stated preferences, and working memory (WM), holding session-local state such as the active
topic and current problem.
The MemoryDispatcherAgent determines, per turn, which memory store to read from and
which to update. The TaskCreationAgent produces personalized practice problems from a
topic and difficulty specification. A pipeline of Course Creation agents (Research → Planning
→ Step Handling → Coding) assembles DAG-structured learning paths. This architecture, and
the dual-memory design in particular, follows the multi-agent coordination pattern examined
more generally by Kostka and Chudziak [40].
B. Infrastructure Adaptation
Development proceeded under administratively restricted macOS privileges, precluding
system-level package management. Two consequences shaped the implementation. First,
Python 3.14 introduced a change to str-subclassed Enum formatting: implicit string conversion
no longer yields the member value, which silently corrupted role labels in downstream code that
relied on the legacy behavior until explicit __str__ overrides were added.
Second, the GraphRAG dependency chain proved incompatible with the available interpreter
and could not be installed. Rather than block on this, GraphRAG was replaced by a stub
preserving the call interface, allowing the remaining agents to be developed and evaluated
independently; this substitution is later made permanent by the behavioral-retrieval extension
9

described in Section III-C, but should be read as a constraint on the comparison this paper can
offer rather than a designed ablation.
The original implementation targets OpenAI models. This work adapts the full codebase to the
Groq inference platform via langchain-groq, motivated by cost and latency considerations. This
adaptation was not a substitution of endpoints. Four Groq-hosted models,
llama-3.3-70b-versatile, gpt-oss-120b, gpt-oss-20b, and qwen3.6-27b, were tested against
LangGraph's structured tool-calling interface, and all four failed in the same characteristic way:
rather than emitting a registered tool call, the model wrapped its intended JSON payload inside
an invented tool name that the graph could not route. The remedy was to remove the affected
agent from the tool-calling path entirely: the TaskCreationAgent now issues a plain llm.invoke()
call carrying explicit JSON formatting instructions in the prompt, and parses the reply with
json.loads() after stripping markdown code-fence artifacts. A related constraint applies to the
MemoryDispatcherAgent: Groq's JSON mode requires the literal token json to appear in the
prompt, and dispatch behavior was unreliable until this was satisfied explicitly.
The general principle drawn from this adaptation is narrow but practical. In multi-agent systems,
the reliability of structured output is a property of the model–framework pair, not of the
orchestration logic. Architectures validated against a single provider should not be assumed
portable without revalidation of every structured interface.
This observation is consistent with the broader tool-use literature, in which reliable API
invocation is treated as a capability that must be learned rather than assumed: Toolformer
teaches a model when and how to call tools through self-supervised annotation [58], and
ToolLLM constructs large instruction corpora over real-world APIs to obtain dependable function
calling [59]. Open-weight models served through a third-party endpoint inherit whatever
tool-calling behavior their training produced, and the failures reported here indicate that this
behavior should be verified per model before an agent graph depends on it.
C. Behavioral Retrieval Extension
The GraphRAG component of the original architecture grounds responses in textbook content.
BR-Tutor grounds them in tutoring behavior instead. The unit of retrieval is not a passage but an
exchange: given what the student just said, what did a real tutor say next, and what pedagogical
move were they making? This shifts retrieval from a content service to a behavioral prior.
The retrieval index is built from the Eedi QATD-2k corpus [13], in which individual messages
carry TalkMovePrediction labels including Press for Accuracy, Press for Reasoning, Revoicing,
Keep Together, and Getting Student to Relate. Approximately 55,000 raw message rows are
grouped by InterventionId, and each tutor turn is paired with the student turn immediately
preceding it. Because a large fraction of student turns are acknowledgements or otherwise
non-substantive, a content filter based on mathematical and reasoning keyword detection is
applied, retaining only exchanges in which the student utterance carries interpretable
mathematical content. This pipeline yields approximately 3,375 usable (student utterance, tutor
response, talk move) triples.

10

Student-side utterances are embedded with all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a sentence-transformer model
built on the Sentence-BERT framework [15] and distilled with MiniLM [60], and indexed in FAISS
[14] using IndexFlatIP over L2-normalized vectors, which makes inner-product search equivalent
to cosine similarity. The index and its metadata, the paired tutor response and talk-move label
for each entry, are persisted to disk so that inference requires no re-embedding of the corpus.
At each turn, the incoming student utterance is embedded and matched against the index.
Retrieval is threshold-gated: the nearest exemplar is injected into the Tutor Agent's prompt only
when cosine similarity exceeds 0.45. This threshold was set empirically by contrasting scores
for deliberately off-topic control queries, which clustered near 0.38, against on-topic queries,
which ranged from roughly 0.59 to 0.84. Gating matters because an ungated nearest neighbor is
always returned regardless of relevance; on genuinely novel student utterances, injecting a
poorly matched exemplar would supply misleading pedagogical framing rather than none.
Retrieval is invoked as a direct Python call inside the TutorAgent's response path, not as an
LLM-selected tool. This is a deliberate consequence of the tool-calling instability documented in
Section III-B: making injection a deterministic application-level decision removes the model's
opportunity to fail at deciding when to retrieve. The cost is that the tutor cannot elect to retrieve
on reasoning grounds unavailable to a similarity score; the benefit is that retrieval behavior is
reproducible and independently auditable.

D. Design Rationale and Trade-offs
Two design decisions in BR-Tutor are deliberate trade-offs rather than incidental workarounds,
and both constrain what the evaluation can show. First, retrieval is executed deterministically on
every student turn and gated by similarity, instead of being exposed as a tool that the Tutor
Agent may choose to call. Agentic tool selection would, in principle, let the tutor retrieve only
when it judges an exemplar useful; in practice, the tool-calling failures documented in Section
III-B made that decision unreliable on the evaluated models, and a retrieval step that silently
fails to fire would be indistinguishable in the metrics from one that fires and is ignored.
Deterministic invocation makes every retrieval event observable and loggable, which is the
property that the planned ablations in Section IV-F require. Second, BR-Tutor substitutes
behavioral grounding for content grounding rather than adding it alongside GraphRAG. The
evaluated system therefore has no textbook grounding at all, and its mathematical correctness
rests on the base model's own reasoning. This choice is appropriate for MathDial, whose word
problems are self-contained and require no curricular lookup, but it means that the comparison
with the original architecture is between different grounding sources, not between the presence
and absence of one. A factorial design that crosses content grounding with behavioral
grounding is the appropriate test of whether the two are complementary; it is specified in Table

E. Datasets and Evaluation Metrics
Two datasets are used, for two distinct purposes, and each is chosen for its fit to that purpose.
MathDial [9] grounds tutoring dialogues in multi-step math word problems and is used here as
the evaluation benchmark: it supplies a simulated-student protocol and the Success@K /
Telling@K metrics adopted throughout Section IV, and it is the same benchmark used to
evaluate the architecture this paper extends [10], which keeps the comparison in Section IV-C
on common ground.
Eedi QATD-2k [13] is used as the source corpus for behavioral retrieval, for a reason specific to
the tutoring setting this system operates in: unlike the TalkMoves dataset [55], which annotates
whole-classroom lecture transcripts, QATD-2k records one-to-one, chat-based tutoring
exchanges anchored to individual problems, which matches the one-on-one interaction
BR-Tutor's Tutor Agent conducts with a single student far more closely than a classroom
transcript would.
The evaluation metrics, Success@K and Telling@K, are used elsewhere beyond their
originating benchmark: Chudziak and Kostka [10] apply the same pair of metrics to evaluate
their GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini Tutor-Prompt conditions, which is what makes a direct numerical
comparison in Section IV-C possible. Reporting both metrics jointly, rather than either alone, is
what prevents a trivial always-answer strategy from appearing to succeed, since such a strategy
would maximize Success@K and Telling@K simultaneously.
IV. Performance Evaluation and Discussion of Results
The evaluation is organized around two research questions whose scope is deliberately limited
to what a single-configuration experiment can answer:
●​ RQ1. What task-success and answer-disclosure profile does BR-Tutor exhibit on
MathDial when deployed on an open-weight model?
●​ RQ2. How does that profile evolve across conversational turns, and at which turn is it
determined?
A third question, whether behavioral retrieval causes any difference in that profile, requires the
controlled ablation described in Section IV-F and is not answered by the present data.

D. Discussion
The central observation is that correctness and pedagogical restraint dissociate. On raw task
success, a 20B open-weight model deployed on commodity inference infrastructure is
competitive with the closed models reported in the original study, and converges faster. On
15

answer revelation it is not close. This is a useful result precisely because it is not a uniform
improvement or regression: it localizes the difference between model families to discourse style
rather than mathematical capability.
Several mechanisms plausibly contribute, and the present data distinguish them only partially.
The strongest candidate is the front-loading pattern visible in the K = 1 figures. A tutor that
disclosed answers as a fallback — after the student stalled — would produce a Telling curve
that rose across turns; this system's curve does not rise, so disclosure is an opening-move
disposition. This is consistent with instruction-tuned helpfulness overriding the prompt's Socratic
framing on the first response, before any evidence of student difficulty exists to scaffold against.
The behavioral-retrieval module is designed to counteract exactly this disposition by supplying a
concrete, real tutor response as few-shot context. The present evaluation cannot establish
whether it does so, because only the extended configuration was run; no ablation isolating
retrieval's contribution is available. This is stated plainly rather than attributing the observed
profile to the extension: the result characterizes the system as built, and does not decompose it.
A secondary consideration is the interaction between threshold gating and the saturation
pattern. Because the system's behavior is fixed by turn two, retrieval has at most two
opportunities to influence a dialogue, and on the opening turn the student utterance is typically a
restatement of the problem rather than a diagnostic expression of difficulty — the condition
under which the QATD-2k exemplars are most informative. Retrieval may therefore be arriving
after the turn at which it would matter most.
This interpretation is consistent with prior evidence. Instruction tuning rewards direct satisfaction
of the user's request [7], and demonstration studies suggest that in-context examples mostly
transmit format rather than overriding such dispositions [53]; a single retrieved exemplar is
therefore a weak counterweight to a strongly trained default. The practical implication, in line
with the guardrail findings of Bastani et al. [6], is that answer restraint is better enforced by
explicit first-turn constraints on what the tutor may reveal, or by optimizing the tutor directly for
pedagogical behavior [37], with retrieval supplying the form of the pedagogical move rather than
being relied on to suppress disclosure by itself.
E. Limitations
●​ Single condition. Only the extended system was evaluated. Without a matched run of
the unextended architecture on identical infrastructure, no claim about the effect of
behavioral retrieval on Success@K or Telling@K is supported by these data.
●​ Sample size. Fifty problems yield Wilson intervals spanning roughly 14 points for
Success and 27 points for Telling. Comparisons at the level of a few percentage points
are not resolvable at this n.
●​ Self-play. The same model plays tutor and student. Language models are known to
recognize and favor their own generations [62], and model-based evaluation more
generally carries systematic biases [63]; a simulated student that shares the tutor's
representations may therefore resolve terse or ambiguous tutor turns that a human
learner or a different student model would not, inflating Success@K. A recent systematic
evaluation of student simulation on real mathematics tutoring dialogues finds that
16

prompting-based simulators, the approach used here, perform poorly relative to
fine-tuned alternatives [64], reinforcing this caveat.
●​ Turn budget. A five-turn cap prevents direct comparison with results reported at K = 10,
although the observed saturation at K = 2 suggests the truncation is not materially
constraining for this system.
●​ No GraphRAG comparison. GraphRAG could not be installed in the available
environment. Behavioral retrieval therefore substitutes for a stubbed component rather
than competing against a functioning one.
●​ Heuristic filtering. Corpus construction relies on keyword-based detection of
content-rich student utterances, which may exclude valid pedagogical exchanges and
admit noise. The 0.45 similarity threshold was calibrated on a small set of control
comparisons and is not established as transferable.
●​ Simulated learners. As in the original study, no evaluation with human students was
conducted. Metric performance in simulation is not evidence of learning gains.

V. Conclusion and Future Work
This paper has extended a multi-agent mathematics tutoring architecture with behavioral
retrieval over talk-move-annotated tutor–student dialogue, and has adapted the resulting
system, BR-Tutor, to run on open-weight infrastructure. On a 50-problem MathDial sample, the
system has reached a Success@5 of 94% and a Telling@5 of 46%, with both metrics saturating
by the second turn, and this profile has been compared directly against the architecture's
original closed-model results and against other reported MathDial baselines.
The immediate priority for future work is the ablation this study lacks: a matched run with
retrieval disabled, and a third condition with gating removed, would separate the contribution of
the exemplars from that of the threshold. Second, given that disclosure is concentrated in the
opening turn, an intervention targeting the first response specifically, for instance, retrieving on
the problem statement rather than the student's initial utterance, is a more direct remedy than
turn-agnostic injection. Third, restoring a functional GraphRAG component would permit a
head-to-head comparison between content grounding and behavioral grounding, and would
allow testing whether the two are complementary. Finally, evaluation against a distinct
simulated-student model, and ultimately with human learners, is necessary before any of these
metrics can be read as evidence about instruction.
Beyond its specific numbers, the study offers a concrete observation for designers of LLM
tutors: on this benchmark, correctness and restraint are separable properties, and restraint is
decided in the opening turn. Retrieval of authentic tutor behavior is a plausible mechanism for
shaping that turn, and the protocol in Table VII provides the means to establish whether it does.
