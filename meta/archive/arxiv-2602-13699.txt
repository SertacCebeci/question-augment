Abstract

Parent: “I have 500 mg aspirin tablets. Can I give
one for a fever at a body weight of 24 kg?”,
LLM:“Yes, but use half a tablet. The standard
antipyretic dose is 10–15 mg/kg, so at 24 kg roughly
250 mg is appropriate.”
The dose calculation is correct, and the model
even corrects the amount the parent proposed, but
the answer is not. 24 kg indicates a child, and
aspirin is avoided in febrile children because of
its well-known link to Reye’s syndrome (Pugliese
et al., 2008). Verifying the output with an expert
may not be promptly feasible and is impractical at
scale, while relying on Judge-LLMs for verification could introduce unpredictable biases or hidden
errors unless the validation is also reviewed by experts (Chen et al., 2024b; Thakur et al., 2025).
Our work focuses on estimating whether an
LLM has answered correctly using signals from
inside the model itself, computed during the same
forward pass that generates the answer. We introduce H EAD E NTROPY, a scalable and interpretable
white-box method that uses the attention spread
inside the model itself to determine correctness
while generating the answer. In the transformer
self-attention mechanism, each attention head computes attention weights by taking the inner product
between query and key vectors—–a measure of
their directional alignment—–and then normalizing these scores across all positions via softmax to
produce a set of token weights. We find that certain
attention patterns are stable during training, while
others are unstable and tend to disappear through
the course of training. We hypothesize that the
presence of unstable attention patterns at inference
time correlates with a generation not grounded in
the training set–otherwise the training gradients
would have modified the attention pattern.

Large language models (LLMs) often generate plausible yet incorrect answers, posing risks in safety-critical settings such as
medicine. Human evaluation is expensive,
and LLM-as-judge approaches risk introducing hidden errors. Recent single-pass whitebox methods detect contextual hallucinations
using linear probes over model internals, but
their generalization to out-of-domain, out-oftask, and training-free settings remains poorly
understood. We introduce H EAD E NTROPY,
a method that predicts answer correctness
by measuring how susceptible each attention
head’s pattern is to modification under further gradient updates. The key insight is that
the trace of the softmax Jacobian is a monotonic function of 2-Rényi entropy, linking attention spread to gradient stability during training,
even without the reference annotation. With no
training, H EAD E NTROPY reaches 0.736 AUROC, outperforms every training-free baseline
in paired comparison and matches a hiddenstate probe out-of-domain, at under 1% of inference cost. We evaluate broadly across 5
instruction-tuned LLMs and 5 datasets spanning general knowledge, multi-hop reasoning,
mathematical reasoning, claim verification, and
medicine. The code is available at https:
//github.com/StanfordMIMI/HeadEntropy

The Jacobian can be used as a measure of how
sensitive a particular set of activations or parameters are. A "large" Jacobian indicates that an identical gradient flow will change the activations greatly.
We use this to identify stable versus unstable attention patterns, where we define "stable" attention
2

patterns as those that do not change substantially
with a gradient update and "unstable" attention pattern as those that change a large amount every time
they appear in training. In this view, observing
many unstable attention patterns may be an indicator that the current example is not like the training
data.
There is no single definition of what makes a
matrix such as a Jacobian "large". In this work,
we use the trace of the Jacobian, noting that the
softmax Jacobian is positive semidefinite (all nonnegative eigenvalues), so its trace coincides with
its nuclear norm (Appendix L).

Hypothesis. A correct answer rests on associations that training has reinforced. We hypothesise
that the attention patterns carrying such associations are ones training has settled: further gradient
updates would barely move them. A pattern that
continued training would still reshape is one training has not settled, and a generation resting on such
patterns is less likely to be grounded in the training distribution. We want to tell the two apart at
inference time.
Testing this directly would need the pretraining
corpus: checking, for each generation, whether
the associations it uses are attested in the data.
That corpus is unavailable for most deployed models, and searching it once per generation would be
prohibitive in any case. We need a quantity computable from the model alone.

derived in Appendix L. Thus, a head’s gradient
stability is fixed entirely by the spread of its attention. We can therefore read it off the forward pass
without a backward pass, access to training data or
additional generations.

Does training settle attention? If training settles
the heads that carry an association, then learning an
example should lower tr(J) on that example, and
failing to learn it should not. We test this directly on
five OLMo-2-1B pretraining checkpoints. At each
checkpoint we run one forward pass over 1000
TriviaQA examples with their gold answers and
record log P (gold) together with per-head tr(J).
Across each of the four checkpoint transitions we
rank examples by how much log P (gold) changed
and call the top decile learned, the bottom decile
unlearned, and the middle 80% the control.

the Appendix Table 6.

Entropy features are computed using quantities
already available from the softmax, adding negligible overhead to a standard forward pass. Memory remains O(m) since only per-head aggregates
are retained while full attention maps need not be
stored. Overall, H EAD E NTROPY adds less than 1%
to inference cost and requires no additional forward
passes beyond those typically used for generation.

Experimental Setup

Models. We evaluate three model families spanning 1.7B–32B parameters. From the reasoningoriented Qwen3 family we use Qwen3-1.7B,
Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-32B; from the generalpurpose Llama family we use Llama-3.1-8BInstruct; and from the Gemma family we use
Gemma-4-12B-it. All generations use near-greedy
decoding (T <0.001) at half-precision floatingpoint. We also test generalization to T =1 (Section 5, Table 11).

Metrics. We measure how well each score separates correct from incorrect model answers with
AUROC. Scores are reported with their intended
orientation, if known from literature, i.e. a higher
ratio for Lookback lens indicates an answer being
more grounded in the context.

Datasets. We use five benchmarks spanning
open-domain QA (TriviaQA, Joshi et al., 2017),
multi-hop QA with long context (HotpotQA distractor setting, Yang et al., 2018), domain-specific
multiple-choice QA (MedMCQA, Pal et al., 2022),
fact verification (FEVER, Thorne et al., 2018), and
mathematical reasoning (MATH, Hendrycks et al.,
2021). For TriviaQA, HotpotQA, and MedMCQA
we sample uniformly at random with a fixed seed
30k training examples with a max of 768 generated
tokens. Truncated answers were excluded from the
training set only. We use the given validation split
for evaluation. FEVER and MATH given validation
split are held out and used for out-of-distribution
evaluation (Table 2). The prompts can be found in
Appendix B.3, B.2, B.1). We define binary correctness via the standard metric for each dataset: exact

5.3

Ablations

Appendix ablations show that performance is stable
with 20% of the training data, at regularization
strength c=0.1, and under sampling at temperature
even with T =1 (AUROC shifts of at most 0.05
on TriviaQA and HotpotQA; Appendices J.1, J.2,
Table 11). The exception is MedMCQA, where
AUROC drops by 0.20 to 0.24. However, model
accuracy there falls below chance (0.18 and 0.12 vs.
0.25), so the generations themselves are unreliable
rather than the signal. We justify mean aggregation
over min/max (Appendix J.3). Regarding the probe
architecture, a logistic regression model performed
on par with XGBoost and MLP probes, with at
most a negligible difference (Appendix J.4). We
show, via SHAP analysis and a layer ablation, that
middle layers carry the most signal (Appendices G,
I).

In-domain. Trained and evaluated on the same
dataset, a H EAD E NTROPY probe averages 0.867

Comparison with prior work. Appendix Table 6 places H EAD E NTROPY’s TriviaQA AUROC
on Llama-3.1-8B (0.879) next to numbers reported
by prior hallucination-detection and uncertaintyestimation methods. Cross-paper comparison is
loose, since setups, backbones and evaluation protocols differ. H EAD E NTROPY is in the range of
reported single-pass numbers (LapEigvals 0.889,
Kadavath et al. 0.864) and above the multi-pass Semantic Uncertainty (0.828) and Semantic Energy
(0.748).

or contradict established evidence. Our work translates broader uncertainty estimation and hallucination detection into the more concrete objective
of estimating answer-level correctness, that is, the
probability that an answer produced by an LLM
given an input is correct under a task metric (e.g.,
exact match, F1 score) (Eq. 4).
Prior work differs in how much of the LLM’s
internal state is used to fulfill these goals: (i) blackbox methods rely only on outputs, often requiring
multiple passes or auxiliary models; (ii) gray-box
methods access shallow signals such as final-layer
logits; and (iii) white-box methods leverage deeper
representations, such as hidden states or attention
maps.
Black-box methods. These methods rely solely
on the LLM’s input and outputs and often require
multiple forward passes or auxiliary models, making them expensive and sensitive to domain shift.
SelfCheckGPT (Manakul et al., 2023) detects uncertainty via disagreement across sampled completions, while Chain-of-Verification (Dhuliawala
et al., 2024) probes consistency through follow-up
questions. Other approaches, such as semantic uncertainty (Kuhn et al., 2023), use embedding models to group paraphrases, introducing the reliance
on well-aligned external tools. LLM-as-a-Judge
methods (Chen et al., 2024b; Thakur et al., 2025)
delegate evaluation to separate LLMs, potentially
introducing biases and weak calibration guarantees.
Gray-box methods. Gray-box methods occupy
a middle ground, drawing on limited internal signals such as token log probabilities or final hidden states. By doing so, they enable single-pass
confidence estimation. For example, predictors
trained over logits can temper the well-documented
tendency of LLMs toward overconfidence (Groot
and Valdenegro-Toro, 2024), while filters based on
final-layer statistics can anticipate distributional

shifts that undermine reliability (Pouget et al.,
2025). Likewise, conformal prediction methods
extend log-probability signals into calibrated sets
of plausible completions (Angelopoulos and Bates,
2021), offering a principled notion of coverage. Despite these strengths, gray-box approaches remain
constrained by their reliance on final-layer information, which often calibrates poorly across domains
and reduces interpretability to heuristics like “low
log-probability” (Geng et al., 2024).
White-box methods. These methods leverage
richer internal signals such as layer-wise hidden
states, attention maps, or neuron activations, often improving accuracy while introducing additional design choices. Several works (Azaria and
Mitchell, 2023; Kadavath et al., 2022) have demonstrated that an LLM’s hidden states encapsulate
latent knowledge about hallucinations. Chen et al.
(2024a) sample multiple responses and use the logdeterminant of their embedding covariance as a correctness signal. Snyder et al. (2024) employ gated
recurrent units (GRUs) and multilayer perceptrons
(MLPs) over internal representations to predict correctness. For more details on this line of work,
please see Appendix D. LLM-Check (Sriramanan
et al., 2024) combines multiple internal signals,
including attention statistics, to construct a correctness metric. Distinct from the works mentioned
above, a small group of methods focuses only on
the attention heads of a single sample. Lookback
lens detects contextual hallucinations by measuring the location of the attention mass by the ratio
of attention weights on the context versus newly
generated tokens (Chuang et al., 2024). While
AggTruth focuses on contextual hallucination detection in RAG settings, analyzing how attention
is distributed across passage tokens using multiple aggregation techniques (Matys et al., 2025).
H EAD E NTROPY targets final-answer correctness

spread of the full attention distribution rather than
the location of attention mass.

Related work on hidden states as features for hallucination detection

A growing line of work uses hidden states as features for predicting ’hallucinations’, a term used loosely
across this literature to cover varied failure modes, including factual errors, inconsistencies, and errors
13

flagged by LLM judges. In contrast, H EAD E NTROPY uses only the attention distribution: it is lowerdimensional, interpretable, automated across layers, and requires a single forward pass at inference
time.
Kossen et al. (2024)’s SEPs trains hidden state probes to predict semantic entropy, while H EAD E NTROPY uses attention entropy to predict answer correctness directly under standard QA metrics.
H EAD E NTROPY offers three advantages over a SEPs-style hidden state probe for the given task: First, we
provide a formal motivation connecting 2-Rényi attention entropy to training gradient dynamics via the
trace (equivalently, nuclear norm) of the softmax Jacobian. SEPs demonstrates empirically that hidden
states encode semantic uncertainty, while H EAD E NTROPY explains why attention entropy relates to
gradient stability. Second, H EAD E NTROPY avoids manual layer and token position selection entirely.
Attention entropy is available at every layer and head, and the sparse classifier automatically identifies
informative heads, whereas SEPs require ablations to find optimal layer and token positions. Third, the
feature vector has dimensionality m (number of attention heads, typically for open source models 16-64)
versus hidden state dimensionality d (typically 1024-8192). Supervising H EAD E NTROPY on binary
semantic entropy labels (analogous to Kossen et al.) is an interesting future direction. For the scope of
this work we focus on exact match and F1 as correctness metrics.
Cencerrado et al. (2025) focuses on predicting answer correctness before generation using linear probes
on hidden states with a heuristic motivation. Although our work emphasizes the attention distribution
using a formal motivation, the task is comparable. Similarities: the best-performing assessor (trained
on embeddings from GPT) is logistic regression, signal increases with larger models, and middle layers
perform slightly better. Key differences: their core claims focus on OOD generalization for predicting
answer correctness from question-only hidden states, a distinct goal from ours. Results are reported on a
subsampled training set, making direct comparison non-trivial as open-source models are almost certainly
trained on it. They use careful per model layer selection, whereas H EAD E NTROPY automates this process.
Ji et al. (2024) addresses a similar task; however, it focuses on self-awareness of uncertainty by
examining how internal states indicate whether the model has seen the query during training, a question
related to our paper. We focus on a formal motivation through the lens of training stability and connect
feature generation directly to training dynamics, with their hidden-state approach serving as one of our
baselines. They employ a more complex multilayer perceptron (MLP) variant.
Huang et al. (2025) investigates whether internal causal mechanisms can robustly predict model
correctness in out-of-distribution settings, covering tasks such as symbol manipulation, knowledge
retrieval, and instruction following. While related to our goal, we examine more closely the contribution
of each attention head using a logistic regression model and note that generalization across tasks exists,
but with some task-dependent component.
Beigi et al. (2024) is in the same field as the studies mentioned above. One focus here is predictor model
selection and contrastive loss construction to cope with high-dimensional inputs from hidden, activation,
and attention states across all layers.
Unlike methods analyzing static, high-dimensional hidden states, Zhang et al. (2025) focuses on the
dynamic update process of the residual stream, which proves more effective at identifying when a model
deviates from factual accuracy across layers. The ICR Probe outperforms prior hidden-state baselines and
provides improved interpretability for understanding hallucination mechanisms.
