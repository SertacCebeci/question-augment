Abstract
Reinforcement learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for unlocking reasoning capabilities in language
models. However, relying on sparse rewards makes this process highly sample-inefficient, as models must navigate
vast search spaces with minimal feedback. While classic curriculum learning aims to mitigate this by ordering data
based on complexity, prior works have primarily targeted small datasets and do not directly transfer to the large-scale
settings typical of modern language model training. Furthermore, the right ordering for a specific model is often
unclear. To address this, we propose Goldilocks, an adaptive data-selection strategy that uses a Selector network
to predict the standard deviation of rewards across the model’s rollouts for each candidate question. The Selector
prioritizes questions with high predicted reward variability, corresponding to questions that are neither too easy
nor too hard for the model’s current capabilities (Goldilocks principle), while training the model with GRPO. By
leveraging the model’s performance on seen samples, the Selector continuously adapts to the model’s evolving abilities.
Across the OpenMathReasoning and Polaris datasets, Goldilocks consistently improves over standard GRPO, requiring
up to 78% fewer optimization steps to reach the corresponding GRPO performance.

Introduction

The reasoning capabilities of Transformer-based models have been a focal point of recent research, with Reinforcement
Learning (RL) emerging as a powerful paradigm for enhancing these abilities. Recent RL methods incentivize the
generation of a detailed Chain of Thought (CoT) [27], enabling models to solve complex problems [32, 10, 13]. Additionally,
increasing the computational budget at inference time, known as scaling test-time compute, has been shown to further
enhance these reasoning capabilities [24]. While RL has proven effective in unlocking complex reasoning behaviors,
the computational efficiency of these methods remains a significant challenge. Recent findings by Khatri et al. [14]
underscore that scaling is a fundamental driver of performance. This observation suggests that while models have the
potential for continued improvement, realizing this potential through brute-force scaling is resource-intensive, making
training efficiency a paramount concern.
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) [6, 22] provides a scalable way to improve reasoning by assigning
rewards using automatically checkable outcomes. However, when the reward is determined primarily by final-answer
correctness, the resulting feedback is sparse, since a generated trajectory is primarily judged by whether it reaches
a correct final answer: a generated trajectory is rewarded if it reaches a correct answer. This creates a substantial
efficiency challenge during training. Questions that are far beyond the model’s current capabilities are likely to produce
groups of uniformly incorrect rollouts, while questions that are already mastered tend to produce uniformly correct ones.
In either case, substantial generation compute may be spent on rollout groups that provide little or no within-group
reward variation, and therefore little useful relative signal for the GRPO update.
This inefficiency becomes increasingly important as language-model training moves toward larger data and compute
regimes. Scaling studies have established the importance of jointly increasing model capacity, data, and compute [12],
while recent frontier models are trained on trillions of tokens [7]. Reasoning post-training is following a similar trend,
with recent systems relying on increasingly large-scale reinforcement learning [11, 31, 14]. Reasoning datasets are also

growing to millions of questions [17]. In such a large-pool regime, an effective data-selection strategy cannot assume
that the model has already interacted with every candidate question. Instead, it should be able to estimate the learning
value of previously unseen questions from limited model feedback, and ideally make this decision before incurring the
cost of generating rollouts.
Curriculum Learning (CL) [4] provides a natural framework for prioritizing training examples, but many existing
approaches rely on information that becomes difficult to obtain in this regime. History-based methods estimate the
difficulty or utility of a question from the model’s previous performance on that same instance [21, 23]; such estimates
become informative only after sufficient observations of the question have been collected. Other approaches schedule
predefined difficulty categories or data partitions [20, 5], requiring structure that may not be available for a large
heterogeneous question pool. A different line of work identifies uninformative examples only after generating their
rollouts and then skips the corresponding policy update [26, 28]; this avoids an unhelpful optimization step, but not the
generation cost already incurred. This motivates a curriculum that can infer the utility of previously unseen questions
from recent model feedback, make the selection before rollout generation, and adapt as the model improves.
In this work, we propose Goldilocks, a pre-rollout data-selection framework that uses a learned Selector. Alongside
model RL training, a lightweight Selector network learns from recent model outcomes to predict the reward variability of
candidate questions. It then prioritizes questions with high reward variability that are expected to produce informative
rollout groups (neither those already mastered by the model nor those currently beyond its capabilities). Because the
prediction is made from the question itself, the Selector can generalize to candidates the model has never encountered
and continuously update its curriculum as the model improves. We show theoretically that this criterion is aligned
with the learning signal of GRPO [22] under binary verifiable rewards, and empirically evaluate it across large- and
small-pool training regimes against predictive and history-based curriculum baselines.
The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 discusses related work. Section 3 analyzes GRPO gradient dynamics
under verifiable rewards. Section 4 presents our joint training framework. Sections 5 and 7 report empirical results and
ablations and Section 8 concludes.

Related Work

Reasoning and Reinforcement Learning. Intermediate reasoning traces have been shown to substantially improve
language-model performance on complex tasks, through supervised scratchpads [18], Chain-of-Thought prompting
[27, 15], and reinforcement learning [22]. Recent RL methods for reasoning optimize such trajectories using verifiable
rewards, but their efficiency is limited by the large generation cost and by questions that provide little useful learning
signal. This has motivated methods that improve which problems are selected for training or how rollout compute is
allocated.
History-Based Curricula and Adaptive Sampling. A large class of curriculum methods estimates question utility
from previous interactions with the same question. MoPPS [21] maintains prompt-specific estimates of model success,
while BOTS [23] similarly uses accumulated evidence to guide online task selection. Such history-based methods are
most effective when questions are revisited frequently enough for their estimates to become informative. We therefore
include MoPPS as a baseline in our smaller-dataset setting, where repeated exposure provides a favorable regime for
question-specific histories. Other approaches operate over predefined data categories [5], adapt rollout allocation [16], or
identify uninformative samples only after generation [26, 28, 8]; these improve training efficiency but do not directly
address predicting the utility of previously unseen questions before rollout.
Predictive Pre-Rollout Selection. The closest prior work to Goldilocks is Prompt Curriculum Learning (PCL) [9],
which, among the methods we consider, is the only approach that learns a prompt-conditioned predictor from model
feedback and uses it to score unseen questions before generating model rollouts. PCL predicts the model’s probability of
success and selects questions near a target difficulty, whereas Goldilocks directly predicts the reward variability of the
rollout group, the quantity motivated by our GRPO analysis. PCL is evaluated on substantially smaller question pools
than the large-scale regime considered in our main experiments; we therefore use it as our primary predictive baseline to
test whether this form of pre-rollout generalization remains effective as the training pool grows.

Joint Training Procedure

We now detail the overall training loop, illustrated in Figure 1. The process operates in a cycle that alternates between
data selection, model optimization via GRPO, and continuous Selector refinement.

4.1

Data Selection Policy (Steps 1-2)

During the data selection phase, the Selector samples a candidate pool, denoted as C, consisting of Kcandidate examples
drawn randomly from the dataset. For each candidate question q ∈ C, the Selector computes a predicted utility score
v̂q = fϕ (q). To balance exploration and exploitation, the final training sample q ∗ is selected using an ϵ-greedy strategy

Model Rollouts and Optimization (Steps 3-4)

Once the prompt q ∗ is selected, the model generates a group of G rollouts, {o1 , . . . , oG }. The verification reward
rver is computed for each output, and the advantages are estimated using the group-relative normalization described

5

Experiments and Results

To evaluate Goldilocks, we primarily focus on the large-scale OpenMathReasoning dataset [17], which comprises over
3 million chain-of-thought problems that motivates our setting. We compare Goldilocks against standard GRPO and
Prompt Curriculum Learning (PCL) [9], our closest predictive pre-rollout baseline. Given the substantial computational
cost of long-run GRPO training on OpenMathReasoning, we prioritize extensive training on this benchmark rather than
broad evaluation across many datasets. As a complementary smaller-scale setting, we additionally train and evaluate on
Polaris [3], which contains approximately 50,000 questions, where questions are revisited more frequently. In this regime,
we compare against both PCL and MoPPS [21], allowing us to contrast Goldilocks with a history-based curriculum
under conditions where question-specific histories are more informative.
Across these settings, we evaluate five models spanning different model families and sizes: OLMo2-1B [19], Qwen2.5-1.5B
[29], Qwen3-4B-Base [30], Phi-4-mini-Instruct (4B) [1], and Qwen2.5-7B [29]. Following common practice in RL-based
reasoning research [22, 2, 5], we focus on mathematical reasoning and train all models with GRPO. All experiments are
implemented using the TRL library [25]. Additional dataset, training, and compute details are provided in Appendices D
and E.

5.1

Experimental Setup

For each model–dataset pair, we first tune the GRPO learning rate and global batch size, and use the best-performing
configuration as the reference for all subsequent comparisons. We then evaluate Goldilocks and the corresponding
baselines using the selected settings. All methods use a rollout group size of G = 16 in the main experiments, with
additional ablations evaluating sensitivity to alternative group sizes.
For all experiments, we use Qwen3-0.6B [30] as the Selector. In all experiments, we utilized a fixed computational
budget of 8 GPUs. Vanilla GRPO uses all eight GPUs for model training, whereas Goldilocks and PCL reserve one GPU
5

for the Selector and use the remaining seven for the model. Accordingly, if the tuned GRPO global batch size is B, we
use a model batch size of 78 B for Goldilocks and PCL, while keeping the per-GPU model configuration unchanged. This
provides a matched hardware budget and explicitly accounts for the GPU required by the learned curriculum predictor
rather than giving Selector-based methods additional compute. MoPPS, which does not require a Selector network,
follows the corresponding model’s GRPO configuration. We justify this scheme in Appendix E.1.

Results

Our primary experiments on OpenMathReasoning use a long-run training regime to reflect the scale of the dataset and
evaluate whether the benefits of adaptive data selection persist over extended training. This extended training regime
is particularly important in the large-pool setting, as it allows us to test whether the benefit of Goldilocks persists as
the model’s capabilities evolve rather than being confined to the early stages of RL training. Our primary comparison
focuses on training efficiency relative to tuned GRPO, while we also report validation accuracy at matched training
steps. Because the models differ in size and are trained with different batch sizes, they are trained for different numbers
of optimization steps under this regime; we therefore report the evaluation step in our tables. Complete hyperparameter
searches, selected configurations, and compute accounting are reported in Appendix E. The total computational cost of
these experiments is detailed in Appendix L.

Polaris: Retaining the Benefit When Questions Recur

The smaller Polaris dataset tests a different explanation for curriculum performance. With more opportunities to revisit
each question, a history-based method can build and reuse estimates across a larger fraction of the training pool. This
setting is therefore favorable to MoPPS, whose prompt-specific posteriors become more informative as observations
accumulate. A benefit confined to sparse-history settings would be less compelling if it disappeared once these histories
became readily available.

Limitations and Future Work

Larger models. Our experiments study models up to 7B parameters and use a 0.6B Selector throughout. They do
not establish how the approach scales to substantially larger models, whose evolving capabilities may be harder for a
small predictor to track. Future work should evaluate larger Selector and model combinations, measure how prediction
quality changes with the capacity gap, and determine whether curriculum gains persist after all Selector costs are
included.
Datasets beyond mathematics. OpenMathReasoning and Polaris differ in scale and question reuse, but both
concern mathematical reasoning with verifiable answers. Generalization to other domains remains untested. Code
generation, theorem proving, and multi-step tasks offer natural next settings, with different verifiers, reward distributions,
and computational costs. Extending the selection target to these datasets, particularly when feedback is graded or noisy
rather than binary, is an important direction for future work.

E.2

Selector Architecture

The Selector functions as a value predictor built upon a trainable language model backbone. For a given input question
q, we extract the final layer embeddings and apply mean pooling to obtain a fixed-size vector representation. This vector
is then passed through a linear projection layer. To strictly enforce the valid range of the standard deviation term
(which lies between 0 and 0.5 for a Bernoulli variable), we apply a scaled sigmoid activation, i.e.,
fϕ (q) = 0.5 · σ(wT · MeanPool(Embed(q)) + b),
where fϕ (q) represents the predicted utility of question q. The parameters ϕ encompass both the projection head and
the pretrained language model, allowing the Selector to learn deep semantic features relevant to difficulty estimation. For
a detailed exploration of alternative Selector backbones and projection strategies, please refer to Appendix E.3.

Baseline Details

Success Probability versus Reward-Variability Targets

If the success probability of everyp
question were known exactly, a curriculum targeting pq ≈ 1/2 and one maximizing
the ideal reward-variability score pq (1 − pq ) would favor the same intermediate-difficulty questions. The distinction
becomes important in practice because pq is not known and must instead be inferred from the question and from finite
rollout feedback.
A predicted success probability near 1/2 does not necessarily mean that the underlying question itself has success
probability near 1/2. For example, the same prediction can arise when the information available to the predictor cannot
distinguish between questions that are very easy and questions that are very difficult. Goldilocks does not train the
Selector only to predict mean success probability; instead, it directly predicts the reward variability realized by rollout
groups.

What the two prediction targets learn

We first consider a predictor trained with squared loss on the empirical success rate p̂. The population minimizer of

E[p̂ | P ] = P,

Computational Cost of Experiments

All experiments were conducted on 8-GPU nodes, using either H100 80GB or H200 141GB GPUs. For GRPO and
MoPPS, all eight GPUs are used for model training, whereas Goldilocks and PCL allocate seven GPUs to the model
and one GPU to the Selector. Across the experiments reported in this work, we ran 66 jobs totaling approximately
115,099 GPU-hours. This includes 54,891 GPU-hours for the main experiments, 12,103 for seed repetitions, 8,133 for
objective ablations, 4,159 for the exploration-probability ablation, 8,318 for the rollout-group-size ablation, 4,159 for the
candidate-pool-size ablation, and 23,336 for batch-size tuning.
