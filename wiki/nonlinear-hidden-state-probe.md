# Nonlinear hidden-state probe

A probe is a small classifier trained on a frozen model's internal activations while the model's own weights stay fixed. The shape of that classifier is a separate choice from what it is trained on. The paper under study makes that choice explicit. Its premise is that a linear readout cannot follow the structure of a hallucination in the latent space, because hallucinations "often stem from subtle shifts in the latent space, linear probes struggle to capture such nuanced variations."[^c1] The replacement is a multilayer perceptron that reads one intermediate layer's hidden state at each token and returns a per-token hallucination probability. The default layer is set to ⌊0.95 × the number of layers⌋: "this work defaults to putting probes at."[^c2] Only the probe's parameters are trained.

The mechanism the paper relies on is that the frozen model already carries the signal, and a nonlinear head can read it where a linear one cannot. The paper states the contrast with output confidence directly: "relying solely on the uncertainty generated by the language model itself is insufficient to reliably capture token-level hallucinations."[^c3] Its uncertainty baselines, perplexity and entropy, are weak on every dataset in its table. The probes are the stronger readout.

The nonlinear gain is measured, not only argued. On LongFact, the MLP probe is reported to give "a 5.605% accuracy improvement and 8.2% recall" over the linear probe, and the text says the MLP probe "captures nonlinear semantic features more effectively in intermediate-layer hidden states."[^c4][^c5] On HealthBench and TriviaQA, "the MLP probe demonstrated stronger generalization capabilities."[^c6] The unit of detection is the entity, not the claim: the paper's labels are token spans for names, dates and citations, and the paper frames entities as easier to localize than whole assertions.

Where the layer sits is treated as a search problem. The paper models probe performance as a function of layer position and uses Bayesian optimization with a Gaussian process over a black-box objective, "evaluated using Recall@0.1 FPR on the validation set."[^c7] The search is what replaces hand-picking a layer. Its theorem on asymptotic optimality is a standard GP-UCB result, and the paper's own separability assumption is what links layer to performance.

## Limits

- The probe comparison is reported on one backbone in the text I read. The statement of setup is "on the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model," and the cross-model evidence is limited to the layer search.[^c8] Whether the MLP advantage holds on other models or other layers was not shown here.
- The labels come from a model with web search, not from human annotators: "the Claude 4 Sonnet model with web retrieval capabilities performs automatic fact verification."[^c9] The probe learns that verifier's judgments of entity support, and its errors are errors against that judge.
- The reported layer is inconsistent in the text. The body says the search settled on layer 22, while the figure caption assigns 29 to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and 22 to Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. The text reads "the optimal layer position for the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model is at" 29, so the reported optimum for the main model is unclear.[^c10][^c11]
- The comparison reports point values with no variance or repeated runs in the text I read. My inference: a few points of accuracy on one split does not establish that nonlinearity helps in general.
- The method detects entity-level hallucinations only. The paper leaves other kinds to future work: "Future work may extend detection beyond entities."[^c12]
- The linear-versus-nonlinear claim is tested against one linear baseline. It says nothing about other nonlinear heads, larger probes, or probes trained on more data.
