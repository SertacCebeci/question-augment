Safety alignment in large language models
(LLMs) is primarily evaluated under openended generation, where models can mitigate risk by refusing to respond. In contrast, many real-world applications place
LLMs in structured decision-making tasks,
such as multiple-choice questions (MCQs),
where abstention is discouraged or unavailable. We identify a systematic failure mode in this setting: reformulating
harmful requests as forced-choice MCQs

MCQ Benchmarks and Structured
Safety Evaluation

Structured output formats, particularly
multiple-choice questions (MCQs), are widely
used to evaluate both the capabilities and
safety of large language models benchmarks
such as MMLU(Hendrycks et al., 2021) and
C-Eval(Huang et al., 2023) adopt MCQs
to assess factual knowledge and reasoning,
while safety-oriented benchmarks such as
SafetyBench(Zhang et al., 2024b) and SGBench(Mou et al., 2024) employ MCQ-based
evaluations to probe ethical judgments and
value alignment.
Despite their prevalence, prior work has
largely treated MCQs as a diagnostic tool,
focusing on factual or logical errors rather
than their impact on safety behavior, or using
them as proxies for apparent alignment(Wang
et al., 2024). Existing jailbreak research has instead emphasized semantic manipulation or sophisticated prompt engineering, including embedding harmful queries into complex ethical
dilemmas (Chua et al., 2025), which typically
require carefully constructed scenarios and iterative induction to bypass safety mechanisms.
Góral et al. (2025) study forced-choice robustness using MCQs with no correct options, including a small safety-oriented subset, where
all options correspond to harmful actions that
fail to satisfy the task objective, and models
therefore tend to default to refusal. In contrast, we identify a more fundamental and lowbarrier failure surface: a structural collapse
of refusal behavior induced solely by forcedchoice constraints.

Threat Model

We consider a low-barrier threat model where
an attacker has no access to model parameters,

prompts. The attacker’s sole capability is to
reformulate harmful requests into structured
task formats. Under this setting, we examine
whether forced-choice constraints can systematically alter a model’s refusal behavior, without semantic obfuscation, multi-turn interaction, or adaptive prompt optimization.

Evaluation Protocol

Determining whether a response constitutes a
safety violation involves a degree of subjectivity. To balance scalability and reliability, we
adopt a hybrid evaluation pipeline combining
automated and human judgment.
We ﬁrst use GPT-4o as an automated judge
to classify each model response as either a suc3

cessful or failed attack. To reduce sensitivity
to prompt wording, we employ three semantically similar but surface-diverse judgment
prompts and evaluate each response independently with all three. The exact judge prompt
formulations are provided in Appendix C. A
response is labeled as a successful attack if
it selects, prefers, ranks, or provides directional justiﬁcation for any harmful option. Responses that output an option identiﬁer are
also counted as successful attacks, as they directly conﬁrm a harmful choice and reduce the
attacker’s decision space.
When the three automated judgments agree,
the consensus label is assigned. Responses
with inconsistent judgments are ﬂagged as conﬂict cases and independently annotated by
three authors, with the ﬁnal label determined
by majority vote.

To assess whether explanation prompts alone
constitute the primary attack vector, we construct a control condition in which the model

Under this setting, ASR increases from 1/90
(1.11%) to 8/90 (8.89%). In contrast, the
MCQ-based Format 5 (choice + explanation)
yields an ASR of 34/90 (37.78%).
This comparison shows that while explanation requests moderately increase unsafe responses, their eﬀect is signiﬁcantly weaker
than that of introducing a forced-choice structure. The MCQ format transforms an openended safety decision into a constrained selection task, under which refusal behavior becomes substantially less stable.

Discussion

This work highlights a gap between how safety
alignment in large language models is commonly evaluated and how these models are deployed in practice. While existing safety assessments predominantly focus on open-ended
generation and refusal behavior, many realworld applications embed models in structured
decision-making contexts, where abstention is
discouraged or unavailable and producing any
option—even without detailed content—can
carry risk.
Our results show that multiple-choice questions expose a distinct class of safety failures
not well captured by open-ended evaluations.
When all available options are unacceptable,
the model’s task shifts from deciding whether
to respond to deciding which option best satisﬁes the prompt. This shift alone can destabilize refusal behavior, even for models that reliably refuse comparable open-ended requests,
and arises without semantic obfuscation or
adaptive prompt optimization.
We further ﬁnd that the eﬀectiveness of
adversarial MCQs is strongly inﬂuenced by
the capability of the data generator. Highcapability models produce adversarial inputs
that transfer broadly across targets, remaining eﬀective even against strong models under structured task constraints, whereas inputs generated by lower-capability models exhibit substantially weaker transfer. This asymmetry suggests that adversarial eﬀectiveness
is governed primarily by generator capability
rather than similarity between the generator
and the target.
A plausible interpretation is that highcapability generators produce inputs closer
to safety decision boundaries: compared to
human-authored requests, these prompts may
be more internally consistent, less overtly malicious, or framed in ways that reduce the
salience of safety violations. When combined

Conclusion

We investigate how structured task formats affect safety alignment in large language models, focusing on multiple-choice questions in
which all available options are harmful. Across
a range of models and datasets, we show
that forced-choice constraints substantially increase attack success rates, even when the
same models reliably refuse comparable openended requests.
Our ﬁndings suggest that safety evaluations
centered exclusively on open-ended generation
may overlook risks arising from structured
decision-making tasks. More broadly, this
work highlights the need to assess alignment
not only by what models generate, but also by
how task structure constrains their decisions,
particularly in settings where refusal is not a
viable option.

Limitations
Despite the insights provided by our experiments, this work has several limitations that
warrant careful consideration.
First, the coverage of task formulations
is limited. Our study primarily focuses on
8

process-oriented harmful queries (e.g., “how
to” or “how should one”) in order to isolate the
eﬀects of structured choice under executable
tasks. While this design highlights the interaction between MCQ structure and safety behavior, it does not fully capture the diversity
of real-world harmful requests, which may be
framed in more indirect, hypothetical, or evaluative forms. Whether similar eﬀects persist
under diﬀerent types of task formulations remains an open question.
Second, our evaluation is largely restricted
to single-turn interactions. Although some
prompt formats introduce extended instructions resembling multi-step responses, we do
not explicitly model adaptive multi-turn attacks in which options or prompts are iteratively adjusted based on prior model outputs.
In realistic settings, such interactions may further reinforce commitment to earlier decisions
and amplify the behaviors observed in this
work.
Third, all experiments in this study are conducted exclusively in Chinese. While this
choice ensures consistency across task formulations and reﬂects the language setting of
our datasets, it limits the generalizability of
our ﬁndings to other languages. Models may
exhibit diﬀerent safety behaviors across languages due to diﬀerences in training data distribution, alignment coverage, or linguistic
structure. Evaluating whether the observed
eﬀects of MCQ-based task constraints extend
to multilingual or cross-lingual settings is an
important direction for future work.
These limitations do not invalidate our core
ﬁndings on the impact of task structure, but
suggest that their generality should be further examined under more diverse task formulations, interaction patterns, and language settings.
