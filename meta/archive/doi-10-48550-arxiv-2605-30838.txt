Abstract
LLM-powered search agents enable multi-step
reasoning and tool use. However, these capabilities introduce retrieval-induced safety degradation, as harmful intents may decompose into
seemingly innocuous sub-queries that lead to
unsafe outcomes. Existing alignment methods
struggle to capture sparse safety signals and
fail to supervise diverse violations across multistep interactions. We propose COMPASS, a
Cognitive MCTS-Guided Process Alignment
framework designed to achieve robust safety
alignment throughout the agent workflow while
preserving general utility. COMPASS integrates cognitive tree exploration (CTE) to efficiently synthesize stealthy attack trajectories,
and introspective step-wise alignment (ISA)
to isolate risky intermediate actions for finegrained process supervision. Empirical results show that COMPASS achieves a favorable
safety-utility trade-off while requiring substantially less training data.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown remarkable abilities in text generation and reasoning (Guo et al., 2025). Utilizing LLMs as base
models, search agents extend conventional singlestep retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) (Lewis
et al., 2020; Borgeaud et al., 2022) by enabling iterative retrieval, allowing agents to reason over external knowledge through multi-step interactions (Li
et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025).
Despite substantial utility gains, search agents
expose vulnerabilities in the base model’s safety
alignment. Industry reports on deployed agentic systems highlight these risks and the need for
stronger model-intrinsic defenses (OpenAI, 2025;
Anthropic, 2025a). This phenomenon is termed
retrieval-induced safety degradation (Yu et al.,
2025; An et al., 2025), where access to external information lowers the model’s refusal threshold. As

These observations suggest that search agent
safety requires more than outcome-level refusals
or surface-level retrieval control. However, applying process-level supervision (Zhang et al., 2025a)
to search agent safety still poses two key challenges. First, how to efficiently explore sparse
safety signals in large state spaces. (CH1) Processsupervised RL requires high-quality trajectories
with informative intermediate states for effective
safety alignment. However, the multi-step interactions of search agents induce an exponentially large
state space (Mehrotra et al., 2024). Unlike utility tasks with clear golden answers, safety signals
are sparse, delayed, and difficult to localize within
agent trajectories. Therefore, discovering stealthy
reasoning paths and risky intermediate states is critical for effective process-level safety alignment.

multi-step interaction. (CH2) Robust process-level
alignment requires diverse preference pairs to enable fine-grained optimization (Lightman et al.,
2023). Safesearch (Zhan et al., 2025), the primary
safety focused search agent approach, concentrates
its safety signals on final outputs and queries, leaving other risky actions under-addressed. However,
in search agent workflows, safety violations may
emerge at multiple stages of the interaction process,
including early risky reasoning, reasoning under
risky contexts (Xu et al., 2024), and final response
generation. These heterogeneous failures require
localized correction, complicating consistent preference supervision across the interaction process.
To tackle these barriers, we propose COMPASS,
a Cognitive MCTS-Guided Process Alignment for
Safe Search Agents designed to harmonize robust
safety alignment with high-performance general
utility. To address CH1, we introduce the cognitive tree exploration (CTE), a Monte Carlo
Tree Search (MCTS) mechanism guided by cognitive Q-value estimation. It replaces costly rollouts
with safety-aware evaluation, enabling efficient
exploration of stealthy multi-step attack trajectories. This enables high-quality process supervision
data with minimal computational overhead. To
address CH2, we develop the introspective stepwise alignment (ISA) module. ISA first identifies unsafe intermediate actions through risk node
selection and model introspection. It then transforms these risky states into step-level preference
pairs for targeted supervision. This enables handling diverse safety violations through localized
correction across the entire agent workflow. We
summarize our contributions as follows: (1) We are
the first to introduce MCTS-guided process-level
exploration into safety alignment for LLM-based
search agents. (2) We propose COMPASS, a cognitive alignment framework that combines Cognitive Tree Exploration (CTE) for efficient high-risk
trajectory discovery with Introspective Step-wise
Alignment (ISA) for robust process defense. (3)
Empirical results show that COMPASS achieves
robust safety alignment with limited data while
preserving general utility.

Related Work

2.1

Evolution of Search Agent

LLM-based search agents are commonly categorized into prompt-based and reinforcement learning (RL)-based methods. Prompt-based strategies (Trivedi et al., 2023; Asai et al., 2023; Jiang

et al., 2023; Jeong et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024a;
Amplayo et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023) rely on manually designed workflows for query generation, retrieval, and evidence extraction, but depend heavily
on the base LLM and struggle to filter noisy information (Li et al., 2025). RL-based methods optimize autonomous search trajectories end-to-end,
with recent outcome-supervised approaches extending RL to agentic RAG for complex inference (Jin
et al., 2025a; Wu et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025;
Zheng et al., 2025). However, outcome-rewarded
RL often suffers from reward sparsity, high computational costs and inefficient exploration. Recent
work has started to address search agent safety.
Safesearch jointly optimizes safety and utility with
final-output rewards and a query-level safety reward (Zhan et al., 2025). However, its intermediate
supervision is limited to generated queries, leaving non-query action risks insufficiently localized.
Although process-supervised RL has improved reasoning (Wang et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025; Khalifa
et al., 2025) and search utility (Leng et al., 2025;
Zhang et al., 2025a; Guan et al., 2025), its application to safety-oriented search agents remains underexplored.
2.2

LLM Safety Alignment

Aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) for helpfulness and harmlessness typically involves Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) (Wei et al., 2022) followed by Reinforcement Learning from Human
or AI Feedback (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al.,
2022b). Standard policy updates typically rely
on Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017) to achieve effective alignment.
Recent advancements such as Direct Preference
Optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) and
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao
et al., 2024) further enhance stability by bypassing explicit reward modeling or utilizing groupwise comparisons. Prior work (Tan et al., 2025;
Huang et al., 2025; Dai et al., 2024) studies the
safety-utility trade-off problem in LLM alignment.
STAIR (Zhang et al., 2025b) explores introspective safety alignment for CoT-style LLM reasoning.
However, search agents introduce distinct workflow risks across actions, requiring more granular
intervention. Accordingly, we propose a processsupervised approach that enforces fine-grained
safety across the agent workflow via cognitive exploration and step-wise preference optimization.

Methodology

3.1

Problem Formulation

We cast the iterative reasoning mechanism of
retrieval-augmented generation into the framework
of a Markov Decision Process (Sutton and Barto,
2018) (MDP), defined by the tuple (S, A, T , R).
Here, S denotes the state space, A represents the
action space, T characterizes the state transition
dynamics, and R constitutes the reward function.
States. The state st ∈ S represents the evolving
interaction history at step t. It is formalized as st =
[(q1 , r1 ), · · · , (qt , rt )], where each pair consists of
a search query qi and its corresponding response
ri . The initial red-teaming query x is treated as the
global context that conditions the entire reasoning
process. The transition to a new state st+1 and its
associated value estimation are defined based on
the joint context (x, st ).
Actions. At state st , the model selects the next
action from its action space A, which consists of
three types of actions:
• Answer Generation: Given the initial question x and previous context, the model decomposes the query and conducts a selfassessment to determine if its internal knowledge is sufficient. It aims to provide precise responses for utility questions or safe
and helpful responses for red-teaming scenarios. If confident, the model outputs the
final answer enclosed in <answer></answer>
tags and the terminal state is st+1 =
[(q1 , r1 ), · · · , (qt , rt ), rt+1 ]. Otherwise, it
proceeds to the Query Generation action.
• Query Generation: Guided by the decomposed sub-questions and identified knowledge
gaps, the model formulates concise search
queries enclosed in <query></query> tags,
invoking the search system to fetch the topk relevant evidence from external corpora.
After this action, the state turns to st+1 =
[(q1 , r1 ), · · · , (qt , rt ), qt+1 ].
• Evidence Extraction: To mitigate noise and
conserve context tokens, the model filters
the top-k search results.
It selectively
extracts core information pieces et+1 instead of indiscriminately appending raw documents to the reasoning process. Then
the query response rt+1 is generated with

et+1 . The new state is updated to st+1 =
[(q1 , r1 ), · · · , (qt , rt ), (qt+1 , rt+1 )].
The overall objective is to find an optimal exploration policy π ∗ that maximizes the expected cumulative reward over the dynamic interaction turns.
Inspired by multi-step jailbreak formulations (Yan
et al., 2025), this is mathematically formalized as
maximizing the expected vulnerability of the target
model across any reasoning step t:

Cognitive Q-value Estimation. Standard MCTS
rollouts encounter substantial computational costs
and high variance, while the exponential expansion
of the state space precludes the efficient isolation
of high-quality attack paths. These limitations arise
from inherent reward sparsity and the expansive action space in multi-step reasoning. To address this,
we utilize a reasoning-enhanced LLM as a cognitive heuristic to directly estimate the intermediate
Q-value Q(x, st−1 , at ). For non-terminal actions,
this evaluator assesses the trajectory’s stealthiness
and attack progression. Specifically, it performs
reasoning to evaluate how effectively malicious
intents are decomposed into seemingly benign subqueries while simultaneously gauging the probability of achieving a terminal violation. By replacing
expensive rollouts with this dense process supervision, cognitive Q-value provides immediate signals
to steer the UCT selection toward highly vulnerable
search spaces.
Terminal Reward Function. Unlike utilityoriented MCTS which leverages deterministic
ground-truth labels for terminal verification, redteaming tasks inherently lack such objective signals.
Given the semantic complexity of harmful content,
we employ a well-aligned reasoning model as the
safety evaluator. The model utilizes its reasoning
and alignment capabilities to quantify the severity
of the generated response. Conditioned on the ini-

tial malicious query, it assigns a terminal reward
score Rtatt in the Answer Generation state.
Instead of converging on overt violations, this
strategy steers the search toward stealthy attack trajectories, facilitating the discovery of non-trivial
reasoning paths that effectively bypass surfacelevel safety filters. This decoupled dual-signal
mechanism ensures the generation of high-quality
process supervision data for subsequent alignment.

3.3

Introspective Step-wise Alignment

Leveraging CTE trajectories, we build a step-level
supervised dataset for robust search agent alignment. As demonstrated in prior work (Lai et al.,
2024), long-chain reasoning workflows are optimized most effectively when errors are penalized at
the precise step they first occur. Inspired by this insight, we extend this step-wise correction paradigm
to the safety alignment of multi-step agent workflows.
Step-level Preference Data Construction. We
construct preference pairs in the form of
(s̃t , ysafe , yunsafe ). In this formulation, the context
s̃t = (x, st ) concatenates the initial red-teaming
query and the interaction history up to step t. The
rejected action yunsafe is the risky output based on
s̃t , while the chosen action ysafe is an introspective
safer response elicited via a specific prompt template (see Appendix C). Specifically, this template
directs the aligned base model to identify safety
risks within the current trajectory and replace the
unsafe action with a format-compliant alternative

that provides constructive guidance or a safe pivot.
To pinpoint optimal states for optimization, we
establish a terminal success threshold ϕ and an
intermediate risk threshold γ. A trajectory is identified as a successful attack if its leaf node risk
satisfies Rtatt ≥ ϕ. For these trajectories, we pinpoint the first risky state as the earliest node where
Rtatt ≥ γ to penalize the root cause, while simultaneously targeting subsequent actions for risk
propagation and the terminal action for the final
harmful response. Furthermore, we extract states
with the first high Q-values action (where Q ≥ γ)
from failed attack trajectories to robustify the agent
against stealthy, highly-rated but ultimately benignending deceptive intents.
This selection mechanism provides the structured supervisory signals. By isolating the first
risky node, subsequent risk propagation, and terminal responses, it transforms heterogeneous deviations into high-quality preference data. These
nodes represent different risk stages, tracking the
safety deviation from initial deviation and risk propagation to the final violation. Consequently, it ensures a comprehensive defense across the entire
agentic RAG trajectory.
Fine-grained Preference Optimization. Traditional alignment paradigms predominantly target
single-turn terminal responses. However, securing agentic workflows requires a dual-objective approach. Our method prioritizes the preservation of
multi-step reasoning ability while simultaneously
imposing step-level safety constraints.
To mitigate the alignment tax (Dai et al., 2024),
we optimize the base model in two sequential
stages using a mixed dataset of utility and safety
samples. The first stage employs Supervised FineTuning (SFT) to instill search agent workflow
norms through a document masking mechanism.
In the second stage, we use Step-wise Direct Preference Optimization anchored at the specific deviation state s̃t . This approach trains the policy πθ to
maximize the margin between chosen safe actions
and rejected risky ones according to the following
objective function:

Experiment

4.1

Experimental Setup

Evaluation Dataset. Following the setting in Safesearch (Zhan et al., 2025), our evaluation setup
comprises two categories of benchmarks. (1) Redteaming Datasets. We assess safety using three
datasets containing harmful inputs. Specifically,
we utilize a 919-example subset from the Redteaming Resistance Benchmark (RRB) (Li and Tang,
2024) along with 313 harmful instructions from
StrongREJECT (Souly et al., 2024). We also incorporate a 500-example subset from WildTeaming (Jiang et al., 2024b), which is particularly significant for its use of diverse jailbreaking techniques such as role-play to elicit higher harmful
rates compared to standard benchmarks. (2) Utility
Datasets. To evaluate utility, we use three standard
benchmarks. These include TriviaQA (Joshi et al.,
2017) for single-hop reasoning, HotpotQA (Yang
et al., 2018) for multi-hop reasoning across documents, and Bamboogle (Press et al., 2023) for
challenging compositional reasoning tasks, following (Zhan et al., 2025). For evaluation, we sample
500 QA pairs from TriviaQA and HotpotQA, and
use the full 125-pair test set from Bamboogle.
Baselines. We compare our proposed method
against seven baseline methods as follows: Base
Model, which relies solely on the zero-shot capabilities of the model without any external retrieval
tools. Naive RAG, which retrieves relevant documents by directly using the user instruction as the
search query. Base Agent, where the aligned base
model is utilized as search agent backbone model
directly. +Query Filtering, which utilizes GPTOSS-120B (Agarwal et al., 2025) as the guardrail to
directly filter the unsafe queries and skip retrieval.
+Document Filtering, which removes the unsafe
retrieved documents with the GPT-OSS-120B as
the guardrail. Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025a), which
fine-tunes the base model exclusively using utility
training data via outcome-rewarded RL training.
Safesearch (Zhan et al., 2025), which fine-tunes
using a mixture of safety and utility data.
Metrics. We evaluate safety using Harmful Rate
(H) and Helpfulness Score (Hp), and assess utility
using Exact Match (EM) and F1 scores. Safety
judgments are obtained from GPT-4o-mini follow-

ing the helpful-and-harmless principle (Bai et al.,
2022a), with detailed evaluation prompts and metric definitions provided in Appendix A.1 & C.
Training Data. To train the target model, we utilize a mixed dataset comprising both utility and
safety data. For the utility data, we follow the
work (Zhang et al., 2025a) and prepare 8,000 QA
questions. And we select 2,000 instances from
WildTeaming training set as the safety data. Specifically, we employ Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (QwenTeam, 2024) as the policy model to generate attack
trajectories and do introspection in our experiment.
We use DeepSeek-V3.2 (Liu et al., 2025) as the
scoring model in the CTE process.
To ensure the fair comparison, all baselines
use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (QwenTeam, 2024) and
Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) as
the backbone model. More implementation details
are listed in the Appendix A.1.
4.2

Overall Performance

Table 1 presents the overall evaluation of COMPASS across search agent safety, safe-response
helpfulness and reasoning utility, with key findings
reported below.
(i) External Retrieval Undermines Base Model
Safety. The experiment further validates the core
motivation of our work. While naive RAG, base
agent and Search-R1 method significantly enhance
utility for different LLM backbones, they trigger
a severe degradation in safety. It is obvious that
naive RAG leads to higher harmful rates, as malicious questions are directly amplified by external knowledge. More importantly, even with a
think-before-search mechanism, safety risks remain non-negligible. Deceptive task decomposition can mask harmful intent, where individually
benign sub-queries lead to combined harmful outputs. This phenomenon underscores the necessity
of fine-grained process supervision to prevent logic
jailbreaking during multi-step execution.
(ii) External guardrails provide only partial mitigation. Although +Query Filtering and +Document Filtering lower harmfulness in some cases,
their average Harmful Rate remains high at 32.0%
and 33.8%, respectively, compared with 22.4% for
Safesearch and 16.3% for COMPASS. This suggests that filtering unsafe queries or retrieved documents alone cannot fully prevent risk propagation
inside the agent workflow. In contrast, COMPASS
strengthens the agent’s intrinsic safety through stepwise alignment, enabling safer reasoning beyond

surface-level retrieval control. Detailed results and
analysis are provided in Appendix A.3.
(iii) Safety-Utility Trade-off with High Data Efficiency. As shown in Table 1, COMPASS achieves a
favorable safety-utility trade-off with substantially
less training data. Across the three red-teaming
benchmarks, COMPASS obtains the lowest average
Harmful Rate of 18.0%, while maintaining competitive helpfulness scores. Although Safesearch
achieves slightly higher helpfulness on the Qwen7B backbone, COMPASS surpasses it on Llama-8B
and uses far fewer safety training samples. For utility, COMPASS is slightly behind Search-R1 on
TriviaQA and HotpotQA, but achieves the best average EM score among all baselines and the secondbest average F1 score. Considering that Search-R1
uses nearly 8× more training data, these results
indicate that COMPASS preserves general search
utility while improving safety in a data-efficient
manner. This efficiency is also reflected in training cost, where COMPASS requires substantially
fewer GPU hours than Safesearch, as detailed in
Appendix A.3.
(iv) Strong Cross-Model Generalization of CTE.
Another finding in our experiments is the robust
transferability of the synthesized datasets. Although the attack trajectories and preference pairs
are originally generated using Qwen-7B, they
prove highly effective when applied to the Llama8B architecture. As shown in Table 1, the Llama
model exhibits a substantial reduction in average
Harmful Rate at 14.6%. Crucially, this cross-model
fine-tuning also significantly enhances the helpfulness score of the Llama model, rising from 1.29 in
its base state to 2.06 after alignment in StrongReject evaluation. We attribute this improvement to
the self-introspection trajectories generated during
the ISA process. This demonstrates the transferability of COMPASS-distilled safety principles across
model architectures. Consequently, our method
provides a scalable solution that can effectively
align and enhance various LLMs without modelspecific trajectory synthesis. Evaluator robustness
is further verified with Qwen3-Max (Yang et al.,
2025) and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (Anthropic, 2025b),
as shown in Tables 7 and 8.

5

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce COMPASS, a processaligned framework designed to enhance the safety
of LLM-based search agents while preserving their
task utility. Specifically, we propose Cognitive
Tree Exploration (CTE) and Introspective Stepwise Alignment (ISA) to identify and rectify risky
action states that lead to logical jailbreaking in
multi-step agentic workflows. Empirical results
demonstrate that COMPASS achieves a favorable
safety-utility trade-off, substantially reducing harmful outputs while maintaining competitive utility
across multiple benchmarks.

Limitations
We acknowledge two primary limitations of
this work. First, constrained by hardware resources from scaling to larger LLMs, we validate
COMPASS only using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and
Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, but both models consistently
demonstrate the effectiveness of our method in enhancing safety while preserving utility. Second, our
safety search agent primarily operates within agentic RAG scenarios evaluated against red-teaming
benchmarks and a local retrieval corpus. Future
research should aim to expand its versatility by exploring a broader range of agent-based operational
contexts.

Ethical Considerations
This work focuses on enhancing the safety of LLMbased search agents through data-efficient alignment. Our goal is to mitigate harmful behaviors
while strictly preserving reasoning utility under limited supervision. We strictly adhere to ethical standards in our experimental design and data usage.
All red-teaming and QA benchmarks utilized in this
study are publicly available. Our research involves
no collection or utilization of private personal data,
ensuring full compliance with privacy regulations.
To foster community growth and reproducibility,
we will publicly release our core code and specialized prompts. Although automated LLM-based
evaluation may introduce residual bias, we mitigate this risk through multi-model evaluation and
human validation of Q-value estimation. These
checks improve robustness but cannot fully eliminate subjectivity in safety assessment. While our
framework is designed for defense, we recognize
the dual-use risk of red-teaming research and mitigate it by focusing on defensive alignment, limiting
harmful procedural details, and providing safe deployment guidelines.

Algorithm

As illustrated in Algorithm 1&2, we provide the
detailed algorithm table of our Cognitive Tree Exploration and Introspective Step-wise Alignment in

Expansion: Upon reaching a leaf node via the
selection policy, the agent performs self-directed
reasoning to expand the current state st by exploring actions sampled from the candidate space A.
Crucially, within our framework, the Answer Generation action is defined as a definitive terminal
operator; its invocation concludes the trajectory
and effectively halts any further state development.
To ensure convergence and efficiency, if a trajectory
reaches the maximum depth Tmax without a natural termination, the expansion phase is truncated,
forcing an immediate Answer Generation based on
the accumulated context to finalize the response.
