# Conclusion reliance under low domain knowledge

When a topic needs specialist knowledge the receiver lacks, the receiver cannot evaluate the evidence or reasoning in an answer. In this lab study of university students (chemical-science topics versus easy general topics; surveys followed by 22 interviews), such receivers leaned on the conclusions stated in the material and mostly kept their initial opinion.[^c1]

**Mechanism as the authors describe it.** Subjects with enough domain knowledge notice the quality of the deduction; ordinary subjects are mainly affected by the conclusions and arguments themselves.[^c2] For non-professionals, any information can help a decision as long as it appears scientific, trustworthy and from a seemingly dedicated source.[^c3] Without domain knowledge, the decision depends heavily on the first impression and first decision on the topic.[^c4] The authors read this through the Elaboration Likelihood Model: effortful central-route processing occurs when the topic is personally relevant, familiar, and the person has domain knowledge; otherwise a peripheral, cue-driven route is used. That framing is their interpretation, not a tested mediation.[^c5]

**Verification cost.** The information validation process becomes more costly as topics become more domain-specific, and many participants either did not want to or could not fact-check what they received.[^c6] In-domain participants, by contrast, quickly fact-checked independently and focused on the professional content rather than the authors' conclusions, even for unfamiliar in-domain topics.[^c7]

**Source labels.** Information labelled as AI-generated was found equivalently helpful as information labelled as human-written.[^c8] In the interviews, no participant reported detecting AI-generated content, and even in-domain participants assumed the sources were accurate.[^c9] Some who disliked AI still found it useful in the task, partly because they were less suspicious of text that appeared to come from a decent non-AI source and saw no obvious errors.[^c10]

**Consequence for asking and answering (my inference).** An answer's conclusion carries the weight, so on expert topics a confident conclusion from any source is likely to be adopted by a non-expert whether or not the reasoning is sound. A question that elicits the evidence is only useful to a receiver who can read it.

## Limits

- Students only, one domain pair (chemistry versus non-chemistry), small interview sample (22); the authors say findings may not generalise across cultures, domains or demographics.[^c11]
- No systematic quality control of the AI content, so the "AI equals human" result may partly reflect the stimuli.[^c12]
- Decision quality was not the primary measure; the study shows who changes opinion, not who ends up correct.
- Interview claims (for example "no one detected AIGC") are self-reports. The ELM and Technology Acceptance Model readings are interpretation, not tests.
- The "AI label is equally helpful" result is about helpfulness, not trust. A later survey and lab study of health text found that the AI label lowered rated trust, with human-labelled text trusted more than AI-labelled text, and no interaction with the actual source.[^c13] Whether a non-expert's opinion moves under an AI label is therefore not settled by this note's finding.
