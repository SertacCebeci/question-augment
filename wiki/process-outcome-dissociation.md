# Process-outcome dissociation

A tool can improve the process around learning (the teacher's workload, the
speed of feedback, the time students spend, the consistency of their marks)
while the outcome that process exists to serve stays where it was. Measuring
one does not measure the other. The vocational proof-of-concept shows both
halves in one deployment. Lecturers "reported streamlined workflows, reduced
cognitive" load.[^p1] On the exam, the AI group scored a mean of 17.04 marks
against 17.43 for the control group, "showing no statistically significant
difference."[^p2] The paper's own summary puts the split plainly: "Despite
clear gains in teaching efficiency, student performance data showed no
significant improvement in".[^p3]

The process gains were not imaginary. The standard deviation of marks fell,
"standard deviation reduction from 5.15 to 4.70 (8.7% decrease)", and the
authors read this as the tool "contributed to more consistent performance
across students."[^p4] The authors draw the general lesson with a hedge:
"These findings indicate that AI tools enhance learning processes and
consistency, without necessarily" translating into assessment outcomes.[^p5]
They also name a condition under which process benefits fail to convert:
they "do not directly translate to improved exam scores when engagement
patterns involve dependency or superficial interaction with content."[^p6]

## What the split changes

A question of whether a tool "helps" has more than one answer, depending on
which quantity is named. Efficiency, reported clarity, consistency and time
on task are quick to collect and here they rose. The score, which is what
the course exists to produce, did not. An answer that cites only the first
set is accurate about the process and silent about the learning. The paper
keeps some value for the process: the extra effort "has intrinsic value for
skill development, even if not immediately reflected in" assessment.[^p7]
That is a claim about what to count, not a reason to treat the two as one.

The same deployment shows a second split, between what people perceived and
what the records showed. Lecturers reported that weaker students "gained
confidence," but "the chat logs show that this confidence was built" on
dependency rather than learning.[^p8] A perception measure and a behaviour
measure disagreed inside one study, and only the second was tied to the
outcome.

## Limits

- One deployment, one module's examination, one cohort. The dissociation is
  the paper's finding in a single case; this note does not establish that it
  is general.
- No baseline. The AI group's final performance matched or exceeded that of
  high-performing comparison cohorts, but "the absence of baseline data
  creates interpretative ambiguity": the data cannot say whether the tool
  lifted weaker students or left strong ones where they started.[^p9]
- The score comparison is a single immediate examination. A null on the mean
  is not evidence of no learning, and no delayed retention measure is
  reported in the sections read.
- The process gains are lecturer- and self-reported as well as measured;
  the sections read do not show them causally attributed to the tool.
- The sections read give the difference of 0.39 marks as not significant but
  do not give an interval for it, so the size of any effect the study could
  have missed is not stated.
