Abstract

This raises the following open question: how does the need
for reasoning tokens scale with input size for a given task? In
particular, what is the minimum reasoning budget required

Inference-time scaling via chain-of-thought (CoT)
reasoning is a major driver of state-of-the-art
LLM performance, but it comes with substantial latency and compute costs. We address a
fundamental theoretical question: how many reasoning tokens are required to solve a problem as
input size grows? By extending the bounded attention prefix oracle (BAPO) model—an abstraction
of LLMs that quantifies the information flow required to solve a task—we prove lower bounds
on the CoT tokens required for three canonical
BAPO-hard tasks: binary majority, triplet matching, and graph reachability. We show that each requires Ω(n) reasoning tokens when the input size
is n. We complement these results with matching or near-matching upper bounds via explicit
constructions. Finally, our experiments with frontier reasoning models show approximately linear
reasoning token scaling on these tasks and failures when constrained to smaller reasoning budgets, consistent with our theoretical lower bounds.
Together, our results identify fundamental bottlenecks in inference-time compute through CoT
and offer a principled tool for analyzing optimal
reasoning length.

There is a growing body of empirical work on compressing
and optimizing reasoning chains (Sui et al., 2025). However,
these evaluations are conducted on benchmarks where input
size is not a natural scaling parameter, and compression is
therefore reported as a constant-factor reduction (e.g. “30%
shorter”). As input sizes grow, does the required reasoning length remain roughly constant, grow linearly, or even
faster? Principled guidance and theoretical understanding of
when compression is even possible is still largely missing.
This paper helps fill in that gap by providing asymptotic
lower and upper bounds on reasoning chain length for three
problems that are hard for LLMs to solve without reasoning (Schnabel et al., 2025). The resulting lower bounds
and experimental results help confirm the token complexity
hypothesis of Lee et al. (2025), which states that every problem has an inherent minimal reasoning chain length, and
align with reasoning length bounds for unique hard-attention
transformers (Bavandpour et al., 2025).
Our theory relies on a previously identified bottleneck in
transformers, namely that the amount of information a transformer can accurately process while producing a single
token is limited. This is formalized in the bounded attention
prefix oracle (BAPO) model (Schnabel et al., 2025). For
problems that are hard in this framework, i.e., require a lot
of information to be communicated, chain of thought (CoT;
Wei et al., 2022)1 enables LLMs to solve them. We show in
this paper that these BAPO-hard problems carry high CoT
inference costs both in theory and practice.

1. Introduction
State-of-the-art transformer-based large language models
(LLMs) rely on inference-time scaling—allocating additional test-time compute by generating intermediate tokens—
to improve performance on challenging tasks (Snell et al.,
2025). A main drawback of inference-time scaling is that it
is costly: even with KV caching, generating n reasoning tokens entails superlinear compute and latency due to the cost
of attention growing quadratically (Vaswani et al., 2017).

In particular, we prove that three BAPO-hard problems—
binary majority, triplet matching, and graph reachability—
require at least Ω(n) CoT tokens to be solved by a constantbandwidth BAPO with input size n. Consequently, for these
tasks, we expect the cost and latency of CoT to scale at
least as fast as the problem size, regardless of the particular
CoT strategy employed. Figure 1 shows an information flow

Intuitively, the final layer of a transformer must have all relevant information about the input in order to produce a correct
next-token prediction. The BAPO model captures this by
considering how input information crosses an arbitrary line
dividing the input into a prefix and a suffix. Preprocessed
information about the prefix (i.e., representations after each
layer) can cross this line, but due to causal attention, the
preprocessing done in the prefix must be independent of the
suffix. Limits on this information flow pathway are captured
by the prefix bandwidth. Alternatively, attention in the suffix can pull in prefix token values in a way that depends on
the suffix, and the attention bandwidth captures how many
tokens can be effectively attended to without information
loss. We provide a deeper introduction to the BAPO model
in Appendix A and refer readers to Schnabel et al. (2025)
for a thorough exposition.

Finally, we test frontier reasoning models to see how many
reasoning tokens they use in practice on these problems.
We find that GPT-5.2 with reasoning exhibits linear token
scaling when solving the binary majority, triplet matching,
and graph reachability problems, as predicted by our theory.
Gemini 2.5 Pro uses even more tokens. We also find that imposing a too small reasoning budget degrades performance
substantially, consistent with our lower bounds.
Overall, our results show that inference-time scaling via
reasoning tokens can be a bottleneck on hard tasks: linear
lower bounds on required reasoning length translate into at
least quadratic compute in practice as context grows, motivating the need for alternatives such as new architectures
or more effective tool use. At the same time, our empirical results show that frontier reasoning models often use
substantially more tokens than the linear lower-bounds require, suggesting room for constant-factor efficiency gains
even when sublinear scaling is information-theoretically
impossible under BAPO bandwidth limits.

5.1. Self-Consistency and cBAPO
In our analysis of upper bounds, we will focus on a restricted version of the original BAPO model that limits
the power of the prefix oracle f in the original model to
align more closely with the way transformer computations
happen. There is a certain self-similarity in how causallymasked transformers work: the result of the computation on
a sequence x1 , . . . , xk+1 , is the result of the transformers’
new computation on xk+1 merged with its old computation on the prefix x1 , . . . , xk . This motivates the additional
constraint of self-consistency: any prefix result needs to
be computable by a BAPO. In addition to capturing this
computational constraint in real transformers better, it also
provides more realistic upper bounds on token complexity.

This makes upper bounds on token complexity unrealistically low as we can construct a linear-time constantbandwidth BAPO-CoT algorithm for any problem. The
BAPO-CoT only needs to write a second copy of the input,
at which point it can use the input-doubling loophole to
solve the problem.

5.2. Upper Bounds via Turing Machine Simulation
Schnabel et al. (2025) show that we can construct a constantbandwidth BAPO-CoT that simulates a Turing machine
running on the problem with a number of CoT tokens scaling with the time and space complexity of the problem.
This means that we can directly translate the complexity of
classical algorithms into token complexity upper bounds.
For BAPO-CoTs, the n + 1 upper bound from Proposition 5.4 makes this approach useless, as simulating Turing
machines only yields higher bounds. However, we show
that this approach is useful for cBAPO-CoTs by proving that
constant-bandwidth cBAPO-CoTs can also simulate Turing
machines. Moreover, we strengthen the one-tape Turing
machine simulation of Schnabel et al. (2025) to multi-tape,
multi-head Turing machines, improving the token complexity bounds we obtain from this approach. For example,
Theorem 5.5 implies a token complexity bound for M A JORITY of O(n2 ) using the two-head Turing machine that
matches up 0s and 1s, improving on the O(n3 ) upper bound
implied by a single-head construction.

For R EACHABILITY, the idea is to perform a graph search
(like BFS/DFS), tracking the set of nodes being explored
and the set of visited nodes in the CoT. Here, we find a
O(n2 ) upper bound, which is tight with our Ω(m) lower
bound in dense graphs with m = Θ(n2 ).

Several papers show theoretically how CoT increases the
power of transformers. Pérez et al. (2021) show that encoderdecoder transformers with unbounded CoT-style steps over
rational vectors are Turing-complete. Merrill & Sabharwal
(2024) show that any problem in TIME(t(n)) is solvable
by a decoder-only transformer in at most t(n) CoT steps, a
finer-grained characterization. Feng et al. (2023) argue that
CoT enables transformers to solve dynamic programming
problems that bounded-depth transformers cannot. Providing a different distributional perspective, Abbe et al. (2024)
show how CoT (or “scratchpads”) can reduce the globality
degree of a task, the number of inputs tokens needed to correlate with the answer (beyond the input token histogram).
This is very similar in spirit to the BAPO-CoT result that
CoT reduces the required bandwidth (≈ number of attended
tokens) of tasks.

6.3. Do Models Naturally Decompose Problems into
BAPO-Easy Steps?
The failure or refusal of GPT-5.2 to count 1s one at a time
(as seen in Table 2, Appendix E.4) highlights that the existence of a BAPO-easy decomposition is not a guarantee
that an LLM will execute it, even when instructed. This is
even true when the model is clearly capable of the decomposition: while we cannot see the internal reasoning applied
by the model in Figure 2, it seems likely, given the linear
scaling of CoT tokens, that GPT-5.2 with internal reasoning
does reliably count 1s when faced with M AJORITY. This
raises an important question: for what problems do models
effectively perform BAPO-easy decompositions when reasoning? Interestingly, with only an instruction to “think step
by step,” GPT-5.2 often applies the linear BAPO-CoT algorithm for M ATCH 3n used in our construction (see Table 3 in
Appendix E.4, e.g., “Compute needed complement for each
a”). For R EACHABILITY, the plain CoT prompt also elicits
a graph search (see Table 4); this is perhaps unsurprising,
as it is the most natural way to approach the problem. The
case of M AJORITY does show that models can take shortcuts (e.g., trying to jump to the ones count in a single step),
leading to failures. For other problems with less obvious
BAPO-easy decompositions, or with more tempting shortcuts, it seems likely that providing explicit guidance for
how to decompose a problem or training models to perform
BAPO-easy decompositions could help performance.

Few lower bounds on CoT length are known. Li et al. (2024)
show if we only allow O(log n) CoT steps, this does not
increase the expressivity of transformers beyond AC0 or
TC0 (with constant- or log-precision, respectively). This implies that any problem outside of TC0 , for instance, requires
ω(log n) CoT steps. Merrill & Sabharwal (2024) show that
O(n) CoT steps are insufficient for recognizing context-free
languages in general (given a complexity theory conjecture about the efficiency of context-free parsing) and that
O(log n) steps are insufficient for NL- and P-complete problems. Most similarly to our work, Bavandpour et al. (2025)
derive bounds on CoT length for transformers with unique
hard attention (UHATs), showing that parity, multiplication,
and median require Ω(n) CoT steps, while reachability on
directed acyclic graphs requires Ω(|E| log |V |). They also
show upper bounds that are tight for each problem (at least
within a log factor). Bavandpour et al. (2025) similarly
use a Turing machine simulation for UHATs to establish

substantial costs in practice. Practically, this motivates a
need for faster inference methods, but more fundamentally,
other architectures or paradigms such as looped transformers (Giannou et al., 2023) might yield better scaling.

construction, the space complexity is encoded in the CoT

CoT alphabet Γ to grow with s(n), then using their tapeupdate construction idea would also produce a cBAPO-CoT

perspective on CoT length is provided by viewing CoT as

Implications for reasoning compression. A key limitation of the current empirical literature is that it does not pin
down the scaling law of minimal reasoning length. Because
many widely used benchmarks lack a clean instance-size
parameter, results are framed as percentage compression or
fixed token budgets (constant-factor improvements). One
of our contributions is to make the scaling question explicit by analyzing parametric problem families. Our results
suggest that for tasks whose minimal required reasoning
scales as Ω(n), any compression method that enforces an
input-size-agnostic budget will eventually fail as n grows.
Conversely, if a task’s required reasoning is o(n) but the
reasoning method uses superlinearly many tokens, then aggressive shortening may be possible. This motivates evaluating compression methods on problems of varying token
complexities and, within each problem, varying input sizes.

On the empirical side, Lee et al. (2025) propose the “token
complexity hypothesis” that every problem has some minimal reasoning chain length. This hypothesis is informed
by their experiments finding that across different chainshortening prompts, LLMs tend to succeed if the chain is
longer than some critical length and fail otherwise (Lee
et al., 2025). Wu et al. (2026) argue that problems have
an optimal reasoning length (increasing accuracy at first as
chain length increases, then decreasing beyond the optimal
length) and analyze a simple subtask decomposition and
error accumulation model that reproduces this phenomenon.
Other empirical research supports the observation that more
reasoning tends to increase performance, especially on more
challenging tasks (Jin et al., 2024). However, this finding
varies across task type: Feng et al. (2026) find on mathematical and scientific reasoning tasks that longer CoT chains
are correlated with lower accuracy.

Open theoretical questions. Our investigation leaves a
number of open questions. Are there problems with superlinear cBAPO-CoT token complexity? In particular, can
R EACHABILITY be solved by a cBAPO-CoT with O(m+n)
CoT tokens, or does it require Θ(n2 )? With constant-size
Σ, can constant-bandwidth cBAPOs be simulated by Turing
machines? (Propositions 4.4 and 5.3 show the answer is no
for BAPOs and for cBAPOs with unbounded Σ.)

Approaches for reducing the token cost of CoT in practice
include fine-tuning models on shortened chains (Liu et al.,
2024; Xia et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025), reinforcement
learning with a reward for shorter chains (Luo et al., 2025;
Yi et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2026), prompting for brevity (Lee
et al., 2025; Han et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Nayab et al.,
2025), and adaptive prediction of the benefit of generating
more tokens (Zabounidis et al., 2026). Fan et al. (2025)
discuss different approaches for empirically measuring CoT
token efficiency. For a survey, see Sui et al. (2025). These
papers typically report constant-factor efficiency gains on
fixed benchmarks, while we focus on how reasoning length
scales asymptotically with input size—a crucial factor when
applying LLMs to ever-larger problems.

Limitations and future work. BAPO-CoT is an abstraction of transformer computation that isolates a per-step communication bottleneck; it does not model all features of
modern systems (e.g., tool use or retrieval). Our experiments are limited to a small set of tasks and model access
constraints. Future work includes: (i) extending token complexity bounds to other problems, (ii) developing principled
evaluation suites for reasoning compression that vary instance size, and (iii) studying how architectural changes or
externalized computation affect token complexity.

8. Discussion
Our main message is that there are fundamental limits to
how efficient reasoning can be for LLMs on tasks that require a lot of information to be communicated inside the
model, as captured through the BAPO framework. Our
main theoretical contributions are token complexity lower
bounds for M AJORITY, M ATCH 3n , and R EACHABILITY
showing they require linearly many reasoning tokens for a
constant-bandwidth BAPO-CoT.

A. Introduction to BAPO
While Schnabel et al. (2025) provides the authoritative introduction of the BAPO model, we provide an overview here
for completeness. The idea behind the BAPO model is that information about the input must somehow propagate to the
last token’s residual stream in order for the transformer to make a correct next-token prediction. This information could
either take the form of (representations of) explicit token values, or some kind of summary statistic computed by earlier
residual streams. For instance, when solving PARITY, the last residual stream does not need accurate representations of
every previous token; a representation of the parities of different portions of the input suffices to compute the parity of the
full input. On the other hand, when solving I NDEX (given a string and an index, find the token at the given index), the last
residual stream needs to know the exact token value at the specified index. The BAPO model captures these two pathways
of information flow in a transformer and hypothesizes that there are bandwidth limits on the amount of information that can
accurately pass through these two pathways.
There are three components of a BAPO, the functions f, g, and h. The prefix oracle f captures all preprocessing performed
by early residual streams of a transformer. The attention function g represents selective attention by later residual streams
to tokens in the prefix. Finally, the suffix oracle h represents the computation of the correct next token in the late residual
streams (and, in particular, the last residual stream), based on the information provided by f , the tokens attended to by g,
and the tokens in those late residual streams.
To capture the idea that every prefix of residual streams of a transformer itself represents the computation of the transformer
on that prefix, we require that a BAPO be able to solve a problem given any split of the input into a prefix and a suffix. We
use k to represent the split index (the last index of the prefix). Thus, given an input x1 . . . xn , the prefix oracle f receives as
input x1 . . . xk only. The attention function g outputs an attention score for each prefix token, computed as a function of the
suffix xk+1 . . . xn (in the original BAPO model, the scores were binary, but we focus on score-BAPO where the scores are
in [0, 1]). For each prefix index i, we say its attention score is gi = g(xk+1 . . . xn , k, xi , i) (note that the attention function
has access to the index i of the candidate token xi , to model its positional encoding). To impose communication bandwidths
on the model, the output of f is limited to a bits and the suffix oracle only attends to the b tokens with highest non-zero
attention scores gi . In the case of attention score ties, ties are broken arbitrarily, and the BAPO must be able to succeed for
any tie-breaking. Equivalently, we can think of ties being broken adversarially.
Putting all of this together, the suffix oracle h must solve a problem given, for any split index k ∈ {1, . . . , n − 1}:

C. From BAPO to cBAPO
We repeat the defintion for cBAPO here and provide additional context. To resolve the issue that the prefix oracle f is too
powerful in the original BAPO model, we require that the function that f expresses is solved by some BAPO, which itself
uses f as a prefix oracle.6 This aligns with the intuition with the prefix streams of a transformer are subject to the same
bottlenecks as the full model and are applying the same transformer blocks.
Definition 5.1. A function f : Σ∗ → {0, 1}a is (a, b)-BAPO-consistent for a problem p if there exist functions g and
h such that (f, g, h) is an (a, b)-BAPO with input token set Σ and output token set {0, 1}a for which f (x1 . . . xn ) =
h(f (x1 . . . xk ), G, xk+1 . . . xn , k) for all n > 1, all prefixes x1 . . . xn of inputs to p, all split indices k < n, and all possible
attended token sets G.
In this definition, note that we allow the output token set of the BAPO (i.e., the codomain of h) to be different from the input
token set.
If a = 0, then f is trivially (0, b)-BAPO-consistent, since we can pick h to be the constant function that outputs the empty
string ε on all inputs. This h always agrees with f , as f has codomain {0, 1}0 = {ε}. In this construction, b is irrelevant, as
the attended token set is unused by h. When a and b do no need to specified, we simply call f consistent. We then apply this
constraint on prefix oracles to define our restricted version of BAPOs. To distinguish it from the original model, we call the
restricted model cBAPO.
Definition 5.2. A (a, b)-BAPO (f, g, h) where f is (a, b)-BAPO-consistent is called self-consistent, denoted (a, b)-cBAPO.
A problem p is cBAPO-easy if an (O(1), O(1))-cBAPO solves p. Otherwise, p is cBAPO-hard.
We show that this new definition preserves all meaningful known properties of the model, but prevents the input-doubling
loophole. Since cBAPOs are a restriction of BAPOs, BAPO-hardness implies cBAPO-hardness (and cBAPO-easiness
implies BAPO-easiness). Moreover, many problems known to be BAPO-easy have constructions with a = 0, making them
cBAPO-easy as well. This includes I NDEX, C ONTAINSp , M ATCH 2n , M AX/M IN, and L EFTMOST/R IGHTMOST. The only
previously mentioned BAPO-easy problems that are not trivially cBAPO-easy are E QUALITY, D ISJOINTNESS, and regular
language recognition (which includes PARITY and A ND/O R). However, we show that all of these problems are in fact still
cBAPO-easy, as we can find consistent prefix oracles for them. (For E QUALITY and D ISJOINTNESS, this appears to require
a 2-layer cBAPO. See Schnabel et al. (2025) for a formal definition of multi-layer BAPO. Applying the self-consistency
restriction to multi-layer BAPOs yields multi-layer cBAPOs. Like score-BAPO, Schnabel et al. (2025) show that multi-layer
BAPOs preserve the hardness of the canonical BAPO-hard problems.)

the input-doubling construction no longer works for cBAPOs, as the prefix oracle may not be able solve the original problem.

only x1 . . . x⌊n/2⌋ in the prefix. The prefix oracle f doesn’t have enough of the input to solve the problem, nor does the
suffix oracle—this appears to be as hard as solving the original non-doubled problem. (Note that same argument shows that
input tripling, quadrupling, etc do not help, since we can apply the recursive unrolling of f from Definition 5.1 repeatedly
until we arrive at a similarly hard split where neither prefix nor suffix contain the whole input.)

D. Pointer chasing
So far, the problems that we have seen either require only a single step to solve (if they are cBAPO-easy) or at least Ω(n).
Are there BAPO-hard problems that can be solved with sublinear CoT tokens?
Consider the problem of finding q κ (1) for an unknown function q : [n] → [n]. This is a pointer-chasing problem: we can
think of q as an array of pointers to cells in the array. The straightforward solution is to begin at 1, then check q(1), then
q(q(1)), etc., chasing the pointers. We can tune the asymptotic difficulty of this problem by making κ a function of n. When
κ = O(1), the problem is relatively easy, but we could also pick κ = log n or κ = n1−α . See Definition 5.9 for the formal
definition of the κ-P OINTER C HASING problem.
This is related to the two-player pointer chasing problem in communication complexity (Nisan & Widgerson, 1991), where
Alice has pointers into Bob’s list and vice versa. We can embed such an instance in a single function by having the first
half of the domain represent Alice’s array and the second half Bob’s array. Iterated function application also relates more
generally to compositional reasoning.
When κ(n) = O(1), this problem can be solved by a constant-bandwidth, constant-layer cBAPO with full attention (as
defined by Schnabel et al. (2025), where full attention means the attention functions can attend to suffix tokens).
Proposition D.1. For any fixed κ = O(1), there is a κ-layer full-attention (0, 1)-cBAPO that solves κ-P OINTER C HASING.
Proof. The attention functions g1 , . . . , gκ of the cBAPO simply perform the pointer chasing: g1 attends to index 1 so that
G1 = {q(1)}, and each subsequent gi attends to the index in Gi−1 , so that Gi = {q i (1)} by induction. As this cBAPO uses
full attention, it doesn’t matter if the next index to attend to is in the prefix or suffix. It’s also trivially self-consistent since
a = 0. After κ such applications, Gκ contains q κ (1), which can be returned by h.
Additionally, a cBAPO-CoT can solve the problem given κ(n) CoT tokens (Theorem 5.10).

E. Experiment details
E.1. Problem generation
E.1.1. M AJORITY
We generate hard yes instances by randomly shuffling a list of bits with ⌊n/2⌋ + 1 ones and n − (⌊n/2⌋ + 1) zeros. We
generate hard no instances by randomly shuffling a list of bits with ⌊n/2⌋ ones and n − ⌊n/2⌋ zeros.
E.1.2. M ATCH 3n
We set the modulus to be m = 2n + 1.
For yes instances, we first generate a uniformly random pair of distinct integers a, b ∈ Zm and set the target a + b. Then we
pad the list with n − 2 other uniformly random integers from Zm .
For no instances, we first pick a target t ∈ Zm . Since m = 2n + 1, there are n pairs of distinct integers (i, t − i) mod m
whose sum is t. The final integer not belonging to these n pairs is 2−1 t mod m, the integer that would form a match with
itself, since 2−1 t + 2−1 t ≡m 2(2−1 t) ≡m t. (Since we ensure m is odd, 2 and m are relatively prime and the multiplicative
inverse of 2 exists.) To generate the instance, we therefore pick a random member of each pair, giving a list of n integers no
two of which sum to t.
E.1.3. R EACHABILITY
First, we sample a uniformly random pair of distinct nodes s, t ∈ {0, . . . , n − 1}.
For yes instances, we pick ⌊n/2⌋ uniformly random nodes u1 , . . . , u⌊n/2⌋ ∈ {0, . . . , n − 1} \ {s, t}. We then add the
edges (s, u1 ), (u1 , u2 ), . . . (u⌊n/2⌋−1 , u⌊n/2⌋ ), (u⌊n/2⌋ , t). Finally, we sample pairs of distinct nodes uniformly without
replacement and add a directed edge between them, until the total number of edges in the graph is min(3n, n(n − 1)/2).
This produces a graph with at most 3n edges (ensuring the input length is linear in n) that has a path from s to t. The path
likely ends up being shorter than ⌊n/2⌋ as the padding edges can introduce shortcuts.
For no instances, we randomly partition the nodes into two sets S and T with |S| = ⌊n/2⌋ where s ∈ S and t ∈ T . We then
add |E| = min(3n, |S|(|S| − 1) + |T |(|T | − 1)) uniformly random directed edges inside S and T (⌊|E|/2⌋ in S and the
rest in T ).
