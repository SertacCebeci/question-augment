A BSTRACT
Anomaly detection aims to identify observations that deviate from normal patterns.
Recent work has increasingly used pretrained vision–language models (VLMs) for
training-free image and video anomaly detection without task-specific retraining.
Anomaly detection is commonly evaluated by how well anomaly scores rank
anomalous images or video frames above normal ones. Generative VLMs, however,
assign probabilities to possible answers and then decode a single answer. This
decoding step may discard ordering information. We call this loss decoded-answer
rank compression. To isolate this effect, we compare two ways of scoring the
same VLM output: one uses only the decoded answer, while the other computes a
probability-weighted score over all possible answers. Probability-weighted scoring
consistently outperforms decoded-answer scoring across image and video anomaly
detection benchmarks, using different VLMs and answer scales. The mean gains
range from 7.66 to 19.95 points on the primary metrics. Most of this gap comes
from decoded-answer ties. Breaking decoded-answer ties with answer probabilities
recovers at least 95% of the average performance gap on every benchmark. We
further investigate how these ties affect evaluation. We find that evaluating tied
scores one input at a time makes the reported AUROC depend on input order,
and that linear PR interpolation can inflate reported performance. These results
reveal that both how VLM outputs are scored and how discrete anomaly scores are
evaluated affect reported anomaly-detection performance.

1

I NTRODUCTION

In computer vision, anomaly detection (AD) aims to identify deviations from normal patterns, such
as defects in images or unusual events in videos. Most image and video anomaly detectors learn a
task-specific scoring function from normal examples or labeled anomalies (Luo et al., 2025; Song &
Lee, 2026a). Adapting these detectors to new environments or anomaly definitions typically requires
additional training. To avoid task-specific retraining, recent work uses pretrained vision–language
models (VLMs) for training-free AD (Xu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Zanella et al., 2024).
These methods are attractive because they can make anomaly judgments and provide natural language
explanations without updating model parameters (Yang et al., 2024; Song et al., 2026).
Image and video AD are commonly evaluated by how well anomaly scores rank anomalous images
or video frames above normal ones, using metrics such as the area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve (AUROC) and average precision (AP). Generative VLMs, however, assign
probabilities to possible answers and then decode a single answer, such as a Yes/No judgment or a
numerical anomaly rating (Zhang et al., 2024; Höfer et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025; Ahn et al., 2026).
But if only the decoded answer is used as the anomaly score, inputs with different answer probabilities
can receive the same score, discarding their relative ordering.
Consider the simplest case with two possible answers, Yes and No. Suppose that “Yes” indicates an
anomaly and that the VLM assigns “Yes” probabilities of 0.56 and 0.94 to two inputs. Both inputs
decode to “Yes”, even though the answer probabilities rank the second input above the first. We call
1

this many-to-one loss of ordering decoded-answer rank compression. We ask how much useful
anomaly-ranking information is lost by decoded-answer scoring and where this loss comes from.

Contributions. To answer these questions, we compare two scoring rules applied to the same VLM
output. Decoded-answer scoring assigns a score using only the decoded answer. Probability-weighted
scoring instead averages the answer values using their probabilities. We keep the VLM, visual input,
prompt, and answer probabilities fixed, changing only how the same output distribution is converted
into an anomaly score.
We evaluate decoded-answer and probability-weighted scoring across image and video AD benchmarks, using different VLMs and answer scales. Probability-weighted scoring consistently outperforms decoded-answer scoring, with mean gains across VLMs and answer scales of 19.95 image-level
AUROC points on VisA and 10.97 frame-level AP points on the video benchmark XD-Violence.
The scoring-rule gap persists with larger VLMs, alternative prompts, reversed answer polarity, and
sampled decoding.
We find most of the gap comes from ties created by decoding. To test this, we preserve the order
between different decoded answers and use answer probabilities only to break ties. Across all seven
answer scales, this tie-breaking recovers at least 95% of the average primary-metric gap on every
benchmark. Finer answer scales reduce rank compression, but even on the finest 91-answer scale
(0.0–9.0), the VLMs use only a small fraction of the available score values, leaving many inputs tied.
We also find that common evaluation choices can make this ranking loss appear smaller. Evaluating
tied scores one at a time can make the reported ROC area depend on input order (Sultani et al., 2018).
Trapezoidal PR-AUC can also inflate reported performance by crediting precision values above those
attainable by thresholding the anomaly scores (Wu et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2022). We therefore
evaluate tied scores together and distinguish trapezoidal PR-AUC from non-interpolated AP, which
averages precision attained at the score’s thresholds (Fawcett, 2006; Davis & Goadrich, 2006).
These results have two implications for VLM anomaly detection. First, the scoring rule is part of
detector design: when answer probabilities are available, probability-weighted scoring retains useful
ordering and improves anomaly ranking without changing the VLM or prompt. Second, how these
scores are evaluated also matters: tie handling and PR-area calculation can materially affect reported
anomaly-detection performance.

R ELATED W ORK

Vision–language models for visual anomaly detection. Recent work uses pretrained vision–
language models for visual anomaly detection in both images and videos, reducing the need for
task-specific training. CLIP-based methods typically derive anomaly scores from the similarity
between visual features and text descriptions of normality and abnormality (Jeong et al., 2023; Zhou
et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024). More recent methods use generative VLMs to make
anomaly judgments or provide explanations (Gu et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a).
Several image and video anomaly-detection methods convert decoded binary judgments or numerical
ratings into anomaly scores (Zhang et al., 2024; Höfer et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024; Zanella et al.,
2024; Ye et al., 2025; Ahn et al., 2026; Shao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Song & Lee, 2026b). Our
work studies this scoring step directly: by holding the VLM answer distribution fixed, we measure
how much anomaly-ranking information is lost when that distribution is reduced to a decoded answer.

Probability-based scoring with language models. Language-model probabilities have been used
to construct scalar scores for document ranking, text evaluation, and answer verification (Nogueira
et al., 2020; Zhong et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Zhuang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b). Our
question is different: how does converting a VLM answer distribution into an anomaly score affect
anomaly ranking? We use probability-weighted scoring as a comparison to measure this effect,
analyze where the ranking differences arise, and study how they affect anomaly-detection evaluation.

5.2

R ANK C OMPRESSION

Where does the improvement come from? Compared with D EC, P ROB can change the ordering of
examples with the same decoded answer or of examples with different decoded answers.
Breaking decoded-answer ties recovers nearly all of the gap. To isolate the first effect, we
preserve D EC’s ordering between different decoded answers. For examples with the same decoded
answer, we break ties by sorting them in descending order of their P ROB scores. Under Yes/No, all
examples decoded as “Yes” remain above those decoded as “No”, while examples within each group
are ordered by P ROB.
Figure 1 shows how much of the full P ROB-D EC performance gap is recovered by breaking decodedanswer ties. For the two binary answer scales, tie-breaking reproduces the P ROB ranking by construction. On the five finer scales, P ROB can also change the order between examples with different
decoded answers. Even so, tie-breaking recovers at least 90.7% of the average primary-metric
gap across these scales on every benchmark. Across all seven scales, it recovers at least 95.2% of
the average gap on every benchmark. Thus, decoded-answer ties explain nearly all of the average
performance gap. Full results are reported in Section C.1.
Answer probabilities preserve useful order within decoded-answer ties. To test whether P ROB
preserves useful information within a decoded-answer tie, we focus on examples decoded as “No”
under Yes/No. D EC assigns all of these examples the same score. We sort them by P ROB and divide
them into ten equally sized groups (deciles). Figure 2 shows that higher-P ROB groups contain a larger
fraction of anomalies on all four benchmarks.
To quantify this ordering, we compute AUROC within the “No” group for each VLM. On every
benchmark, all VLMs achieve above-chance, with values ranging from 71.3 to 89.4 (Table 14). Thus,
answer probabilities preserve useful ordering information even among examples decoded as “No”.

5.3

A NSWER S CALES

If decoded-answer ties account for most of the performance gap, a natural way to reduce rank
compression is to use a finer answer scale. We therefore compare seven answer scales ranging from 2
to 91 possible answers. We ask whether finer scales improve D EC and narrow the P ROB–D EC gap,
and whether the VLMs actually use the additional answer choices.
Finer answer scales help, but most additional answers are not used. Averaged over the four
VLMs, all five nonbinary scales improve D EC over Yes/No and narrow the P ROB–D EC gap on every
benchmark’s primary metric (Table 2). Figure 3 illustrates this behavior on UCF-Crime and shows
how the number of distinct scores changes across answer scales. The improvement in D EC is not
monotonic in the number of possible answers. To understand why, we examine how many distinct
D EC scores the models actually produce. Under the 6- and 10-answer integer scales, D EC uses all
or nearly all possible answers. The decimal scales allow 11, 51, and 91 possible answers, but D EC
produces only 7.5, 8.8, and 13.5 distinct scores on average, respectively. In contrast, P ROB produces
69,538–69,542 distinct scores for the 69,634 UCF-Crime segments under the same decimal scales.
The other three benchmarks show the same pattern (Section C.4). Thus, increasing the number of
possible answers does not translate into a comparable increase in the number of distinct D EC scores.

5.4

A LTERNATIVE S CORING S TRATEGIES

Can the remaining P ROB–D EC gap be removed by changing other choices in how the VLM output is
converted into an anomaly score? We test alternative answer selection and aggregation rules, temporal
smoothing, and prompt and output variations.
Alternative answer selection does not close the gap. We first ask whether the gap is specific
to selecting the most probable answer. Using the saved Yes/No answer distributions, we simulate
temperature sampling, nucleus sampling, and majority voting on both video benchmarks. Each
strategy still reduces the model output to a single binary score. Averaged over the four VLMs, none
outperforms D EC on either benchmark, and all remain below P ROB. For example, the best tested
strategy on UCF-Crime reaches 72.13 AUROC, compared with 72.20 for D EC and 85.32 for P ROB.
We then average the sampled answer values instead of selecting a single answer. For Yes/No,
the fraction of “Yes” answers among k samples estimates the probability of “Yes”, which P ROB
uses directly. Figure 4 shows that performance improves with the number of samples on all four
benchmarks. On MVTec AD, mean I-AUROC increases from 81.18 with 16 samples to 86.68 with
256 samples, compared with 88.94 for P ROB. Even with 256 samples, mean performance remains
below P ROB on every benchmark (Section D.1). Thus, changing which single answer is selected
does not recover the lost ordering, while averaging multiple answers progressively approaches
probability-weighted scoring.
Temporal smoothing narrows but does not close the mean gap. Temporal smoothing can reduce
decoded-score ties by averaging scores from neighboring video segments. We apply the same
Gaussian smoothing to D EC and P ROB within each video, using σ ∈ {1, . . . , 9}, and average results
over the four VLMs and seven answer scales. As smoothing increases, D EC produces more distinct
scores and the mean P ROB–D EC gap narrows. At σ = 9, the mean number of distinct D EC scores
on UCF-Crime increases from 7.0 to about 31,745, while the mean AUROC gap falls from 7.66 to
2.28 points. On XD-Violence, the mean AP gap falls from 10.97 to 3.01 points. P ROB nevertheless
remains higher on average at every tested value of σ on both benchmarks (Section D.2).
Prompt and output variations do not reverse the comparison. We finally test whether the P ROB–
D EC comparison depends on the base prompt or output format. Across three Yes/No paraphrases

Decoded-answer scoring often assigns the same anomaly score to many examples. This raises two
evaluation choices: how equal scores are handled and how area is computed from precision–recall
points. We examine these two choices.
Evaluate equal scores together. When multiple frames receive the same anomaly score, the
detector provides no ordering among them. As a score threshold passes that value, all tied frames
should be included together. The UCF-Crime evaluation instead processes tied frames one at a time
and updates the ROC curve after each frame (Section E.1). This treats tied frames as if they had an
order, even though their scores do not distinguish them. It also creates intermediate ROC points that
do not correspond to any score threshold. Because these points depend on the order within each tie,
the resulting ROC area can also depend on that order (Fawcett, 2006).
Let τ denote the fraction of anomalous–normal pairs that receive the same score. Let AUROC−
and AUROC+ denote the smallest and largest item-wise ROC areas obtainable by ordering frames
within ties. Relative to the half-credit AUROC in Equation (5), these extrema are

D ISCUSSION AND L IMITATIONS

Decoded-answer performance can understate useful anomaly information. An anomaly score
derived from a decoded answer does not necessarily reflect all of the useful ordering information in
the VLM output. Our results show that examples receiving the same decoded answer can still differ
systematically in their answer probabilities. Poor performance with decoded-answer scoring should
therefore not, by itself, be interpreted as evidence that the VLM provides little useful information for
anomaly detection.
Tied scores make evaluation choices consequential. When examples receive the same anomaly
score, different evaluation conventions can assign different credit without any change in the scores.
Processing tied scores one at a time introduces an ordering that the scores do not specify and
can change AUROC. Linear interpolation in precision–recall space can likewise add area between
threshold points and, in our experiments, even reverse method comparisons. These differences
arise from the evaluation procedure rather than from changes in the anomaly scores themselves.
Evaluation results should therefore state how ties are handled and distinguish non-interpolated AP
from trapezoidal PR-AUC.
Access to answer probabilities. Probability-weighted scoring requires the probabilities assigned to
the possible answers. Our experiments use open VLMs for which we can compute these probabilities.
We do not evaluate proprietary model APIs, where the available model outputs may differ. Our
sampled-answer experiments draw from the answer distributions computed for the open VLMs and
therefore do not evaluate repeated sampling through such APIs.
Comparisons between answer scales. Within an answer scale, D EC and P ROB operate on the
same answer distribution and differ only in how it is converted into an anomaly score. Across answer
scales, however, the prompt, answer format, and meaning of numerical answers can also change. Our
comparisons across scales therefore measure the combined effect of these changes, not the effect of
the number of possible answers alone.

8

C ONCLUSION

We show that decoded-answer rank compression can substantially degrade anomaly ranking. Across
the image and video benchmarks we evaluate, decoded-answer scoring consistently yields lower
AUROC and AP than probability-weighted scoring. Using answer probabilities only to break decodedanswer ties recovers at least 95% of the average primary-metric gap on every benchmark. We also
show that trapezoidal PR-AUC can mask this ranking loss and even reverse the comparison between
D EC and P ROB without changing the anomaly scores. Evaluations should group equal scores and
distinguish non-interpolated AP from trapezoidal PR-AUC.
These results show that converting a VLM output into an anomaly score is not a neutral postprocessing step. This conversion can discard useful ordering information, and evaluation choices can
further change how that loss appears in reported performance. Both score construction and evaluation
are therefore important when interpreting the performance of VLM-based anomaly detectors.

C.1

T IE - BREAKING RECOVERY

We rank by H = (sDEC , sPROB ) lexicographically in descending order. Thus P ROB orders inputs
only within a D EC tie. For metric M , define
∆ = M (P ROB) − M (D EC),

T = M (H) − M (D EC),

R = M (P ROB) − M (H).

Then ∆ = T + R. For a set of configurations S, recovery is 100 T S /∆S . A value above 100% means
that preserving the order between decoded levels outperforms the full P ROB ranking. For binary
scales, tie-breaking reconstructs the P ROB ranking by construction. The nonbinary rows measure the
empirical contribution of within-answer ordering.
Recovery varies across configurations. At the finest video scale, the four-model values are 73.26% on
UCF-Crime and 75.46% on XD-Violence. The lowest model-level value in Table 13 is 37.90% for
Qwen2.5-VL-7B on XD-Violence at this scale.

S AMPLED ANSWERS

Single-answer video simulations use temperatures 0.6 and 1.0, nucleus top-p values 0.8 and 0.95

49.09 XD-Violence AP, below the corresponding P ROB values of 85.32 and 68.98.
For sample-mean scoring, we use T = 1 and k ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256} and score each
input by its fraction of Yes answers. Metrics are computed for each model–seed run before averaging
over three seeds and four VLMs. Image seeds are 20260903, 20260904, and 20260905. Models share
uniform random streams within each dataset and seed, and smaller budgets reuse prefixes of 256
draws. Video seeds are 0, 1, and 2, with binomial draws for each segment and sample count before
frame expansion. Table 17 reports the image results, and Figure 4 shows all four benchmarks.
