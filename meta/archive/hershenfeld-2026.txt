42361274 TO THE EDITOR: The American Society of Hematology (ASH) has developed &gt;20 clinical
practice guidelines (CPGs) using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and
Evaluation (GRADE) methodologic framework. 1 A core principle of GRADE is that the strength of
recommendation (SOR; either conditional or strong) should be concordant with the certainty of
evidence (COE) that supports the recommendation (rated as very low, low, moderate, or high).
Generally, strong recommendations for an intervention require a foundation of high- or
moderate-quality evidence that the benefits outweigh harms. However, GRADE has defined 5
paradigmatic situations in which discordant strong recommendations can permissibly arise from low or
very low COE, including situations in which there is low certainty of benefit but either
life-threatening consequences or high certainty of resultant harm or high costs. 2 We sought to
document the COE and SORs issued by authors of ASH CPGs and assess the frequency and justifications
for discordant recommendations. Recommendation statements were extracted (in duplicate by S.A.H. and
M.C.C.) from the most up-to-date versions of ASH CPGs, along with GRADE COE and SOR ratings.
Discordance was defined as a strong recommendation with underlying low or very low COE. When
described within the guideline, the justification for the discordant recommendation was documented
and categorized as either a paradigmatic justification or an alternative (nonparadigmatic)
justification. Descriptive statistics were used to report the frequency of COE and SOR, including
discordance. We performed exploratory analyses using Spearman rank-order correlation to evaluate the
correlation between COE and SOR pairings and Fisher exact test to compare the use of discordant
recommendations within malignant and classic hematology guidelines. We used Fisher exact test to
compare the proportion of discordant recommendations with an explicit paradigmatic justification by
early (2018-2021) vs later year (2023-2026) of publication. All analyses were performed in R
software (version 4.3.1). From 2018 to February 2026, ASH published 449 GRADE recommendations across
22 CPGs on 19 classical hematology and 3 malignant hematology topics. COE for these recommendations
was rated as very low (n = 288 [64%]), low (n = 89 [20%]), moderate (n = 64 [14%]), or high (n = 8
[2%]). For the majority of recommendations, SOR was conditional (n = 372 [83%]); SOR was strong for
77 (17%). There was a moderate positive correlation between COE and SOR (ρ = 0.48; P &lt; .0001).
However, among the strong recommendations, 33 (43%) were discordant, that is, supported by low or
very low COE ( Figure 1 ). Figure 1. Open in a new tab Sankey diagram. COE (left) and SORs (right).
Strong recommendations within the 3 malignant hematology guidelines were more likely to be
discordant (10/12 strong recommendations) than those within the classical hematology guidelines
(23/65 strong recommendations; P = .0002). Of the 33 discordant ASH recommendations, a GRADE
paradigmatic justification was documented for 25 (76%). The paradigmatic justifications cited were
as follows: (1) when low-quality evidence suggests benefit, whereas high-quality evidence suggests
harm (n = 12), high costs (n = 2), or both harm and high costs (n = 2); and (2) when low-quality
evidence suggests benefit in a life-threatening situation (n = 9). Alternative (nonparadigmatic)
justifications were offered with 4 discordant recommendations, including (1) ensuring access to
care, 3 (2) large effect sizes, 4 (3) reduced mortality, 5 and (4) reduced outcomes of interest 6
(see Table 1 for examples of paradigmatic and nonparadigmatic justifications). There were 4
discordant recommendations for which no justifications were offered. Discordant recommendations
published within more recent guidelines (2023-2026) were more likely to have an explicit
paradigmatic justification than those from earlier guidelines (2018-2021; 100% vs 62%, respectively;

Federation of Hemophilia. Overall, we found a substantial proportion of strong recommendations (43%)
issued by ASH CPGs to have underlying evidence of low or very low certainty. For most of these
discordant recommendations (76%), the guideline authors documented paradigmatic justifications
supported by GRADE. The proportion of discordant recommendations in ASH CPGs were comparable to
those found in other societies (range in the literature, 12%-64%), with a similar correlation
between COE and SOR (ρ range, 0.33-0.66). 11 , 12 However, ASH CPGs had a stronger record of
documenting justification for discordant recommendations within the guideline text; in reports from
other societies, the frequency of justifications for discordant recommendations ranged from 16% to
68%, and often, paradigmatic justifications were not explicitly cited within the guidelines
themselves. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Still, there were circumstances in which discordant ASH
recommendations were either not explicitly justified or in which nonparadigmatic alternative
justifications were proffered. We also found that the proportion of discordant recommendations with
an explicitly cited paradigmatic justification was higher in more recently published guidelines than
in earlier guidelines, potentially reflecting increased adherence to GRADE methodology over time.
There may be explanations as to why discordant recommendations are applied within hematology
guidelines. The majority of the evidence base (&gt;80%) across all ASH guidelines was of low/very
low certainty, with the evidence often downgraded by the GRADE framework for indirectness and
imprecision. This may be particularly prevalent with ASH guidelines that focus on targeted
subpopulations in which guidance is warranted, but high-quality evidence is lacking (eg, acute
lymphoblastic leukemia in the adolescent and young adult population, 7 acute myeloid leukemia in the
older population, 16 and venous thromboembolism in pregnancy 6 ). Furthermore, we noted that
malignant hematology recommendations were more likely to offer strong discordant recommendations,
potentially related to ASH’s focus on high-acuity diseases (acute lymphoblastic leukemia and acute
myeloid leukemia) that naturally allow for paradigmatic justification, given their life-threatening
nature and use of therapies with high risk of toxicity. Finally, a number of discordant
recommendations were issued due to higher costs of care or access to care, possibly reflecting
concerns of increasing health care costs in hematology, for example, to access novel targeted
therapies and genomic technologies that increasingly form the basis of hematology care. Discordant
strong recommendations should be used sparingly 2 but are justified in certain situations that are
not uncommon in hematologic practice. Given ASH’s commitment to transparent and methodologically
rigorous guideline development, we recommend such recommendations be used judiciously and
accompanied by an explicit documentation of their underlying justification. Conflict-of-interest
