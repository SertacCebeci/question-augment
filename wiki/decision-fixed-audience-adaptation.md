# Decision-fixed audience adaptation

An answer that goes to two audiences can be split into two objects: what is decided, and how that decision is worded for the reader. The design in one paper keeps the two in separate parameters. In LEXMA, a correctness adapter (ACC) carries the decision boundary and the risk-focused content and is shared by both audiences, while a tone adapter (TONE) is switched on only for consumer-facing explanations.[^c1] Expert explanations use the correctness adapter alone; consumer explanations are "generated by activating ACC and TONE together", so style and level of detail change while the decision rule stays put.[^c2]

The separation is enforced during training as well as at inference. The tone stage trains only the tone adapter while the correctness adapter stays frozen, and the authors say the tone update is meant to improve readability and politeness "without changing the decision distribution."[^c3] The decision stage alternates expert and consumer prompts, which the authors use to make decisions "consistent across audiences," reducing sensitivity to how the question is framed.[^c4]

The measured outcome is an agreement in aggregate. The fine-tuned model reached F1 0.897 under the expert prompt and 0.893 under the consumer prompt.[^c5] The check that tone tuning does not cost decisions is the same kind of measurement, reported for the tone checkpoint: "tone tuning preserves decision correctness."[^c6]

What transfers beyond a credit setting is the structure. One parameter set answers "what is decided," another answers "how it is said," and the second is trained against signals that do not touch the first. The authors state the principle as "separating decision correctness from communication style through modular adapters, so that consumer-facing tone can be improved without shifting the underlying decision rule."[^c7] A reader who wants the same property in a different domain needs a decision that can be fixed, a wording that can be varied, and a way to train the wording without re-deciding. Whether the second requirement holds elsewhere is not shown by this paper.

This note is distinct from [[audience-epistemic-mismatch-backfire]]. There, the same true reply has different effects on different audiences. Here the decision is held at one value while the wording varies. Holding the decision constant says nothing about whether the audiences respond equally to the wording, which is the question the backfire note raises.

## Limits

- One domain and one model. The setting is mortgage approval on public HMDA data, and the base model is a single small LLM: "We fine-tune the Qwen3-4B model".[^c8]
- Two audiences only, expert and consumer. The design is not tested for more audiences, or for audiences whose needs pull against the decision.
- No ablation isolates the adapters. The text I read does not compare the two-adapter design with one adapter trained with the same tone reward. So whether the separation itself, or the sequencing of training, keeps decisions stable is not shown (my reading).
- The invariance is measured in aggregate, as F1 and accuracy on one held-out set. Case-level flips between the expert and consumer answers are not reported (my reading of the tables).
- The authors' human ratings (risk relevance, clarity, trust) are their own measures. They do not test whether a consumer understands the decision better, and the wiki's own reading should not treat "preferred" as "understood."
