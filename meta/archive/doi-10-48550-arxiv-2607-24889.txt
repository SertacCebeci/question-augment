A BSTRACT
Language model agents can now construct complete financial models, but it remains
unclear whether they have learned the financial judgment that gives those models
meaning. Existing benchmarks often anchor correctness to an expert-authored
solution (e.g., numerical targets or detailed rubrics) which introduces a implicit
assumption: one expert solution can serve as ground truth. This is appropriate when
finance provides a unique answer, but not when judgment is required. Evidence
from professional practice challenges this assumption: When financial models
built by different analysts for the same company are graded against one another,
the median score is only 0.33 under standard tolerances, revealing that singlereference grading confounds professional disagreement with error. We therefore
introduce GAUGE, a benchmark that decomposes financial-model evaluation into
deterministic checks, rubric judgments and numerical rules. Built from 1,001
professional valuation spanning 922 companies and all 25 GICS industry groups,
GAUGE checks mechanical properties deterministically and evaluates judgmentbearing quantities against ranges in professional practice. We then validate GAUGE
as a measurement instrument by testing expertise ordering, held-out professional
values, and robustness to LM judgements. Our findings show that current LM
agents are far better at constructing financial models than at deriving the companyspecific assumptions that drive valuation, a gap that even persists after fine-tuning.

I NTRODUCTION

A financial model is a core tool for company valuation. It reconstructs a company’s three financial
statements from reported history, projects them several years forward under explicit assumptions,
and converts those projections into a value per share, most commonly through discounted-cash-flow
analysis (DCF, i.e., forecasting the company’s future cash flows and discounting them at a rate
that reflects their risk). Building such models requires substantial expert time and effort: recent
benchmarks report 8–10 hours for a DCF model and 13–16 hours for a three-statement model
completed by human experts (Krumdick et al., 2026). Analysts in equity research and investment
banking build these models routinely (Demirakos et al., 2004; Imam et al., 2008). LM agents can now
build them end to end, raising the prospect of automating a substantial part of this labor-intensive
workflow (Lau et al., 2026; Krumdick et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Kundurthy et al., 2026).
This progress makes reliable evaluation increasingly important. Producing a complete workbook is
not enough. Some parts of a financial model are mechanically verifiable, i.e., historical figures must
match the source data, the balance sheet must balance, and forecast cells must contain live formulas
rather than pasted constants. Other parts depend on financial judgment. Revenue growth, operating
margins, discount rates, and target prices can reasonably differ across competent analysts, and prior
1

work has long documented such disagreement in forecasts and valuation practice (Diether et al., 2002;
Imam et al., 2008). For these judgment-bearing quantities, disagreement with one analyst is not
necessarily an error. This leads to a key question: Can we evaluate agent-built financial models by
checking what has a clear answer while allowing for reasonable differences in financial judgment?
Existing financial-agent benchmarks, however, typically use an expert-authored reference to grade
outputs that depend on financial judgment (Lau et al., 2026; Krumdick et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026;
Kundurthy et al., 2026). Such grading makes an implicit assumption: when an agent differs from
the reference analyst, the agent is wrong. Yet professional valuation gives us little reason to make
this assumption. We test it directly by grading independently built professional models against one
another. Across 108 same-company pairs from 65 companies, the median score is only 0.33, and
92.6% of pairs fall below 0.70 under standard tolerances. Even at four times these tolerances, a third
remain below 0.70. Among models built from the same fiscal-year data, no pair agrees on the implied
share price within 10% (Section 2). In other words, a grading rule that treats one analyst as ground
truth rejects much of the real, meaningful variation that already exists among professionals.
This finding suggests a simple principle: use exact answers where finance provides them, and
professional practice where judgment can reasonably differ. We build GAUGE on this principle
which draws on 1,001 valuation workbooks created by practicing analysts for real commercial
engagements, spanning 922 companies and all 25 GICS industry groups. To our best knowledge, this
is the largest professional valuation corpus used in a financial-agent benchmark. From it, we construct
196 full-model tasks that ask agents to build a complete model from three years of historical financials.
Scoring follows the same principle. Mechanical properties are checked directly with deterministic
code, while major structural errors trigger hard gates. Quantities that depend on financial judgment
are compared with an envelope, i.e., ranges observed in professional practice. Qualitative aspects are
scored by a frozen LLM judge under explicit rubrics (Zheng et al., 2023; Guerdan et al., 2025).
We evaluate GAUGE on 55 human participants under the same task setup and scoring rules used for
agents. Senior analysts junior analysts, and finance students obtain mean scores of 88.3, 66.0, and
43.2, respectively. This ordering remains unchanged across our robustness checks (Section 4). This
wide spread provides a human scale from students to experienced professionals for interpreting agent
performance. The practice envelopes include held-out professional values while rejecting deliberately
shifted ones, and replacing Claude Opus 4.8 with GPT-5.6-sol as the judge produces nearly the same
agent ranking. Against this human baseline, we evaluate 24 leading proprietary and open-weight
LM agents (Section 5). Every agent performs better on mechanical construction than on financial
judgment, with a median gap of 26 points. Claude Fable 5 leads the panel with a score of 53.4, above
the finance-student mean of 43.2 but below all 12 senior analysts and 15 of the 18 junior analysts.
More interestingly, senior analysts show only a 3.6-point gap on the same facets, suggesting that
current agents have learned to build financial models much faster than they have learned to exercise
judgment through them. Even trajectory fine-tuning on rollouts generated from the GAUGE training
split improves valuation performance without closing this gap, suggesting that benchmark-aligned
supervision can teach agents where plausible values lie more readily than the judgment behind them.
In summary, we make three contributions. (1) We show that single-reference grading breaks down for
financial judgment: when independently built professional models of the same company are graded
against one another, most fail under standard benchmark tolerances, with disagreement concentrated
on the quantities where judgment matters. (2) We introduce GAUGE, a benchmark of 196 full-model
tasks built from 1,001 licensed professional workbooks spanning 922 companies and all 25 GICS
industry groups. GAUGE checks mechanical correctness directly and evaluates judgment against
professional practice, with a 55-participant human baseline and audits on held-out professional values
and an independent judge. (3) GAUGE reveals a gap across 24 LM agents between building financial
models and making the judgments that drive them. Every agent is stronger at mechanical construction
than financial judgment, with the best agent still below every senior analyst. Further analysis localizes
this deficit to company-specific derivation rather than broad risk ranking, and trajectory fine-tuning
on GAUGE rollouts improves valuation performance without fully closing the underlying gap.

2.1

R EVISITING THE S INGLE -R EFERENCE A SSUMPTION

Existing financial-agent benchmarks commonly evaluate outputs against an expert-authored reference.
For quantities with a uniquely determined answer, this is natural. For quantities that depend on
financial judgment, however, the same design embeds a stronger assumption: disagreement with
the reference analyst can be treated as error. We test this assumption directly on professionals
themselves. Concretely, if one professional analyst’s model were graded using another analyst’s
independently built model of the same company as the answer key, how well would it score?
To answer this, we use 137 workbooks from Section 3.1 covering 65 companies, with two or three
independently built models each. For each ordered same-company pair, one workbook serves as
the reference and the other is graded against it. We compare nine quantities: revenue forecasts for
the next two fiscal years, implied share price, and six discount-rate quantities, namely WACC, the
risk-free rate, equity risk premium, beta, tax rate, and terminal growth. Base tolerances are ±5%
for revenue, ±50 bp for WACC, and ±10% for implied share price; the rest use criterion-specific
tolerances. A pair’s score is the fraction of criteria within tolerance. Of 158 directed pairs, 108 report
at least three criteria and enter the analysis; we repeat it with tolerances widened to 2×, 3×, and 4×.
Professionals score poorly when graded against one another. At standard tolerances, the median
score is only 0.33, and 92.6% of pairs score below 0.70 (Figure 2). The median rises to 0.50,
0.67, and 0.80 as tolerances widen to 2×, 3×, and 4×, respectively, yet even at 4× one third of
professional pairs remain below 0.70. The pattern persists among same-vintage models: across the
14 price pairs built from the same fiscal-year data, none agrees on implied share price within 10%,
and restricting the audit to same-vintage pairs leaves the median score at 0.33. The differences are
economically substantial as well: the median same-company WACC difference is 147 bp and the
median implied-price difference is 25%, with 90th percentiles of 374 bp and 107% (Appendix A).
These results expose a basic limitation of single-reference grading: any difference from one analyst’s
estimate is counted as an error, even when professional practice itself contains a range of defensible
values. An expert-authored estimate can therefore serve as a reference without serving as ground
truth. More importantly, Figure 2 shows that disagreement is not evenly distributed across quantities;
it is concentrated precisely where professional judgment matters most, which we examine next.

2.3

T HE G RADER S HOULD F OLLOW W HAT I S A NSWERABLE

The problem is not simply that the tolerances are too narrow. Even after every tolerance is widened to
4× its standard value, one third of professional pairs still score below 0.70. Widening them further also
weakens the test itself: for example, a ±40% implied-price band would admit substantially different
valuations while providing little discrimination. The more useful distinction is between quantities
that have a uniquely verifiable answer and those that admit professional judgment. Historical figures,
accounting identities, and formula relationships can be checked directly. Forecast assumptions and
valuation outputs instead require a reference that reflects the range of values observed in professional
practice. This leads to the principle behind GAUGE: verify what is mechanically answerable, and
evaluate judgment against professional practice. Section 3 turns it into benchmark’s scoring design.

GAUGE

3.1

C ORPUS : P ROFESSIONAL P RACTICE AS THE B ENCHMARK F OUNDATION

GAUGE uses Finance Artifacts both to construct evaluation tasks and to characterize professional
practice for judgment-dependent scoring. The corpus contains 1,001 valuation workbooks created by
practicing financial analysts for commercial deal and coverage work, licensed through a commercial
data provider. It spans 922 distinct tickers and all 25 GICS industry groups across four complexity
tiers, with workbooks ranging from roughly 2,000 to 35,000 spreadsheet cells and from 3 to 30
stated analyst-hours. This gives GAUGE, to our knowledge, the first large-scale basis for evaluating
from-scratch valuation models against observed professional practice rather than an expert-authored
answer key. Crucially, these workbooks also contain the range-bearing artifacts needed to define
professional-practice scoring bands: 35% use multiple valuation methods, 31% contain machinereadable sensitivity grids, and 54% contain at least one such artifact. Section 3.4 uses these signals to
construct the corresponding professional-practice bands. To keep task construction, calibration, and
future evaluation cleanly separated, we partition the corpus by company identity into 196 evaluation
tasks, a 137-workbook subset covering 65 multi-covered companies for the audit in Section 2 and
envelope calibration, a 200-company training split, and roughly 600 withheld workbooks for future
tasks. Additional corpus curation and quality-control procedures are described in Appendix C.
4

3.2

TASK : A C OMPLETE VALUATION W ORK P RODUCT

Each of the 196 tasks defines a fixed input–output contract. The input pack contains three fiscal
years of audited historical financials, segment and KPI skeletons, management guidance where
disclosed, and an as-of date that defines the information available to the agent. Consensus and broker
forecasts are withheld because they constitute the evaluation targets. For each task, the agent must
return three artifacts: an eleven-sheet, formula-driven workbook covering the three statements, a
driver-based revenue build, and a debt schedule, alongside WACC, DCF, sensitivity analysis, and selfcheck tab; a short investment memo with three to five falsifiable quantified claims; and a supporting
machine-readable assumptions file in which every key assumption has a resolvable source pointer.
To ensure that task inputs are valid and auditable, a provenance-tracked extractor records the source
sheet, row, and label for every input number and verifies the accounting identity (e.g., total assets =
total liabilities + equity) before release. Of 200 candidate tasks, 196 (98%) pass one of five archetype
schemas: 175 three-statement models, 13 income-statement/valuation or REIT/DDM models, 5 bank
payout/DDM models, and 3 pure-DCF models. The four unresolved candidates are recorded as
abstentions, and no reference value is imputed. All agents then run in the same scaffold with access
to only two tools, a Python interpreter and file writes, without network access, for at most 50 tool
turns and 1,800 seconds. Complete prompts and scaffold constants are provided in Appendix D.
3.3

S CORING : F ROM C APABILITY TAXONOMY TO A FAILURE -AWARE S CORE

Facet taxonomy and grading. GAUGE scores each run on 56 atomic and individually auditable
facets across five pillars: input comprehension, model construction, forecast reasoning, valuation,
and communication & auditability. Each facet receives one of three scores: 0 for fail, 1 for pass,
or 2 for excellent; facets whose preconditions are absent are marked N/A. Complete rubrics are
provided in Appendix G. Grading follows the type of facet being assessed: 29 deterministic facets

rule-based facets use workbook values under the rules in Section 3.4. The rule-based facets include
two envelope-band checks, one hidden-reference accuracy check, and one self-referenced consistency

performance, held-out professional data, and analyses of its scoring components.

E NVELOPE VALIDITY: HELD - OUT COVERAGE AND COUNTERFEIT REJECTION

Because dispersion estimates and band widths are calibrated on the same 65-company multi-covered
corpus that could otherwise validate them, we evaluate the envelope layers with company-grouped
cross-fitting (20 repetitions of five-fold splits keeping all of a company’s workbooks in one fold;
dispersion tails and industry bands re-estimated with each held-out company removed from its
calibration pools). In this audit, E-method is the minimum-to-maximum range of the reference
workbook’s own multi-method valuations and sensitivity grids, tested on the implied price, with a
near rule that widens the band by the held-out p90 cross-analyst dispersion. On held-out companies,
E-method covers 53.8% of peer implied prices strictly (95% CI 38.5–68.6%) and 91.2% under the
near rule (82.6–97.2%); strict held-out E-industry coverage is 82.4% for WACC, 90.2% for terminal
growth, and 75–85% for the remaining assumption facets (Appendix N).
Coverage alone cannot distinguish a valid band from a wide one, so we add a counterfeit-rejection
control: displace every held-out professional value by ±k times its facet’s typical same-company
professional disagreement (k ∈ {1, 2, 3}; e.g., 147 bp for WACC) and re-test membership under the
unchanged rule. The E-industry layer admits 80.8% of real held-out values while rejecting 66.1%
of ±2-unit and 85.4% of ±3-unit counterfeits, monotonically in k for every facet. A p75-to-p90
percentile sweep traces the frontier between coverage and selectivity; the released p90 setting is an
interior point on it. One component still earns only partial trust: the near-price band is asymmetric
and rejects only upward counterfeits, so membership in it earns at most partial credit, while the strict
price band that alone earns full credit rejects 65.4% of the same counterfeits (Appendix N).

5.3

S CORE COMPOSITION AND RANKING STABILITY

Sources of score loss. Facet shortfalls dominate losses for 21 of 24 agents, while gate deductions are
at most 4.2 points (Figure 4). The free cash flow definition passes in 98% of applicable cases versus
2% for separating maintenance from growth capital expenditure and 9% for sensitivity analysis on
operating drivers. This suggests difficulty adapting standard calculations to a company’s operations.
WACC estimates. Among 532 completed runs reporting WACC, 40% of estimates are multiples of
50 bp and 27% of 100 bp, compared with 12% and 7% in 120 workbooks. Agent–ref rank correlations
across companies (ρ = 0.36–0.42) are nevertheless close to cross-analyst agreement on the same
companies (ρ = 0.38). Similar rank agreement thus coexists with coarser numerical assumptions.
Ranking stability. Across 50,000 paired bootstraps, the leader and top-three set are preserved in
99.998% and 99.848% of replicates, respectively, while the top-five set is preserved in 22.4%. The
leading group is stable, with uncertainty around the top-five cutoff. Completion affects ranking: at
25% completion, Doubao 2.1-pro falls from rank 8 under completed-only scoring to rank 22 under
ϕ0 . The construction–judgment gap persists across corpus tiers and all 11 sectors (Appendix M).
5.4

C AN THE GAP BE TRAINED AWAY ?

GAUGE’s training split (200 companies, disjoint from evaluation and calibration) lets us test whether
the gap yields to corpus supervision. In-context, adding E-industry assumption tables (with no
figures from the test company) improves the valuation-judgment subscore by +4.0 ϕ (95% CI
[+1.3, +6.7]) while mechanical scores are unchanged; worked-example cards yield a non-significant
+1.0. Fine-tuning on 146 rejection-sampled trajectories moves valuation by +8.4 ϕ but assumptions
by −4.9, leaving the overall score unchanged (−0.8, CI [−3.6, +2.0]). In both experiments the gains
concentrate on envelope-scored facets, the exact quantities the corpus statistics describe (Appendix O):
corpus data teaches an agent where professional numbers live, not the judgment that surrounds them.

C ONCLUSION

GAUGE addresses the failure of single-reference grading through deterministic checks, validity
gates, and judgment scoring grounded in professional practice. Validation with 55 humans, held-out
professional values, and an independent judge supports this design. All 24 agents score higher on
construction than judgment, and the best remains below every senior analyst in our study. Fine-tuning
improves valuation scores without closing this gap. GAUGE thus provides a testbed for improving
agents’ ability to derive and defend company-specific valuation assumptions.

methods, key input–output relationships, and sensitivity notes. Per the vendor’s data card, every
artifact cleared a commercial-intent and internal-review bar (review hours are excluded from the

personal data.
Vendor-stated vs. QC-verified. We re-derive every count we use from the delivered bytes rather than
quoting the data card. The card states 400/350/200/50 folder units per tier; our sheet-level parse of all

methods) are quoted as definitions, but our 20-workbook deep audit found Large-tier cell medians of

share; we report both deviations rather than suppress them. Input-material diversity grows with
tier by design (Small: filings, decks, basic market data; Medium adds earnings calls; Large adds
industry reports and expert calls; Premium adds bespoke operating analyses), which makes tier a
usable mechanical-scale covariate in Appendix M.
GICS reconciliation (26 vs. 25). The vendor’s data card lists 26 industry groups over ∼1,033 ticker
entries. Two normalizations produce the numbers used in this paper: (i) the card counts the legacy

classify under the current 25-group standard; (ii) the card’s ticker list includes duplicate listings and
alternative names for the same company, which our identity pass de-duplicates to 922 distinct tickers.
All corpus statistics in this paper therefore read “922 tickers, 25 GICS industry groups.”
README caveat. README files are template-instantiated — 546 of 1,001 contain an identifiable

TASK C ONSTRUCTION AND AGENT H ARNESS

This appendix gives the system prompt (D.1), deliverable specification (D.2), rendered input-pack
excerpt (D.3), and scaffold constants (D.4).
Input packs. A provenance-tracked extractor builds each visible pack from the reference workbook’s
historical sections. Every number carries its source sheet, row, and label; the accounting identity
T A = T L + T E is checked before release. Packs contain three fiscal years of audited historicals
(revenue, COGS, opex, D&A, interest, tax, net income, the three balance-sheet totals, and capex),
plus as-of share price, share count, total debt, and cash. They contain no peer multiples, consensus,
or broker data. Five archetype schemas are listed in Section 3.2.
Prompt contents. The rendered prompt sets the role (junior equity-research associate reporting to a
senior analyst), as-of date, and pack-only data rule. It then inserts whitelisted pack tables; a byte-scan
of rendered prompts across models found no reference WACC, fair value, or EV. The final block
requires one workbook with eleven named sheets, five formula-live forecast years, tie-outs, a memo
with 3–5 falsifiable quantified claims matching the workbook, and an assumptions file of {name,
value, source} records with a resolvable Sheet!Cell or input-pack pointer.

D.4

S CAFFOLD C ONSTANTS

Every model runs the same scaffold: two JSON-schema function tools (run_bash, write_file;
no network and no market-data access, so consensus is withheld by construction), a 50-turn budget,
6,000-character tool-output truncation, a 1,800 s wall clock, and at most one infrastructure retry —
capability failures are never retried (Section 5). Only the wire adapter (how a tool call is serialized for
a given provider) differs per model: anthropic, openai, gemini, and kimi_official, plus
three recovery shims applied uniformly (JSON-string argument unwrap, tool-call-as-text extraction,
leaked think-block stripping) whose triggers are logged per run. Each run archives the rendered
prompt, the full transcript, the workbook, a LibreOffice-recalculated sidecar (formula values rederived independently of the authoring process), the memo, and the assumptions file. Table 2 lists
every constant.

E

W IRE A DAPTERS AND R ECOVERY S HIMS

Running 24 agents from twelve providers through one scaffold (Appendix D) forces a design layer
most leaderboards leave undisclosed: tool-call serialization differs per provider, serialization bugs
happen, and every such bug must be classified as either infrastructure (repaired uniformly, in the
harness) or capability (fed back to the model and scored). That classification is a validity decision
— a harness that silently repairs one provider’s malformed tool calls but not another’s is no longer
measuring the same thing — so this section expands the one-line inventory of Appendix D.4 into a
full disclosure; to our knowledge no prior agentic-finance benchmark provides one.
The four adapters. All per-provider code lives in harness/agent_loop.py as four wire
adapters behind one interface (seed, build request, parse tool calls, feed results, nudge):
• _OpenAI — /v1/chat/completions;
• _Anthropic — /v1/messages;
• _Gemini — :generateContent, thinkingBudget:0;
18

• _KimiOfficial — a subclass of _Anthropic that changes only credentials and base
URL, because Kimi k3 is served from Moonshot’s own Anthropic-Messages-compatible
coding gateway rather than the shared serving proxy.
The loop, prompts, tools, and budgets are shared; the adapter isolates the model — and, for dualprotocol models, the wire protocol — as the variable under test. Protocol support was frozen from
a live probe of the proxy (/v1/models support_apis plus trivial tool-call probes), not from
documentation; several models expose both the Anthropic-native and the OpenAI-chat protocol,
which is what makes within-model protocol comparison possible at all. In the frozen campaign each
agent is pinned to a single wire, recorded per row in the ledger: 11 agents on anthropic, 10 on
openai, 2 on gemini, 1 on kimi_official (Table 3).
Recovery shims normalize transport, never semantics. Three shims run identically on every
wire that can trigger them. (1) The shim _normalize_tool_args unwraps two non-standard
serializations of tool arguments (docstring verbatim below); it is applied on both the anthropic
and openai wires. (2) _toolcall_from_text recovers a tool call that a model emitted as
JSON text with finish_reason=stop instead of the native tool_calls field (seen on Step
3.7 Flash over the openai wire); it is deliberately conservative — per its docstring it “only accepts
JSON whose name is one of OUR tools, so legitimate prose is never mistaken for a call” — and it tags
every synthesized call with the id call_synth0, so activations remain greppable in the archived
histories. (3) _strip_think drops leaked literal <think>. . . </think> blocks from assistant
text. The dividing line we enforce: a shim may re-parse the envelope, never the content. None injects
text, repairs an argument value, or hides an error; when the model’s semantics are themselves broken
(an empty command, a truncated script), the harness feeds back an explanatory tool result and the
recovery — or the failure — is the model’s, and is scored.
Transport shim — _normalize_tool_args docstring (verbatim)
Coerce a model’s tool-call arguments into a plain
dict.
The serving proxy serializes large tool arguments
in two non-standard ways (observed on kimi-k2.6):
(1) the whole ‘input‘ arrives as a JSON-encoded
string rather than a parsed object; (2) the parsed
object has a single ‘raw_arguments‘ key whose
value is the JSON string of the real arguments.
Both must be unwrapped or downstream tools see
empty/‘missing path‘/‘empty command‘. Applied on
every wire so behavior stays uniform across
models.

The three serialization incidents. A pre-campaign smoke test on the Kimi family (2026-07-18)
surfaced three latent transport failures, all silent and none provider-documented. (i) Arguments as a
JSON string: tool_use.input arrived as a bare JSON-encoded string rather than a parsed object;
the transcript stub crashed on .items() and the driver died before writing any transcript, leaving
only an AttributeError in gen_error. (ii) Token-cap truncation loop: a single oversized
run_bash call truncated mid-argument by the 16,384-token per-turn output cap delivered an empty
command; the original placeholder feedback (<empty command>) gave the model nothing to
correct on, so it repeated the identical call for ∼16 turns until the truncated assistant turn poisoned
the history and the proxy rejected the request body outright (HTTP 400). (iii) raw_arguments
wrapper: large arguments arrived wrapped as {"raw_arguments": "<json string>"},
so write_file saw no path. Incidents (i) and (iii) are envelope corruption and were fixed
uniformly by the shim above. Incident (ii) is a genuine capability event — the model chose an
oversized single call — so it is not shimmed away: the empty command still reaches the model as a
failed tool result; we only replaced the uninformative placeholder with a diagnostic that names the
likely cause, and the model must still recover on its own turns.

Thinking overrides and retry classification. Two further per-provider accommodations are disclosed
rather than hidden. First, extended thinking is suppressed wherever the wire protocol exposes a
control — thinking:disabled on the anthropic wire, thinkingBudget:0 on gemini
— because the proxy emits empty thinking blocks on complex prompts and thinking inflates first-turn
latency past the proxy’s ∼298 s response cap. Two models reject the uniform setting at the API level:
Claude Fable 5 returns HTTP 400 for both thinking:disabled and thinking:enabled (it
uses a newer adaptive scheme, so the key is omitted and the model defaults to adaptive), and Kimi
k2.7-code requires thinking enabled, run with a 4,096-token budget. We report both deviations rather
than suppress them: the fleet is thinking-suppressed except where a provider makes suppression
impossible. Second, HTTP retry classification in _post (four attempts, exponential backoff):
408/429/5xx and network errors are transient; a proxy-wrapped HTTP 424 is retried only when the
inner upstream status is 5xx and not the deterministic ResponseTimeout (that means the turn’s
generation simply exceeded the response cap — retrying burns ∼298 s and fails identically, so we fail
fast); and an HTTP 403 permission_error whose body reads “Service temporarily unavailable,
please retry later” (observed on Claude Fable 5) is classified as upstream capacity, not authentication,
because the message itself requests the retry. Everything else raises immediately and lands in the
ledger as gen_error; capability failures are never retried (Section 5).
Post-hoc audit of the frozen campaign. Every run archives a human-readable transcript (postnormalization calls plus every tool result); runs after a late-campaign harness update additionally
archive the full wire-native message history with zero truncation — 144 final attempts, the last
three agents to run (Hunyuan hy3, Qwen3.7-max, GPT-5.6-luna; 96 of them openai-wire runs).
Scanning all 1,152 final attempts (24 agents × 48 tasks; superseded infrastructure retries excluded):
the call_synth0 marker of the text-extraction shim appears nowhere in any archived transcript or
history — and structurally it could fire only on the openai wire, where the recovery is implemented,
while Step 3.7 Flash, the model that motivated it, is pinned to anthropic in the frozen matrix. The
truncation diagnostic was fed back 150 times across 131 of the 1,152 final attempts (11.4%), spanning
11 models and concentrated where Table 3 shows — DeepSeek v4-pro hit its own output cap in 44 of
48 runs, a capability signature, not a harness artifact. Exactly one serialization variant escaped the
deliberately narrow unwrap: one DeepSeek v4-flash run (UNH) emitted write_file arguments as
{"path": ..., "raw_arguments": "<json>"} — a real key mixed with the wrapper,
so the sole-key condition did not fire, the tool wrote 0 bytes, the literal result (“wrote 0 bytes to . . . ”)
was fed back, and the run is scored as-is. We report the miss rather than widen the shim after the fact:
a normalizer edited post hoc to chase every observed malformation migrates, one exception at a time,
from transport into capability.

The scaffold exposes two tools (Appendix D.4). We analyze 1,152 archived final-attempt transcripts
containing 23,186 executed calls from 24 agents, measuring tool choice, returned errors, recovery, and

Accounting contract. Archived transcripts stub each tool result to its leading 300 characters (of
the 6,000-character tail fed back to the model), so text-detected categories — Python tracebacks,
exception names — are floors: a traceback that follows long stdout is invisible in the stub. Harnesssynthesized results (<write_file: missing path>, the truncation diagnostic, the sandbox
refusal, <unknown tool>) are short and always fully visible, so those counts are exact. The 144
runs that also archive full-fidelity wire-native histories (Appendix E) calibrate the floor: on that subset
the stub detector recovers 84.4% of true traceback events (309 of 366), and true per-call error rates
run 16.8–18.8% where the stub floors read 14.1–18.8%. Every rate below is labeled floor or exact
accordingly; none is imputed. One further convention: per-cell ϕ values quoted in this appendix are
the frozen deterministic-layer scorecards of the rescored ledger — the layer the quoted gates and
checks live in — while leaderboard aggregates additionally merge the judge layer (Section 3.3), so a
cell’s ϕ here and its leaderboard contribution can differ by a few points.

What the environment feeds back. Of 23,186 calls, 2,009 (8.7%, floor) return an error. The split
is diagnostic. Python exceptions dominate: 1,187 stub-visible tracebacks plus 163 syntax errors
(5.8% of all calls). The exact harness-synthesized classes follow: 317 write_file calls with no
path and 150 bash calls with no command — both signatures of tool-call arguments destroyed
by the per-turn output cap (Appendix E) — plus 163 sandbox refusals, 8 calls to tools that do not
exist, 15 command-not-found shell errors, 4 tool-layer faults, and 2 in-command timeouts; the classes
sum to the 2,009 exactly. Per tool, write_file fails more often than bash (12.5% vs. 7.9%): its
failure mode is not code but payload — the single-shot script whose serialized arguments outrun
the per-turn token cap. Within the tracebacks (Figure 7c), NameError (291) leads TypeError
(175) and SyntaxError (148), with KeyError fourth (95). BankerToolBench reports the reverse
ordering — KeyError/TypeError “account for nearly half” of its bash failures (Lau et al., 2026)
— and the contrast is mechanistic, not cosmetic: GAUGE inlines the entire input pack as text, so there
is no external store to mis-key; what remains is the agent’s own code shape, and NameError is the
signature of its dominant architecture — a monolithic build script accreted in chunks, where chunk n
references a name that chunk n−1 was supposed to define.
Most visible Python failures are followed by a completed artifact. 547 cells contain at least
one visible Python failure; 539 of them (98.5%) still ship a workbook. Python’s own repair
hints are taken at a measurable rate — of 49 Did you mean: suggestions, 15 (31%) are
adopted within two turns — and the openpyxl domain traps that recur across the fleet (21 of

Grader wiring discipline. The facet–grader map is, in its own words, “the honest record of how
much of the 56-facet taxonomy the current harness can actually measure.” Its wiring rule is “Samegrader-class only”: a deterministic facet may be fed only by a deterministic check, a judged facet only
by a judge, an envelope facet only by a band grader (29/23/4 of the 56 facets respectively, Section 3.3).
Where a legacy check tests the same content in the wrong class, it is deliberately left unwired and the
overlap documented, “so a same-class grader gets built rather than the grader contract being quietly
broken.” The map’s ladder policy also records the ceiling that forced the judge upgrade: under the
original binary wiring no source could award a 2, so a flawless model topped out at ϕ = 60; the
taxonomy-aware 0/1/2 ladder judge (Appendix K) exists because that documented ceiling made the

Evidence construction. The workbook dump is sheet-aware and label-dense with formulas visible,
under a 38,000-character budget allocated by sheet relevance: valuation, sensitivity, DCF, WACC,
and comps tabs carry weight 3; the three statements, assumptions, and revenue builds weight 2;
everything else weight 1, with a 500-character floor per sheet so late tabs of large models are never
truncated away (a misnamed sheet is re-classified by content before weighting). The memo and
assumptions file are appended under 8,000-character budgets each. For input-comprehension facets
(pillar P1), a 9,000-character dump of the input pack itself is prefixed, so “did you read the workbook”
is judged against what the agent was actually given. The {{INDUSTRY_EMPHASIS}} slot injects
the overlay’s sector-sharpened rubric language (Appendix I) when present, and is empty otherwise.
Vote reduction (k=5). Each facet receives k independent draws. N/A wins only as a strict majority
(#N/A · 2 > k); otherwise N/A votes are dropped and the numeric votes reduce by mode, with ties
among leading values resolved by the median rounded toward the lower rung — a split jury is not
credited the higher score. Outputs failing JSON validation are retried, never silently coerced; if every
draw fails, the facet records an explicit error and scores N/A (dropped), not zero. Per-facet vote
vectors and agreement rates are released with the harness.
Caching parity. The shared evidence block is served via prompt caching (one priming call, then
facet×vote calls fan out); caching changes cost only, not model inputs — a paired pre-registered
check confirmed score parity between the cached and uncached serving paths. The serving path
does not accept a sampling temperature for this model, so vote sampling is the only variance-control
mechanism — which is exactly what Appendix U measures.

N.2

P ERTURBATION C ONTROL : C OVERAGE VS . S ELECTIVITY

A wide-enough band admits everything; coverage alone cannot separate selectivity from permissiveness. We therefore re-test every held-out peer value after displacing it by ±k qf , k ∈ {1, 2, 3},
under the unchanged frozen coverage rule, so that real values and counterfeits face the identical
test. For assumptions, qf is the facet’s leave-one-company-out median absolute same-company peer
difference (WACC 147 bp, terminal growth 100 bp, beta 0.32, tax 385 bp, ERP 114 bp, risk-free
36 bp)—one quantum of typical professional disagreement; for price, the p90 directed cross-analyst
relative dispersion (δ=0.995), applied on the ratio scale so counterfeits stay positive. E-industry
bands are per-GICS-group and leave-one-company-out throughout. Because each counterfeit must
be paired with its real value, this experiment uses the deterministic leave-one-company-out split
rather than the 20 repeated five-fold averages above; its coverage column therefore differs from those
averages by at most 3.7 points (e.g., near price 94.9% vs. 91.2%), and the comparison of interest is
within-row, coverage against rejection under the identical rule.
Table 15 reports the result. The E-industry layer is selective: it admits 80.8% of real held-out values
while rejecting 66.1% of ±2qf and 85.4% of ±3qf counterfeits, monotone in k for every facet.
Sweeping the band percentile traces the coverage–selectivity frontier (p75: 56.3%/89.6% through
p90: 80.8%/66.1% at ±2qf ); the released p90 setting is an interior operating point on that frontier, not
its permissive end. The E-method near-price band is the honest exception: its multiplicative near rule
with δ≈1.0 drives the lower edge toward zero, so it rejects upward ±2-dispersion counterfeits at 38.5%
but downward ones at 0%. Near-price membership is therefore read only as the partial-credit zone of
Eq. (4); the strict price band, which alone earns full credit, rejects 65.4% of the same counterfeits
against 53.8% real-peer coverage. Full per-facet counts, non-positive-counterfeit accounting, and the
pooled variant are in reference/envelope_selectivity_gics.json.

On identical facet outcomes, replacing the gate caps of Eq. (3) with equal-weight additive credit

Section 5.3 splits the 141 never-completed cells into “three different engineering failures — planning
collapse, silent truncation, and structural incompleteness — that a single ‘accuracy’ number would
conflate.” Here we walk three real trajectories from the frozen w2_main campaign: the first two
instantiate the first two never-completed mechanisms; the third is the boundary case — a workbook

Trajectory 1: planning collapse — the full-budget rebuild loop (Doubao 2.1-pro × MA). All

2,652 s across its two attempts), and the MA cell shows the mechanism in miniature. The agent

Cells. Six generation cells drawn from the main run, one from each of six distinct generator models

so every k condition is analyzed from the same underlying pool rather than from separate paid runs.
Raw votes are released with the harness.

replacement from the vote pools. (i) Subscore std: a resampled judged subscore is the mean over
facets of the reduced numeric scores (N-A dropped); we report the mean over cells of the per-cell

resampled re-judgings of all six cells are ranked and compared by Kendall’s τ ; we report the mean
over B pairs. (iii) Flip rate: per facet, the probability that a k-vote reduction differs from that facet’s
modal reduced outcome, averaged over all facets and cells.

variance for k close to the pool size; conclusions about small k (the operating regime) are unaffected.

Checklist compliance. Mapping GAUGE onto the checklist of Zhu et al. (2026b), each axis against
shipped artifacts:
(1) Outcome validity. The grading target is a calibrated envelope, not one analyst’s spreadsheet, and

R ELATED W ORK

Benchmarks for professional finance agents. Until recently, financial evaluation of language models
mostly meant question answering over filings or market data. A newer generation of benchmarks
instead asks agents to do the professional job end to end, and each makes a distinct measurement
choice. BankerToolBench evaluates complete junior-banker workflows, from data rooms and marketdata tools through Excel models, presentations, and written reports, and grades them with stakeholderoriented criteria authored with practitioner input (Lau et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026a). FrontierFinance
is the closest to GAUGE in task design: it contains 25 from-scratch financial-modeling tasks across
five model types, each with an expert-built reference model, a detailed rubric, an initial validity gate,
and a human-expert baseline (Krumdick et al., 2026). BigFinanceBench evaluates 928 open-ended,
multi-source research questions by scoring the visible steps of the workflow, such as source choice,
definitions, adjustments, and calculations, against point-weighted rubrics (Wang et al., 2026). BlueFin
covers 131 spreadsheet synthesis, manipulation, and comprehension tasks with 3,225 granular criteria
and validates its agentic judge against expert labels (Kundurthy et al., 2026).
These are strong designs for what they measure, and Table 20 makes the comparison explicit along
the measurement dimensions this paper is about. The shared choice that GAUGE departs from is
the referent: when a task’s target is a forward-looking valuation, all four grade the agent’s numbers
by proximity to one expert’s numbers, and Section 2 measures what that rule does to professionals
themselves. The critique is scoped accordingly. It concerns from-scratch valuations scored against a
single author’s point estimates; it does not concern the uniquely verifiable spreadsheet operations
that several of these benchmarks intentionally target, for which a single reference is the right design.
GAUGE’s second departure is the treatment of structural failure: categorical defects impose hard
score caps instead of being folded into a weighted average, with FrontierFinance’s initial validity
gate as the closest published analog.
Spreadsheet and artifact-level evaluation. SpreadsheetBench and related spreadsheet suites express correctness as a deterministic cell, formula, or state match (Ma et al., 2024), and repositorylevel code benchmarks such as SWE-bench grade a complete artifact rather than an isolated an54

swer (Jimenez et al., 2024). A financial model shares the artifact-level dependency structure of a
repository, since one wrong link propagates through everything downstream, but it adds a property
that code mostly lacks: non-identifiability. Two linked, internally consistent models can legitimately
differ in their revenue paths, capital structures, and discount rates, so state matching cannot serve
as the whole grading rule. GAUGE therefore keeps deterministic checking exactly where it is valid
(accounting identities, formula liveness, statement linkage) and switches to empirical ranges only for
the judgment-bearing quantities. LiveBench and LiveCodeBench motivate continuously refreshed
evaluation data as a contamination control (White et al., 2024; Jain et al., 2024); GAUGE implements
the same idea for licensed private data through its withheld refresh pool, with overlap anchors so that
scores remain comparable across versions.
Measurement validity and LLM judging. A growing measurement-science literature treats the
benchmark itself as the object under study. Construct-validity audits ask whether a score supports
its intended interpretation rather than merely being repeatable (Bean et al., 2026), and BetterBench
codifies benchmark-quality criteria at the level of documentation and design (Reuel et al., 2024).
The agentic-benchmark checklist examines task validity, contamination, honest failure accounting,
and grader integrity (Zhu et al., 2026b); work on rating indeterminacy separates judge error from
items that genuinely admit several defensible answers (Guerdan et al., 2026); and item-sampling
methods study how much evaluation a finite budget buys (Polo et al., 2024; Hofmann et al., 2025).
GAUGE operationalizes this program for a professional domain. The peer-workbook audit tests
the single-reference assumption directly; the human study supplies known-groups evidence; the
perturbation control measures selectivity rather than coverage alone; and the released per-vote judge
records make judge noise inspectable instead of laundering it into a scalar. Appendix V maps each
checklist axis to a shipped artifact.

L IMITATIONS

The envelope is a reference distribution of observed practice, not ground truth: an in-band value is
defensible in the sense that professionals demonstrably use it, which is weaker than being correct, and
only 17 undirected pairs support the implied-price disagreement tail. The corpus, its seniority labels,
and the human participants all come from one commercial provider network with a US listing skew,
and we did not independently re-verify individual credentials. Twenty-three of 56 facets depend on
one frozen LLM judge; the audits of Section 4 establish stability, 460-case expert agreement, and
rank-neutrality under a rival judge, but not per-case correctness. The longitudinal panel covers 48 of
196 tasks at one generation per agent–task pair, so full-bank generalization and generation variance
are quantified by bootstrap and sentinel-set estimates rather than measured exhaustively. Finally,
the peer-workbook audit of Section 2 applies a shared tolerance rule rather than reproducing each
competing benchmark’s complete rubric, and observed workbook differences mix legitimate choice
with date, horizon, purpose, and quality heterogeneity; the audit’s conclusion is correspondingly
scoped to the single-reference rule itself.
