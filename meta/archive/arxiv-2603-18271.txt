Abstract
Ambiguity poses a major challenge to large language models (LLMs) used as robotic planners. In this
paper, we present Scene Graph-Chain-of-Thought (SG-CoT), a two-stage framework where LLMs
iteratively query a scene graph representation of the environment to detect and clarify ambiguities.
First, a structured scene graph representation of the environment is constructed from input observations, capturing objects, their attributes, and relationships with other objects. Second, the LLM is
equipped with retrieval functions to query portions of the scene graph that are relevant to the provided instruction. This grounds the reasoning process of the LLM in the observation, increasing the
reliability of robotic planners under ambiguous situations. SG-CoT also allows the LLM to identify
the source of ambiguity and pose a relevant disambiguation question to the user or another robot.
Extensive experimentation on simulations demonstrates that SG-CoT consistently outperforms prior
methods, with minimum improvements of 4% in ambiguity detection and 4% and 15% in success rates
in single-agent and multi-agent environments respectively.
Keywords: Human-robot interaction, Ambiguity resolution, Scene graphs, Large language models

To bridge the above mentioned gap, a Scene
Graph-Chain-of-Thought (SG-CoT) is proposed
in this paper. SG-CoT is a robotic planning framework that integrates scene graphs with the reasoning capabilities of LLMs to enhance robustness
against ambiguity. The framework works in two
stages. First, a structured scene graph of the environment is built using a VLM capturing semantic
attributes and relationships among objects. This
representation moves beyond high-level descriptions and reveals environmental nuances, which
are crucial for detecting ambiguities in various
scenarios. Secondly, the LLM is paired with a tooluse capability. The LLM receives the high-level
descriptions of its observations and the instruction as input. It then begins its thought process
and calls a valid API function to access nodes
and edges of the scene graph, required to complete the instruction, grounding its thoughts into
visual context. Upon retrieval of all relevant information, the LLM makes a decision on whether
multiple valid interpretations of the user instruction exist or not. In this way, ambiguities arising
from both the environment and underspecification
by the user can be resolved. In contrast to purely
text-based planners, SG-CoT provides integration
of scene graphs with iterative reasoning, allowing
the LLM to pinpoint the reason for the ambiguity
and hence generate clarification questions targeted
towards resolving the ambiguity.
Two experiments were conducted to validate
the proposed method. First, evaluation was performed on simulated tabletop environments presented by SayCan (Ahn et al. 2022). Second,
the methodology was tested on multi-robot tasks

2023). To these environments, ambiguity across
three different categories was introduced by applying perturbations to both the environment states
and the instructions, removing contextual information from the robot’s view. Based on the
presented results, it was observed that SG-CoT
demonstrated superior performance compared to
previous approaches in both executing tasks and
asking the correct clarification questions whenever
required.

2 Related work
The literature survey has been organized into
three components, namely task planning with
LLMs, ambiguity in LLMs and scene graphs, each
of which aligns with a central element of the proposed method. The survey is concluded with the
summary of our main contributions.
Task planning with LLMs: Robotic task planning (Srivastava et al. 2014) involves translating natural language commands into executable action sequences based on input and past
observations. There have been several previous
approaches (Huang et al. 2022; Park et al. 2024;
Ahn et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2023; Liang et al.
2023; Song et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2022) that
use LLMs for this purpose. To increase reliability of LLMs as planners, ProgPrompt (Singh
et al. 2023) used a programmatic prompt structure, LLM-Planner (Song et al. 2023) used a
framework for dynamic grounded re-planning and
ReAct (Yao et al. 2022) interleaved reasoning
traces with actions. To further enhance reliability,
LLMs employed feedback loops from the environment (Huang et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2024; Bhat
et al. 2024) to detect failures and iteratively refine
their plans. However, these approaches assume the
environment contains sufficient context to infer
a definitive action and will execute an action
regardless of the state of the environment. As mentioned in KnowNo (Ren et al. 2023), such behavior
can cause actions with clear safety hazards, like
placing a metal bowl in the microwave.
Ambiguity resolution in LLMs: Several prior
works have proposed methodologies to tackle the
inability of LLMs to handle ambiguity (Kuhn
et al. 2023; Murzaku et al. 2025; Zhang and Choi
2023; Hu et al. 2025). For robotics in particular,
most previous research focuses on underspecification by the users. KnowNo (Ren et al. 2023)
2

used conformal prediction to generate a statistically guaranteed set of plausible actions, asking
for help when this set contains more than one
option. CLARA (Park et al. 2024), which classifies
user commands as clear, ambiguous, or infeasible
through uncertainty estimated via stochastic context sampling, is used as the basis for the problem
formulation, with focus restricted exclusively to
ambiguity. Furthermore, the problem is expanded
beyond simple user underspecification to address
a broader class of ambiguities: those arising from
the environment itself, the inherent uncertainty
of partially observable environments, and those
occurring in more complex instructions.
Scene graphs: A scene graph is a semantic representation of a scene in which the environment
is modeled as a set of objects, their attributes,
and the relationships between them (Chang et al.
2023). They are applied in computer vision for
image retrieval (Johnson et al. 2015), image captioning (Yang et al. 2022; Nguyen et al. 2021),
visual question answering (Hildebrandt et al.
2020) and image generation (Johnson et al. 2018).
Various works have also explored their application
in robotics (Nguyen et al. 2021; Zhu et al. 2021;
Werby et al. 2024) and more recent approaches
have integrated them with LLMs for generation
and understanding (Liu et al. 2023; Zhai et al.;
Jiang et al. 2024; Ekpo et al. 2024; Rana et al.
2023; Yin et al. 2024). The proposed framework
is built upon graph-grounded LLM planners like
SayPlan (Rana et al. 2023), that pairs an LLM
with an API allowing it to expand and collapse a
hierarchical 3D scene graph to dynamically manage its own context. However, unlike SG-CoT,
SayPlan does not ask for clarification, making it
less reliable in ambiguous situations. In addition,
it assumes a pre-constructed scene graph and does
not support incorporation of open-vocabulary representations of the graph structure.
Based on the above mentioned three components, this paper makes three primary contributions. First, it proposes SG-CoT, a framework
that integrates scene graph construction with
iterative chain-of-thought reasoning by an LLM
to enhance robotic task planning under ambiguous situations. Secondly, ambiguous scenarios are
formally classified into three distinct types, including environment-induced ambiguities, which are
largely unexplored by prior approaches. Third, the
correctness of clarification questions generated by

the LLM is measured to determine whether they
successfully pinpoint the source of ambiguity.

3 Proposed Methodology
In this section, the proposed framework, referred
to as SG-CoT, is introduced. The problem statement is presented first, followed by an introduction
of scene graph generation and representation, and
finally, the reasoning process that utilizes the
scene graph for disambiguation and interaction.

3.1 Problem Statement
The problem setup involves a single or multi-agent
robotic system integrated with an LLM as a highlevel planner for each individual robotic agent Ri .
Given a natural language instruction I and an
image observation O from the environment, the
objective of the system is to generate a sequence
of high-level actions ai ∈ A, where A denotes the
predefined set of executable actions available to
the robot, to accomplish the defined task. To evaluate the planner’s robustness in handling ambiguity, controlled perturbations were introduced
in both the instruction and the environment,
as shown in Fig. 1. Instruction-level ambiguity,
referred to as underspecification, involves omission
of explicit referential grounding in the instruction.
For example, replacing a grounded reference (e.g.,
“place the red block over the green bowl” ) with an
ungrounded term (e.g., “place something over the
green bowl” ). Environment-level ambiguity relates
I to the set of objects X present in O. Let A(I )
be the set of attributes describing a target object

A(I )} be the set of objects in the scene satisfying these properties. Absence is defined as the
condition Xmatch = ∅, and multiplicity as the condition |Xmatch | > 1. Multi-agent systems are also
subject to observation-level ambiguities occurring
from the partially observed nature of the environment. Let each robot Ri be able to observe
only a subset ORi ⊆ O of the environment, with

A scene graph provides a structured representation of the environment, derived from the observation O. Formally, the scene graph is defined as
G = (V, E ), where V = {v1 , v2 , . . . , vn } represents the set of nodes, each corresponding to an
object o ∈ Xscene detected in the environment.
Each node vi is associated with a set of attributes
A(vi ) = {a1 , a2 , . . . , ak } that describe its observable properties, such as color, size, or higher-level
states like is broken or is open. The edge set E
captures pairwise relations between objects, where
each edge is represented as a tuple (vi , rij , vj ) ∈ E ,
where rij denotes a semantic relation between vi
and vj generated from an open-ended vocabulary.

follows an iterative reasoning framework allowing it to utilize the graph effectively for task

ambiguity, allowing it to ask a relevant clarification question that will help in disambiguating the

The approach follows a loop of observation,
reasoning, function calling and clarification which
continues until the LLM has sufficient context to
generate a definitive action sequence or a targeted
clarification question. This loop ensures that each
reasoning step is validated with the scene graph
or the user, preventing propagation of hallucinations. The process is initialized by constructing an
initial prompt P0 , which contains the user instruction I , the set of detected objects, and the schema
of the scene graph. The schema includes the set
of all valid attribute types (e.g., color, size) and

edge types (e.g., on top of, inside of), providing the LLM with the necessary vocabulary for
querying the graph. A conversation history, H , is
maintained and is initially set to this prompt, P0 .
Following initialization, the process enters an
iterative loop where at each turn t, the LLM
is prompted with the accumulated history Ht .
It generates a response consisting of two parts.
First, a reasoning trace, Tt , which details the
step-by-step articulation of its reasoning. Secondly, if the LLM concludes that more information is needed it ends its turn by outputting
either a clarification question Qt or a retrieval
function ft . if the output is a retrieval function, the system intercepts ft from the response
generated by the LLM. The function queries G
and retrieves the relevant nodes or edges. The
LLM has access to two different retrieval functions, retrieve node and retrieve edge. The
first function, retrieve node, extracts a list of
objects from the graph based on some condition. It can be either a direct object name or by
a specific attribute key-value pair. For example,
a query like retrieve node(attr key=’color’,
attr val= ’red’) would return the names of all
nodes in the scene that have the color red.
The second function, retrieve edge, can be
used to query the spatial and semantic relationships between nodes. It accepts optional

in both single-agent and partially observable

the method was compared against three existing baselines: an uncertainty-estimation based

the VLM. Objects were also detected using the
same model. Through the experiments, we aim to
answer the following research questions: (1) How
well does SG-CoT perform under various kinds
of ambiguities? (2) Given an ambiguous environment, can it ask a relevant clarification question?
(3) Can different robots communicate with each

To incorporate the ambiguity types described

of vague words. For absence, the target object was
removed, and for multiplicity the target object
was duplicated. In total, 400 trials were conducted
with 100 randomized configurations per ambiguity category, and 100 clear scenarios. In each trial,
the LLM received the current observation and the
task instruction. The action space consisted of
two high-level actions: pick and place and ask,
which together define a planning interface over the
environment state. The action pick and place
expects two arguments, the object to be picked
and the object where it should be placed, and is
executed on the precondition that no object is currently held, resulting in the object being placed
inside or on the specified receptacle. The action
ask expects a question string and an ambiguity
tag defining the type of ambiguity, and is invoked
when the observation does not allow a unique
grounding of the instruction. If the tag predicted
by the LLM matches the ground truth ambiguity
type, the LLM is provided with the missing target.
However, if the predicted tag does not match the
ground truth, the trial is immediately terminated,
resulting in an SR of 0 for the trial, as the LLM
was not able to identify the source of ambiguity.
The goal condition for each trial is satisfied when
the intended target object is successfully placed in
the specified container or spatial location.

task. In total 200 trials were conducted each with
random configurations.
Results: As shown by Table. 4, SG-CoT
achieves SR of 75% with gemini-2.5-flash
and 59% with Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct over
observation-level ambiguity, corresponding to
improvements of 16% and 15% respectively over
the next best baseline, ProgPrompt, showing its
effectiveness in communication-driven reasoning
under partial observability. Since prior approaches
depend on simpler environmental representations,
they can hallucinate details about the environment and resort to executing an action instead
of asking a question. SG-CoT forces an LLM to
query parts of the scene graph for details of the
environment, preventing it from making up new
details and rely solely on the returned results for
its reasoning.

The graph must have the following
structure:
Nodes: The nodes must be represented
by objects. You will already be
provided with the object list.
Edges: These should represent the
relationships between each pair of
objects. These relationships can
include spatial directions (left of,
above, below, on top of, etc.) or
semantic relations (joined with,
inside of, etc.) to properly model
how each object is related to the
others.
Attributes: Each node must have an
attribute of the object like color,
type etc.
Input: You will be given the image
of a tabletop scene as input, as well
as the object list over the table to
label the nodes.
Output: Must strictly be a JSON
object with the following schema:
{"nodes": [{"id": i, "attributes":
{a 1, a 2, ..., a k}}], "edges":
[{"source": i, "target": j,
"relation": r}]}

In this paper, Scene Graph-Chain-of-Thought
(SG-CoT) was proposed, a framework that allows
large language model (LLM) based robotic planners to iteratively query and reason over a scene
graph representation of the environment in order
to make decisions. This framework allows the LLM
to be resistant to ambiguity by grounding its reasoning in reality, shown by a 4% enhancement in
overall success rate in single-agent environments
and 15% in multi-agent settings. The method
also allows the LLM to pinpoint the reason of
ambiguity and ask a question required for disambiguation, leading to a minimum 10% improvement in success rate. However, there are some
limitations to SG-CoT. The current evaluation
is limited to simulation-based experiments, and
no real-world experiments were performed where
perception noise and execution uncertainty are
inherently present. In addition, the method relies
on a vision–language model (VLM) to infer spatial
and semantic relationships in a single step, which
can hallucinate with real-world objects and introduce incorrect edges into the scene graph, potentially hampering the reasoning process. Finally,
the token usage and inference latency increase
with the number of ambiguities present in the
scene, which may limit scalability for more complex instructions . In the future, the framework
could be extended to embodied AI tasks such as
vision–language navigation in real-world indoor
environments.
