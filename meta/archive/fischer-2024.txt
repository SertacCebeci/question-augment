Improving Wald’s (approximate) sequential probability ratio test
by avoiding overshoot

Abstract
Wald’s sequential probability ratio test (SPRT) is a cornerstone of sequential analysis. Based
on desired type-I, II error levels α, β, it stops when the likelihood ratio crosses certain thresholds,
guaranteeing optimality of the expected sample size. However, these thresholds are not closed form
and the test is often applied with approximate thresholds p1 ´ βq{α and β{p1 ´ αq (approximate
SPRT). When β ą 0, this neither guarantees error control at α, β nor optimality. When β “ 0
(power-one SPRT), this method is conservative and not optimal. The looseness in both cases is
caused by overshoot: the test statistic overshoots the thresholds at the stopping time. Numerically
calculating thresholds may be infeasible, and most software packages do not do this. We improve
the approximate SPRT by modifying the test statistic to avoid overshoot. Our ‘sequential boosting’
technique uniformly improves power-one SPRTs pβ “ 0q for simple nulls and alternatives, or for
one-sided nulls and alternatives in exponential families. When β ą 0, our techniques provide
guaranteed error control at α, β, while needing less samples than the approximate SPRT in our
simulations. We also provide several nontrivial extensions: confidence sequences, sampling without
replacement and conformal martingales.

Outline of the paper and our contributions In this paper, we propose a general sequential
boosting method to uniformly improve (super)martingale based sequential tests by avoiding an overshoot
at 1{α (Section 2). This means that our boosted sequential tests are never worse than the initial tests,
but may stop earlier and are more likely to achieve a rejection. In Section 3, we use this to uniformly
improve conservative power-one SPRTs for simple and one-sided nulls and alternatives. We provide
nontrivial extensions and applications of our method in Section 4, improving some existing confidence
sequences, methods for sampling without replacement and conformal martingales. In Section 5, we
demonstrate how our boosting approach should be applied if a stop for futility is to be included and
type II error control is desired. Finally, we discuss extensions of our method and existing approaches
for dealing with overshoot when using the SPRT (Section 6). All formal proofs are provided in the
supplementary material.

Challenging Wald’s two-sided approximate SPRT

While the previous sections focused on power-one SPRTs (β “ 0) where we only stop to reject the null
but not to accept it, we now consider the case of β ą 0, where we also wish to stop if it is unlikely that
we would reject the null if we continued testing. We will show how we can use a boosting technique
similar to that in Section 2 to construct powerful anytime-valid tests that allow to “stop for futility”
(stop and accept the null).
Our main comparison point will be Wald’s approximate two-sided SPRT, which uses thresholds of
p1 ´ βq{α and β{p1 ´ αq. Importantly, out of the three desiderata of (a) type I error control at α, (b)
type II error control at β, (c) optimal sample size, these approximate thresholds guarantee none of the
three properties. In this section, we will first develop a general method with valid type I error control
for test supermartingales, and then show how the stop for futility can be chosen to obtain tight type I
and type II error control in the SPRT case. The latter method often outperforms Wald’s approximate
SPRT in the sense that while guaranteeing (a) and (b), our method also manages to typically/often
stop sooner than Wald’s (though not guaranteeing (c)).

Discussion

6.1

Testing a forecaster

The classical SPRT by Wald [38, 39] assumes that the null hypothesis and the alternative hypothesis
are specified in advance in the form of simple distributions P0 and P1 , respectively. However, in
Section 3.2 we have seen that the SPRT remains even valid if we replace the fixed alternative P1 with a
predictable plugin Pt1 that depends on the data X1 , . . . , Xt´1 P X observed so far. In general, the null
distribution Pt0 and the alternative distribution Pt1 considered at each step t P N can be chosen freely
based on the previous data X1 , . . . , Xt´1 and some additional information Z1 , . . . , Zt P Z. Shafer [26]
formulated this as a forecaster-skeptic testing protocol, where the forecaster announces a probability
distribution Pt0 at each step t and a skeptic tests the forecaster by specifying an alternative Pt1 . For
example, one can think of the forecaster as a weather forecaster, which is tried to be invalidated by a
skeptic. We summarized this general testing protocol in Algorithm S.1 (see supplement).
The extension of our boosting technique (Algorithm 1) to the general testing problem in Algorithm S.1 is straightforward, since at each step t we only require the current martingale value

6.2

Other approaches to avoid Wald’s approximated thresholds

The problem of “overshoot” in the SPRT was already noted by Wald [38, 39] and examined in many
previous works. One line of work approximates the exact type I and type II error of SPRTs based
on Brownian motion and renewal theory [27, 29, 48, 17]. This approach was particularly driven
by Siegmund, who gives a comprehensive overview in his book [29]. The provided approximations
are asymptotically (for α Ñ 0 and β Ñ 0) correct under certain assumptions. In Supplementary
17

Material S.6, we compare our boosted SPRT to the SPRT with Siegmund’s approximations in a simple
Gaussian setting (indeed, this is the only setting where the application of Siegmund’s approximations
seems to be straightforward), which shows that the latter method requires even less sample size.
Hence, if Siegmund’s approximations apply and are reliable, we recommend their use. However,
the advantage of our proposed boosting technique is its much broader applicability. For example,
Siegmund’s approximations cannot be applied to the one-sided SPRT without separation (Section 3.2)
or any of the applications provided in Section 4, since the employed test statistics are not independent
(if pδt qtPN and pκt qtPN are predictable in Sections S.1 and 4.3). In contrast, we do not make any
separation or independence assumptions. Also note that in contrast to Siegmund’s approximations our
boosting technique provides non-asymptotic validity.
Another approach is to truncate the SPRT by prespecifying a maximum number of observations N .
For simple distributions of the likelihood ratio and sufficiently small N it is then possible to calculate
the type I and type II error for given thresholds exactly [1], which can be used to find appropriate
bounds for the SPRT. Such an approach is frequently employed in group sequential trials [14, 43].
For more complex distributions one could approximate the error probabilities using simulations. This
often leads to considerable computational effort, which sometimes can be reduced using importance
sampling [28].
One limitation all the above approaches have in common is that the test statistics and their
distributions must be specified in advance. This is not possible in the general forecaster-skeptic testing
protocol described in the previous section. Hence, while our boosting approach is applicable to such
complex and adaptive testing protocols, all of the aforementioned methods fail. Furthermore, after
calculating a boosting factor bt with our method, one can always plug it back into the expectation
((12) in the one-sided or (27) and (28) in the two-sided case) and only accept the boosting factor if the
expectation is indeed bounded by 1. If a boosting factor cannot be accepted, one can try to calculate
another boosting factor or set bt “ 1 if no greater boosting factor can be found. In this way, one can
always ensure that the resulting test is conservative, meaning that it provides the desired type I and
type II error. This is neither possible with Siegmund’s approach nor when using simulations.
Moreover, our approach provides a test supermartingale, which yields an e-value at stopping times
[22, 8, 26]. For example, this can be important for optional continuation [8, 26], combining the evidence
with other test results [35] and in multiple testing [49, 7].

7

Summary

We introduced a general approach to uniformly improve nonnegative (super)martingale based sequential
tests by avoiding an overshoot at the upper threshold 1{α (where α is the desired type I error). We
illustrated its application by uniformly improving the conservative power-one SPRT for one-sided
null and alternative hypotheses. We also obtained an alternative to the two-sided SPRT with Wald’s
approximated thresholds, where our alternative provides valid type I and type II error control while
often reducing the sample size. Our sequential tests can also be used to tighten confidence sequences
and apply in nontrivial settings like sampling without replacement and conformal martingales.

S.4

Implementation of the boosted two-sided SPRT

In this section we provide some details on our implementation of the two-sided boosting method
proposed in Theorem 6 that we, for example, used to generate Figure 4.
According to Proposition 7, one possibility would be to just replace the inequalities in (27) and
(28) with equalities and then use a solver for nonlinear equation systems to find the optimal boosting
factors. Appropriate solvers for this purpose are provided by the R packages nleqslv [10] and BB [32].
While these work most of the time, we noticed that the algorithms sometimes fail to find a solution
and then produce a warning, e.g. if our current boosted likelihood ratio value Λboost
t´1 is very close to
1{α. For this reason, in our implementation we used a slightly different approach by formulating the

We solved this using the NLOPT_LN_COBYLA algorithm from the nloptr [15] R package. While
it produced the same results as the previously mentioned approaches in most cases, we found it to be
more reliable in extreme scenarios (e.g. when our current boosted likelihood ratio value is very close to
one of the thresholds). To avoid inflated error probabilities by all means, we verify every boosting
factor by plugging it into (27) and (28). If one of the two expected values is greater than 1 ` 10´7 , we
set both boosting factors to 1. This ensures that we always have (sufficiently accurate) type I and
type II error control, even if the optimization algorithm fails to provide valid solutions. In order to
avoid conservatism by setting the boosting factors to one in those cases, one could also consider to
apply the different algorithms one after the other if one fails to provide a sufficient solution. However,
in our simulations we found the described proceeding to perform sufficiently well.
