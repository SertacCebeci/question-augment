Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models usually supervises reasoning with scalar
outcome rewards, such as binary correctness. Such rewards provide an optimization direction but
rarely explain how a model should revise its mistaken reasoning, which can encourage shortcut
learning and brittle policies. We propose SocraticPO (Socratic Policy Optimization), a policyoptimization framework that augments RL rollouts with Socratic-style natural-language guidance.
During rollout, the student first answers independently; if the answer is incorrect, a teacher
diagnoses the attempt and provides concise corrective guidance, after which the student continues
under the expanded context. Crucially, this guidance is paired with reward decay: correct answers
obtained after teacher intervention only receive decayed rewards, preventing the policy from
treating teacher help as a free path to reward. Since SocraticPO only modifies the rollout process
while leaving the standard expected-reward objective intact, it can be plugged into existing policygradient backends such as Reinforce++. Moreover, because the teacher provides only text-level
guidance, SocraticPO can leverage stronger black-box teacher models without requiring access
to logits or distribution matching. On undergraduate-level scientific reasoning benchmarks from
SciKnowEval, SocraticPO improves over strong RL and self-distillation baselines. Ablations show
that both targeted guidance and reward decay are necessary, with reward decay mitigating reliance
on assisted correction.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have advanced rapidly with transformer architectures, scaling, and instruction
tuning [1, 3, 5, 31, 35]. Their post-training increasingly relies on reinforcement learning (RL), where a policy
is optimized from task rewards or preference-derived signals [8, 22, 27]. For reasoning tasks, however, RL
feedback is often reduced to a scalar outcome signal, such as binary correctness. Such feedback tells the model
which trajectories should be reinforced, but not how a mistaken reasoning process should be repaired [7].
This limitation is especially problematic in multi-step scientific reasoning. A wrong final answer may arise
from a localized conceptual error, an incorrect comparison, or a misplaced intermediate value. Outcome
rewards provide gradient-level optimization pressure, but they do not provide thought-level correction. As a
result, the policy may discover shortcuts that satisfy the verifier without learning robust reasoning.

Human learning offers a different pattern of supervision. Teachers rarely provide only a grade; they inspect the
learner’s attempt, identify the source of the mistake, and give hints, critiques, or next-step suggestions [34, 39].
The Socratic tradition frames this process as guided inquiry rather than direct answer transfer [20, 24].
Inspired by this view, we introduce Socratic Policy Optimization (SocraticPO), which brings Socratic-style
guidance into the rollout stage of RL. A student first attempts the problem independently. If the attempt
is incorrect, a teacher provides natural-language guidance conditioned on the student’s response, and the
student continues under the expanded context.
Guidance must be introduced carefully. If a response corrected after teacher intervention receives the same
reward as one solved independently, the policy may learn a training-time shortcut: wait for help, use
the teacher-provided clue, and still collect full reward. Assisted correctness can then improve without a
corresponding improvement in unassisted reasoning. SocraticPO therefore pairs teacher guidance with reward
decay: correct answers obtained after teacher intervention receive decayed rewards, and the reward decreases
as more guidance rounds are needed. This encourages the student not only to reach the correct answer, but
also to internalize the correction and solve earlier with less assistance.
SocraticPO changes only the rollout process. The student still optimizes a standard expected-reward objective,
while teacher utterances are appended as text context for subsequent student attempts rather than optimized
as imitation targets. As a result, SocraticPO can be instantiated with existing policy-gradient backends such
as Reinforce++ [15]. Since the teacher is required only to produce text-level guidance, the framework can
also leverage stronger black-box teacher models without accessing logits, token-level likelihoods, or matching
teacher and student distributions, unlike probability-matching approaches such as OPD [2, 11].
We evaluate SocraticPO on undergraduate-level scientific reasoning benchmarks derived from SciKnowEval [10].
Compared with strong RL baselines, including Reinforce++ [15] and GRPO [25], as well as self-distillationstyle training such as SDPO [17], SocraticPO achieves the best average performance. Ablations further show
that both components are necessary: targeted guidance provides useful correction beyond blind retrying,
while reward decay mitigates reliance on teacher-assisted correction.
Our main contributions are:
• A general SocraticPO framework. We propose an interactive policy-optimization framework that augments
RL with teacher-guided reasoning correction and remains compatible with existing policy-gradient backends.
• Reward decay for SocraticPO. To make teacher-guided RL train the student rather than reward dependence
on assistance, we introduce a reward decay mechanism for assisted correctness. We further characterize its
normalized-advantage properties, showing non-negativity, monotonicity, and saturation decay.
• Black-box teacher compatibility. Our method only requires teachers to provide text-level guidance instead
of distribution matching, making it compatible with stronger API-based or otherwise black-box teachers.

Related Work

2.1

Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

Reinforcement learning has become a central paradigm for aligning LLMs with human preferences and task
rewards [8, 19, 22, 27, 43]. PPO [23] is widely used in RLHF pipelines, and feedback can also be provided
by AI systems rather than direct human annotation [4]. More recent LLM training methods adopt simpler
or more scalable policy-gradient variants, including REINFORCE-style updates [15, 28, 38] and group-wise
relative objectives such as GRPO [25].
Most of these methods optimize scalar reward signals [12]. In reasoning tasks, such rewards identify whether a
trajectory should be reinforced but do not explain which part of the reasoning process should be corrected [6,
29]. Process-supervision work shows that intermediate feedback can be more informative than outcome
supervision alone [33], yet how to inject corrective information into on-policy RL rollouts remains underexplored.
SocraticPO addresses this gap by adding natural-language correction to rollout sampling while retaining a
standard policy-gradient objective.

2.2

Human Guided Teaching

Educational research emphasizes that effective teaching is interactive rather than purely evaluative. Tutors
scaffold learning by inspecting a student’s current attempt, diagnosing misconceptions, and providing hints or
next-step guidance [34, 39]. The Socratic tradition similarly frames learning as guided inquiry: questions and
critiques help the learner revise an answer without simply copying an expert solution [24]. Recent NLP work
has explored Socratic tutoring and personalized teaching dialogues with LLMs [20].
SocraticPO adapts this teaching principle to RL training. The teacher does not merely label an answer as
wrong; it provides language-level correction conditioned on the student’s failed attempt. At the same time,
SocraticPO differs from tutoring systems whose goal is to optimize dialogue quality. Our goal is to improve
the student policy through RL, so teacher guidance is paired with a reward design that encourages the student
to solve earlier and rely less on future intervention.

2.3

Student--Teacher Paradigms in LLMs

Teacher–student learning is widely used in LLM post-training to transfer capability from stronger models to
smaller or weaker students. One common paradigm is teacher-generated supervised fine-tuning (SFT), where
a stronger model produces instructions, answers, or reasoning traces that are then treated as demonstration
data for the student. Self-Instruct, Alpaca, WizardLM, Orca, and step-by-step distillation all follow this
broad pattern [14, 21, 30, 37, 40]. Recent work also studies how to synthesize student-consistent SFT data
from a teacher so that the demonstrations better match the student’s learning state [16].
Another paradigm is knowledge or policy distillation, where the student is trained to approximate a teacher’s
behavior more directly. Classical distillation matches teacher distributions or soft targets [13], while onpolicy distillation adapts this idea to LLMs by using model-generated or teacher-shaped trajectories during
training [2, 11, 26]. Self-distillation and hybrid distillation variants further use the model’s own successful
responses, privileged trajectories, or routed samples as training targets [9, 17, 18, 32, 41]. Despite differences
in data source and objective, these methods generally ask the student to imitate, approximate, or absorb
teacher-like behavior.
SocraticPO adopts a different teacher–student relation. The teacher is not a demonstration generator whose
answer should be copied, nor a distributional target whose probabilities should be matched. Instead, it
acts as an interactive guide: after a failed student attempt, the teacher diagnoses the mistake and provides
concise corrective guidance, while the student still produces its own subsequent response and is optimized
only through task reward. Because this guidance is text-level, SocraticPO can also use black-box teachers as
an additional benefit of the design.

Method

Traditional scalar rewards only tell the model whether an answer is correct or incorrect. They provide an
optimization signal, but not a diagnosis of the reasoning error that caused the failure. In human learning, a
teacher often plays this diagnostic role: by pointing out where the student’s current attempt goes wrong, the
teacher can help the student revise more efficiently than a grade alone. SocraticPO turns this idea into a
policy-optimization mechanism. It modifies the rollout stage by inserting conditional teacher feedback after
failed student attempts, so that the policy learns from trajectories that contain both the original mistake and
a correction-oriented context for the next attempt.

4.3

Reward Decay

Step-wise rewards alone, however, do not solve credit assignment across samples in the same batch. If every
correct response receives the same reward, then a problem solved independently at step 1 and another problem
solved only after several rounds of guidance would contribute the same positive reward, even though they

student can benefit from teacher intervention during training without learning to solve earlier or independently.

further calibrates credit across the batch by decaying rewards for later-step correctness.

The first property shows that SocraticPO does not punish corrected reasoning. Once a sample is answered
correctly, the corresponding student tokens receive a non-negative normalized advantage, even if the answer is
obtained after teacher guidance. Thus, reward decay does not discard assisted successes; it keeps them as
valid positive learning signals.
The second property formalizes the batch-level credit assignment discussed above. Among samples that
eventually become correct within the same batch, SocraticPO gives a larger normalized advantage to those
corrected at earlier steps. This prevents an independently solved problem and a problem solved only after
multiple rounds of guidance from receiving indistinguishable credit. In this sense, reward decay calibrates
positive reward across samples by favoring trajectories that become correct with fewer guidance rounds.
The third property explains how reward decay mitigates assisted reward hacking. If teacher guidance makes
almost all active samples correct, then pk → 1 and the advantage approaches zero. Once a guidance round
becomes non-discriminative because nearly everyone can solve the task with that amount of help, trajectories
from that round no longer provide strong positive reinforcement. This means that reward decay can prevent
the student policy from gaining excessive advantage merely by relying on teacher intervention.

further include diagnostic analyses on teacher-side information and teacher–student similarity to better
understand when the interaction mechanism helps.

Experimental Setup

Models. We use Qwen3-4B-Instruct as the student policy πθ . For teacher guidance, we consider two settings:
Qwen3-4B-Instruct as a self-guidance teacher and Qwen3.5-27B as a stronger teacher. This allows us to

separate the effect of the interaction mechanism from the effect of teacher capacity.

Datasets and metric. Following the SDPO evaluation setup, we use four undergraduate-level scientific
reasoning benchmarks from the reasoning subsets of SciKnowEval [10]: Chemistry, Physics, Biology, and
Material. We report accuracy on each domain and the unweighted average across domains.
Baselines. We compare against Reinforce++ [15], GRPO [25], GSPO [42], and SDPO [17]. Reinforce++, GRPO

and GSPO represent RL methods driven by scalar rewards, while SDPO represents a self-distillation-style
baseline.

correctness, but reward decay is needed to turn those guided repairs into independent reasoning ability.

5.4

Additional Analysis

Teacher access to ground truth. We study whether the teacher should receive ground-truth answers. Although

such access seems useful for generating precise feedback, Figure 5 shows that it can degrade student performance
in most cases. We attribute this to two factors. First, even when the prompt explicitly asks the teacher not
to reveal the answer, a teacher with ground-truth access may still leak solution-specific hints. This failure
mode is inherently limited by the teacher model’s instruction-following ability. Second, ground-truth access
can shift the teacher from diagnosing the student’s reasoning process to reverse-engineering hints from the
known answer. Both effects may encourage shortcut learning rather than robust correction.
At the same time, ground-truth information is not intrinsically harmful. If the teacher can avoid answer leakage
and use the reference solution to diagnose the student’s error, then access to the answer or even a standard
reasoning process can substantially improve the quality of guidance, as suggested by the Qwen3-8B-Instruct
self-guidance result on Chemistry. The effect of ground-truth access is therefore conditional: it can strengthen
Socratic guidance when the teacher follows the teaching protocol well, but it can become negative when the
teacher turns the answer into overly direct hints.

Conclusion

We presented SocraticPO, a policy-optimization framework that addresses a limitation of scalar reward
feedback in LLM reinforcement learning. Outcome rewards indicate whether a response should be reinforced,
but they do not explain how a mistaken reasoning process should be repaired. SocraticPO augments rollout
sampling with natural-language teacher guidance: the student first attempts a solution, receives targeted
correction only after an incorrect response, and continues under the expanded dialogue context.
Teacher guidance must be controlled carefully. If assisted correct answers are rewarded as strongly as
independent correct answers, the student may exploit teacher intervention instead of improving its own
reasoning. SocraticPO therefore pairs guidance with reward decay, assigning smaller rewards to answers
that become correct only after more interaction rounds. Experiments on SciKnowEval scientific reasoning
benchmarks show that SocraticPO improves average performance over strong RL and self-distillation baselines,
while ablations highlight the importance of both targeted guidance and reward decay.
Our findings suggest that natural-language correction can serve as process-level supervision inside RL without
requiring teacher distribution matching. Because the teacher is used only as a text-producing guide, SocraticPO
can leverage black-box mentors and remains modular with respect to the underlying RL optimizer. Future
work can further study teaching protocols, teacher-side information, and broader tasks where reasoning errors
are diagnosable before the final answer.
