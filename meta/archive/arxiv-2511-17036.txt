Do Vision–Language Models Understand Visual Persuasiveness?
A Diagnosis via Visual Persuasive Factors
Gyuwon Park1 and Hyounghun Kim1,2
1
Graduate School of Artificial Intelligence, POSTECH
2
Department of Computer Science and Engineering, POSTECH
{gyuwonpark,h.kim}@postech.ac.kr

Abstract
Visual persuasion uses images to shape cognition, emotion, and behavior, with its effects
depending on both visual attributes and semantic context. Despite recent progress, it remains unclear whether Vision–Language Models (VLMs) understand visual persuasiveness.
This motivates us to ask: can VLMs assess
whether an image persuasively supports an intended message, which visual factors shape
this judgment, and do they align with human
judgments? Through empirical analyses on
image-message pairs where human raters consistently agree on the persuasiveness judgment,
we show that VLMs exhibit a recall-oriented
bias: they over-predict images as persuasive
while achieving high recall. We introduce Visual Persuasive Factors (VPFs), a taxonomy
informed by cognitive psychology for quantifying visual cues that shape persuasive judgments. Our factor-level analysis reveals that
VPFs distinguish human persuasiveness judgments, whereas VLMs only partially reproduce
these patterns—often generating false positives
by treating persuasion-relevant cues as sufficient evidence. Building on this insight, we
evaluate VPF-guided interventions and find that
properly framed VPF knowledge can improve
performance, but merely specifying visual cues
or adding step-by-step reasoning is insufficient.
By analyzing model rationales at the level of
functional reasoning steps, we further identify
a central bottleneck in connecting object identification to semantic message alignment.1

Introduction

Visual persuasion refers to the use of images and
visual elements to influence cognition, emotion,
and behavior, and it plays a central role in advertising, political communication, and social media
(Chandler and Munday, 2011; Joo et al., 2014;

Kim et al., 2025). Visuals are not just decorative—they reliably shape attitudes and behavior,
as classic persuasion theory (Petty and Cacioppo,
1986) and public-health evidence on pictorial warnings have shown (Noar et al., 2016; Brewer et al.,
2016). Visual elements are therefore central to persuasion, but their effects unfold through their interaction with semantic context, persuasion strategies,
and individual psychological dispositions (Kumar
et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025). However, existing
work provides limited insight into how well VLMs
actually understand visual persuasiveness in this
broader context—why certain images are persuasive, which visual factors contribute to their effects,
and how these factors differ from those underlying
human judgments remain unclear.
In this work, we address this gap by introducing
a framework for examining the role of persuasionrelevant visual factors and comparing their associations with VLM and human judgments. To isolate
the effect of visual factors, we control for the inherent subjectivity of persuasiveness judgments (Matz
et al., 2024). Specifically, we use the Personalized Visual Persuasion (PVP) dataset (Kim et al.,
2025), which provides image-message pairs with
multiple human persuasiveness ratings, and retain
562 clearly separable persuasive (High) and nonpersuasive (Low) binary cases with almost-perfect
agreement (Landis and Koch, 1977). We then evaluate whether VLMs can assess how persuasively an
image supports a given target message, as shown
in Fig. 1 (Left). Surprisingly, we find that even in
these seemingly straightforward scenarios, VLMs
struggle to exhibit genuine understanding of visual
persuasiveness; they tend to over-predict persuasiveness, revealing a pronounced recall-oriented
bias. Together with insights from the Elaboration
Likelihood Model (Petty and Cacioppo, 1986), we
hypothesize that VLM limitations stem from how
models identify, weight, and contextualize visual
and semantic cues that contribute to persuasiveness.

To make this hypothesis operational, we propose
Visual Persuasive Factors (VPFs), a novel structured taxonomy and behavioral diagnostic framework informed by cognitive psychology (Adams
and Osgood, 1973; Janiszewski, 1990; Elder and
Krishna, 2012) for measuring cues that shape persuasiveness judgments in image-message pairs, as
illustrated in Fig. 1 (Right). VPFs span three
levels—perceptual, compositional, and semantic—
covering color and brightness; saliency and
message-relevant region placement with respect
to the center and rule of thirds; and the presence
of key object, human, and text. A validation survey with 141 annotators, who are distinct from the
original PVP raters, provides evidence for the validity of these factors. Using VPFs, we compare
human ground-truth persuasion labels with VLM
predictions, revealing three key insights:
• VPFs differentiate high- and low-persuasiveness
cases based on interpretable visual and semantic
dimensions.
• VLMs only partially reproduce human-aligned
VPF patterns, capturing some persuasive signals
while showing inconsistent weighting across
models and distorted associations with factors
such as human and text presence.
• VLM false positives arise from overgeneralizing
persuasion-relevant cues: among ground-truth
Low items, models often predict High when
images contain plausible persuasive cues—e.g.,
key object presence—mistaking them for sufficient evidence of persuasiveness.
Leveraging these insights, we further examine

whether VPF-guided interventions can reduce the
human–VLM gap in persuasive judgment. Focusing on key object presence as a semantic cue that
reliably separates human judgments yet also contributes to VLM false positives, we test whether
knowledge injection and reasoning scaffolds can
help VLMs use persuasion-relevant cues more appropriately. We find that VLMs can improve when
such cues are explicitly framed as auxiliary cues
to be interpreted in context rather than as fixed
evidence for persuasiveness. However, naive cue
provision or explicit reasoning (Wei et al., 2022)
is not sufficient for robust improvement: direct
grounding often degrades performance, and reasoning with explicit cues can even amplify the
recall-oriented bias, suggesting a failure to reliably connect visual cues to the final persuasive
judgment. To better understand this limitation, we
extract and decompose model-generated rationales
into three functional steps—(i) object identification,
(ii) semantic message support, and (iii) final judgment connection—and ablate each step to examine
how it affects model predictions. This step-wise
ablation shows that the difficulty lies in linking
object-level and semantic evidence to communicative intent.
Our contributions are threefold: (1) we propose
Visual Persuasive Factors (VPFs), a taxonomy informed by cognitive psychology, as a structured
framework for visual persuasive understanding; (2)
we use VPFs as a cognitive factor lens to compare human-labeled and VLM-predicted persuasiveness, showing that VPFs distinguish human

judgments while VLMs only partially reproduce
these human-aligned patterns and overgeneralize
persuasion-relevant cues; and (3) we conduct VPFguided intervention and reasoning analyses, revealing that semantic grounding alone does not close
the gap and that the key bottleneck lies in connecting visual evidence to communicative intent.

2

Related Work

2.1

AI-based Persuasion

Research on computational persuasion began in
text-only dialogue settings, with corpora such
as Persuasion for Good (Wang et al., 2019) enabling strategy-based persuasive response generation; later work incorporated affect, user traits,
and LLM-generated messages to study empathy,
personalization, and human judgments of persuasiveness (Samad et al., 2022; Matz et al., 2024; Bai
et al., 2025a). In visual persuasion, early work used
syntactic visual cues to infer communicative intents
in persuasive images (Joo et al., 2014), while recent
datasets such as PVP pair 28,454 persuasive images
with 596 messages annotated for nine persuasion
strategies (Kim et al., 2025). While these studies
establish visual persuasion as a computational task,
they leave open how VLM judgments relate to the
visual factors that humans use to assess persuasiveness. Recent evaluation protocols measure persuasiveness more directly, including human-in-theloop and controlled prompting setups for assessing
model persuasion and safety implications (Durmus
et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2024). Our study extends this paradigm to the visual domain by using
factor-level analysis to compare model and human
decisions.
2.2

Cognitive Foundations for Visual
Persuasive Factors

Our taxonomy draws on cognitive psychology and
communication theory. The Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty and Cacioppo, 1986) posits
two paths to persuasion: a central route operating
via message-relevant elaboration, and a peripheral
route relying on immediate or heuristic cues. The
VPFs can operate through either route, depending
on how it is processed.
Numerous behavioral studies demonstrate that
color is a fundamental component of visual perception and persuasion (Adams and Osgood, 1973;
Mapelli and Behrmann, 1997; Gegenfurtner and
Rieger, 2000; Labrecque and Milne, 2011), and is

especially influential in advertisements and campaigns (Labrecque et al., 2013; Jr. et al., 2018;
Armstrong et al., 2021). Beyond color, composition and visual hierarchy guide attention, as shown
by center bias in natural scenes (Tatler, 2007;
Zhang et al., 2008) and the rule-of-thirds in photographic composition (Krages, 2005; Mai et al.,
2011). Advertising and eye-tracking studies further show that visual layout shapes gaze allocation and brand responses, highlighting composition as an attention-guiding factor in persuasive
media (Janiszewski, 1990; Maughan et al., 2007).
At the semantic level, persuasiveness depends on
whether image objects align with the intended argument and form a coherent narrative, requiring
models to select relevant objects and connect them
to an argument structure (Chung et al., 2024). Such
core objects may include products (Elder and Krishna, 2012), text (Zhang et al., 2018; Seo, 2020),
or people (Selove, 2014; Meng et al., 2026).
2.3

High-Level Vision–Language Reasoning

Recent foundation models (OpenAI, 2025; Comanici et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025) have advanced rapidly on complex tasks, while chain-ofthought prompting improves reasoning in arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic settings (Wei
et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Yasunaga et al.,
2024). VLMs extend this paradigm by coupling
LLMs with visual encoders for multimodal inputs (Liu et al., 2023, 2024; Bai et al., 2025b; Team
et al., 2025). Recent VLM research has moved beyond object recognition toward abstract and symbolic reasoning, including visual metaphor understanding, where models must bridge perception and
semantics (Akula et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2024).

3

From VLM Persuasive Understanding
to Visual Persuasive Factors

The first goal of our study is to examine whether
VLMs possess a meaningful notion of visual persuasive understanding. In the following sections,
we establish the evaluation setting using a highagreement subset (§3.1), introduce our novel taxonomy Visual Persuasive Factors (§3.2), describe how
factors are extracted and measured (§3.3), and validate them through an independent survey (§3.4).
3.1

Evaluating VLMs’ Understanding of
Visual Persuasiveness

To assess whether VLMs understand visual persuasion, we derive a high-consensus subset from the

PVP dataset (Kim et al., 2025). We bin the original 0–10 persuasiveness ratings into Low (0–2),
Mid (3–7), and High (8–10) bands, and retain 562
image-message pairs with four-annotator agreement in the Low or High band, i.e., almost-perfect
agreement under Landis and Koch (1977). We then
frame persuasion as a binary judgment task (image + message → {High, Low} treated as ground
truth; details in Appendix A). Within this highagreement subset, we find limited evidence that
VPF effects are broadly moderated by personality.2
Hence, this subset comprises persuasive and nonpersuasive cases that are relatively clear to viewers
Yet VLMs (OpenAI, 2025; Team et al., 2025; Bai
et al., 2025b) consistently show poor performance
and a recall-oriented bias: they predict High too
often, achieving near-perfect recall with inflated
false positives, as shown in Tab. 3 baseline.3
3.2

Defining Visual Persuasive Factors

Building on prior findings in §2.2, we define Visual
Persuasive Factors as a three-level decomposition
of persuasive content in image-message pairs.
• Perceptual factors capture immediate visual impressions, including colorfulness, color entropy,
representative color congruence, and brightness.
• Compositional factors describe the spatial organization of persuasive cues, including how visual
saliency and message-relevant regions are distributed with respect to center bias and rule-ofthirds structure.
• Semantic factors capture various objects, including whether a key object, human, or text
is present and whether they provide evidence that
modulates the intended persuasive appeal.
Our VPFs are not intended to be exhaustive; they
organize visually measurable cues into three diagnostic levels for VLM evaluation.

Semantic Level. We extract three factors from

semantic factor as a binary presence indicator and
either ground-truth labels or model predictions as
the outcome, reporting odds ratios (ORs). Full

5

VPF-Guided Knowledge Injection and
Reasoning

The analysis in §4 motivates two questions. First,
if VLMs are already sensitive to VPFs but overuse
them as generic positive signals, can explicit VPF
knowledge help models treat these cues as contextdependent evidence rather than sufficient evidence
of persuasiveness? Second, can reasoning scaffolds
help models integrate available visual evidence into
a persuasive judgment? We refer to the latter ability
as persuasive integration: the ability to use visual
cues as evidence for or against the communicative
intent of an image-message pair. We test this via
VPF-guided interventions, focusing on key object
cues because they reliably separate human judgments yet also contribute to VLM false positives.
5.1

Task Description

Testing cue use through knowledge injection.
To test whether explicit VPF knowledge helps models avoid treating persuasion-relevant cues as determinative of persuasiveness, we consider two
injection variants: Factor-aware Cognitive Injection and Aligned Key Object Context. (i) Factoraware Cognitive Injection provides the model with
explicit knowledge of the proposed VPFs during
evaluation, either one factor at a time or all nine
factors together, while presenting them only as
context-dependent auxiliary cues and not prescribing any fixed effect on the persuasive judgment.
(ii) Aligned Key Object Context supplies compact
structured information about which detected key
object candidates are semantically aligned with the
message (e.g., “devices” for the message “remove
unnecessary electronic devices”). If no key object

is found, no additional context is provided.
Testing persuasive integration through reasoning scaffolds. To examine whether structured
reasoning can improve persuasive judgment, we
consider three scaffold variants: (i) general Chainof-Thought (CoT), which appends the instruction
“Let’s think step by step” to encourage explicit stepwise reasoning (Wei et al., 2022); (ii) KnowledgeConditioned Chain, a lightweight reasoning scaffold that explicitly prompts the model to (a) identify
candidate key objects, (b) assess their alignment
with the message, and (c) make a final persuasiveness judgment; and (iii) Key Object-Aware CoT,
which uses the same step-by-step prompting while
additionally informing the model of the relevant
key object cue (i.e., Aligned Key Object Context
setting). Both task prompts are in Appendix E.

5.3

Diagnosing Persuasive Integration
through Rationale Analysis

We analyze model rationales by decomposing them
into functional steps to identify which components
are most critical for persuasive integration. Prior
work treats rationales as supporting evidence for
model predictions and evaluates their alignment
and faithfulness (Lei et al., 2016; DeYoung et al.,
2020). Building on this view, we use rationales as
a diagnostic proxy for examining how models use
evidence and integrate it into persuasive judgments.
Step-wise rationale ablation. For each detected
key object from the VPF pipeline (§3.3), we prompt
the model to generate a per-object rationale explaining whether and how that object contributes
to persuasiveness (details in Appendix E.3). We de-

compose each rationale into three functional steps:
S1, which identifies the relevant object; S2, which
explains how that evidence semantically supports
or undermines the message; and S3, which connects the preceding analysis to the final persuasive judgment. The functional spans are tagged
by GPT-5 under a text-preserving constraint. To
ensure semantic parsing reliability, we report results only on the 92.04% of rationale segmentations
verified as correct by human evaluators (details in
Appendix E.4).
We then assess both individual-step contributions and their interactions through single- and
pair-step ablations, selectively removing one or
two tagged spans and re-evaluating the model on
the same item relative to the Rationale Injection
output in Tab. 3. The full rationale injection prediction serves as the baseline for comparison. For
each ablation, we measure (i) the item impact rate,
defined as the rate at which predictions change,
and (ii) the change in prediction accuracy against
the ground-truth label. In addition, as a robustness
check against incidental sentence-form effects, we
shuffle the rationale steps while preserving their
content and find that model behavior remains 93.79–
96.45% consistent, indicating that it is driven more
by the rationale content itself. Full parsing procedure and details are in Appendix E.4.

non-trivial portion of samples, and pair-step ablations all produce double-digit item impact rates
across models. Thus, earlier steps are not simply
ignored; rather, each part of the rationale actively

6

Conclusion

Our work investigates visual persuasive understanding in VLMs from a cognitive psychology perspective. We introduce Visual Persuasive Factors,
a structured taxonomy for analyzing how visual
cues shape persuasiveness judgments. We show
that even in high-consensus cases, VLMs exhibit a
recall-oriented bias; VPF analysis further reveals
that VLMs only partially reproduce human-aligned
factor patterns, and their false positives arise from
conflating persuasion-relevant cues with message
support. Building on this insight, we evaluate VPFguided interventions and find that explicit factor
knowledge can help only when framed as auxiliary
cues rather than fixed evidence, while naive cue
grounding or step-by-step reasoning is insufficient.
Finally, rationale ablation identifies persuasive integration as a key bottleneck, highlighting that VLMbased visual persuasion evaluation requires connecting visual evidence to communicative intent.

Limitations
The primary objective of this study is to establish
a foundational diagnostic framework for assessing
the interpretive capabilities of VLMs regarding visual persuasiveness through the proposed VPFs.

Accordingly, VPFs are proposed as a behavioral
diagnostic framework for VLMs in the PVP-based
setting, rather than as an exhaustive model of visual persuasion in general. In light of this goal,
we acknowledge several constraints in our current
scope. First, by focusing on high-agreement scenarios to limit variability in human judgments, our
study did not explicitly model individual personality traits, which can significantly influence perceptions of persuasion. A comprehensive understanding would require integrating these personal
factors into the analysis. Future research will therefore aim to integrate the proposed VPF framework
with user-centric modeling and psychographic profiles to investigate how VLMs can navigate the
inherent subjectivity and individual variance in persuasive communication. Second, our VPF analysis
primarily examines factors at the level of individual perceptual, compositional, and semantic cues.
However, visual persuasion can emerge from interactions among multiple factors, such as how object
semantics, spatial composition, color, and message
framing jointly shape judgment. Future work will
extend VPFs beyond level-wise analysis toward
modeling cross-factor interactions and combined
cue configurations.

Ethical Considerations
We acknowledge the dual-use concerns inherent in
persuasion research. A deeper understanding of the
factors driving visual persuasion could potentially
be misused to create more effective disinformation
or manipulative propaganda. However, we also believe this research has significant positive societal
potential, such as in designing more effective public health and safety communications (e.g., “always
turn off the stove”). Our contribution is focused
on the fundamental analysis of VLMs’ cognitive
alignment and its current limitations, not on building persuasive applications. We hope this research
provides a foundation for developing VLMs that
can better understand complex human cognitive

processes in a responsible manner.
Furthermore, while we report effect sizes based
on standard criteria for formal consistency (Cohen, 1988), we emphasize that these effects can
have a much larger impact in real-world settings
than the reported metrics suggest. As argued in
the field of psychology by Funder and Ozer (2019),
effects typically categorized as “small” by conventional standards can be highly consequential when
they operate repeatedly, affect large populations,
or accumulate over time. In real-world visual persuasion settings, even small effects may become
consequential when repeatedly deployed at scale.
This motivates caution in interpreting and applying VPF-based analyses, especially in systems that
could be used to generate or optimize persuasive
visual content.

Models Used. These components collectively allow us to detect whether semantically referenced
entities are visually grounded in the image. Fig. 7
summarizes the extraction pipeline for key object
and human presence detection.
• Key Noun Extraction:
We used the
en_core_web_sm model as the parser from the
spaCy library for Part-of-Speech (POS) tagging
and noun phrase extraction. This lightweight
model was selected for its balance between
processing speed and linguistic accuracy.
• Key Object Presence Detection: We employed
the OWL-ViT v1 (Minderer et al., 2022) model.
OWL-ViT enables zero-shot object detection by
aligning vision and text embeddings, allowing us
to flexibly identify key visual concepts based on
extracted textual nouns.
• Human Presence Detection: We used the pretrained YOLOv8-nano (Jocher et al., 2023) as
the primary person detector, optimized for inference efficiency on GPU. In cases where no human instance was detected, we optionally crossvalidated results using the open-vocabulary detector above.
• Text Presence Detection: We identify text presence using a lightweight heuristic based on the
dataset-provided image descriptions. Specifically, we extract candidate textual content from
quoted spans (e.g., “...”, ‘...’) and ALL-CAPS
phrases that resemble signage or logos, based
on the observation that such patterns often correspond to explicitly rendered text in the associated images. This rule-based parsing yields 144
candidate instances out of 562 samples. Given
the relatively small number, we manually verify
each case to confirm whether the extracted text
is visibly present in the image, resulting in 124
validated instances (22.06%).

Validation Survey Details

Annotators. We recruited adult annotators via
Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk)8 and conducted
the survey through a custom web-based interface.
The HIT title warned workers that the task could
contain adult or sensitive content, and the task
description described it as an academic study on
message–image analysis and visual persuasion.
Workers were required to have more than 10,000
approved HITs and an approval rate above 98%.
Each HIT contained 12 image-message items and
was designed to take approximately 12–15 minutes based on repeated pilot runs of the task interface. Workers were compensated at $2 per HIT,
corresponding to an estimated hourly rate of approximately $8–$10. We did not collect protected
demographic attributes beyond platform-provided
worker identifiers used for task administration and
quality control. The entire data collection process
was conducted anonymously to protect participants’
privacy; no personally identifiable information (PII)
was collected or stored. In total, 476 participants
were collected, of which 141 were retained after
quality filtering.
Task Design. Each trial consisted of an imageonly stage followed by an image-message stage.
Participants were first asked to provide a binary
persuasiveness judgment (Low/High) based only
on the image, using a slightly adapted version of
the original dataset prompt to match the binary
format. This question was included to assess alignment with the original dataset judgments, yielding
approximately 80% consistency. The instruction
pages shown to annotators are provided in Fig. 10
and 11, while the two-stage task interface is illustrated in Fig. 12 and 13.
Subsequently, annotators rated the influence of
each Visual Persuasive Factor (VPF) on a 5-point
Likert scale (1: not at all influential, 2: slightly
influential, 3: moderately influential, 4: very influential, 5: extremely influential). For quality check,
when semantic-level factors (e.g., key objects, humans, or text) were present, annotators were additionally asked to provide brief explanations (1–2
sentences) describing whether and how these elements affected their judgment. Finally, annotators
provided a second binary persuasiveness judgment
based on the full image-message pair.

Annotation Protocol. Each item was annotated
by three independent participants. The full dataset
of 562 image-message pairs was covered three
times, resulting in triply annotated labels for all
items. To prevent redundancy and learning effects,
participants were not allowed to re-annotate items
they had previously seen, even if they participated
in multiple survey batches. As illustrated in Fig. 12
and 13, the task followed a fixed sequence within
each trial: (Q1) image-only persuasiveness judgment, followed by (Q2) factor-level ratings, and
(Q3) image-message persuasiveness judgment.
Quality Control. We implemented strict quality
control procedures to ensure annotation reliability.
As described in the instruction interface (Fig. 10
and 11), each task included 2 hidden attentioncheck (trap) questions among the 12 items; responses from annotators who failed both checks
were excluded from analysis. Participants were informed of the presence of such checks but not their
locations.
In addition, all free-text responses were manually reviewed by the authors, and annotations

Visual Persuasive Factors definitions

We have identified 9 key factors that can influence visual persuasion, categorized into three levels: perceptual, compositional, and semantic.
Please rate how much each factor influenced your overall impression of persuasiveness for the given image–message pair.

Perceptual-level

At the perceptual level, you evaluate how factors such as color and brightness—which are most immediately apparent—influence
persuasiveness.

Color : overall color palette (e.g. dominant hues, saturation, and color contrast).
Brightness : overall lightness

Compositional-level

Center refers to the middle area of the image. Rule-of-Thirds (RoT) refers to the 3×3 grid guideline: subjects placed near the grid
intersection points often feel visually emphasized (please refer to the below example).

D

Generalization Across Agreement
Levels and Model Variants

We further examine whether the main findings in
§4 generalize beyond the original high-agreement
subset and model set. Specifically, we test progressively broader annotator-agreement levels and
additional VLMs spanning different model scales,
families, and reasoning variants. Across these settings, we consistently observe the same three patterns: the recall-oriented bias, partial reproduction
of human-aligned VPF patterns, and false-positive
cue overgeneralization.
D.1

Generalization Across Agreement Levels

Our main analysis uses 562 image–message pairs
with almost-perfect annotator agreement to reduce
ambiguity in human persuasiveness judgments. To
examine whether the observed patterns depend
on this high-agreement setting, we progressively
broaden the evaluation set following the agreement
levels (i.e., Tab. 7). We consider (i) the original
Almost Perfect subset (N = 562), (ii) samples
spanning Almost Perfect through Fair agreement
(N = 834), and (iii) samples spanning Almost Perfect through Slight agreement (N = 4,531). For
the broader subsets, we retain items with a majority Low or High label and remove ties, thereby including substantially more diverse judgments while
maintaining at least minimal annotator agreement.
The recall-oriented bias persists across
broader agreement levels. As shown in Tab. 21,
all models continue to exhibit substantially higher
recall than precision. For the Almost Perfect–
Fair subset, recall ranges from 88.89% to 98.35%,
whereas precision remains between 37.44% and
47.19%. The same pattern persists after expanding to 4,531 samples through Slight agreement,
where recall remains 87.47–95.30% compared with

39.03–46.01% precision. Thus, the tendency to
over-predict High persuasiveness is not specific to
the original high-consensus subset.
VPF-based findings likewise remain stable
as agreement is relaxed. As shown in Tab. 22,
VLM predictions continue to reproduce only part
of the human-aligned VPF pattern. Across both
expanded agreement ranges, several persuasionrelevant factors—including color entropy, strategyconditioned brightness, compositional saliency,
and key object presence—remain associated with
predicted High persuasiveness, although their
strength and consistency vary across models. This
indicates that VLMs remain sensitive to meaningful persuasive cues without reproducing the full
human factor pattern.
The false-positive analysis shows a similarly persistent pattern. Among ground-truth Low items,
many of the same persuasion-relevant cues are repeatedly associated with erroneous High predictions. This includes perceptual, compositional, and
semantic factors such as color entropy, strategyconditioned brightness, saliency, key object presence, and human presence.
Overall, expanding the analysis from 562 highconsensus samples to as many as 4,531 samples
does not qualitatively alter our main findings. The
recall-oriented bias, partial reproduction of humanaligned VPF patterns, and false-positive cue overgeneralization persist under progressively broader
annotator agreement.
D.2

Generalization Across Model Variants

We next examine whether these findings are specific to the original model set. We expand the evaluation along three dimensions: model scale, using
Gemma3-4B, Gemma3-12B, and Gemma3-27B;
model family, using InternVL3-8B-Instruct; and
reasoning orientation, comparing Qwen3-VL-8BInstruct with Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking.
The recall-oriented bias generalizes across
model scale, family, and reasoning orientation.
As shown in Tab. 23, every additional model exhibits substantially higher recall than precision.
The pattern remains across the Gemma3 scale series and InternVL3, indicating that it is not specific
to a particular model size or family. It also persists across Qwen3-VL reasoning variants: Qwen3VL-8B-Thinking reaches 96.63% recall but only
44.91% precision. Thus, increased scale or explicit
reasoning orientation alone does not eliminate the
tendency to over-predict persuasiveness.

effect is not uniform across steps or models. In
particular, ablating the evidence-to-claim mapping
step, either alone or together with evidence detection, often yields the largest false-positive reduction, suggesting that models may overestimate persuasiveness when their rationales construct overly
strong links between visual evidence and the target
message. Fig. 15 further shows that this improvement should be interpreted as a correction–damage
trade-off: some ablations successfully convert false
positives into true negatives, while others also introduce new TN-to-FP errors. Overall, these results
indicate that false positives are caused by intermediate reasoning steps that over-align visual cues
with the persuasive claim.
