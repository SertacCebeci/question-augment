# Coincident failure

> Sources built independently from the same specification do **not** fail
> independently — measured twice, forty years apart, on human programmers and
> on coding agents. Independent *production* is not evidence of independent
> *error*, so the count of agreeing sources overstates the evidence they carry.

## The finding

**Knight & Leveson (1986), *IEEE TSE* 12(1), 96–109.** N-version programming[^s1][^s2]
proposes tolerating faults by running several independently written
implementations of one specification and voting on their outputs; its
[[calibration|reliability]] claim rests on the axiom that independently developed programs fail
independently. The authors tested the axiom: 27 versions of a launch-interceptor
program written independently from the same specification by students at two
universities, then subjected to one million tests against an oracle. Their
result, from the abstract: the programs were "individually extremely reliable"
but "the number of tests in which more than one program failed was
substantially more than expected". The axiom is rejected — not the utility of
redundancy, but the arithmetic that redundancy was being priced with.

**Ron, Baudry & Monperrus (2026), arXiv:2606.20158.** The same specification,
the same million-test campaign, with 48 implementations generated by five
families of coding agents across three programming languages. They report
"substantial common-mode failure, along the findings of Knight-Leveson", and
trace many co-occurring failures to places where the specification is
"particularly hard or ambiguous". They also report that redundancy still pays:
across three-version majority-voting units, the mean failure count falls from
387.44 for single versions to 130.99 for triples, with 11,844 units showing no
observed failures.

Two findings, not one: failures are correlated **and** voting still helps. What
the correlation destroys is the *prediction* — the independence model says how
much voting should buy, and it buys measurably less.

## Why it matters

1. **It attacks the premise of every count-based redundancy rule.**
   `byzantine-agreement-bound` (draft dropped) bounds $m$, the number of bad sources, and
   [[tree-search-methods]] counts $N$ samples. Both are sound only if the
   sources' errors do not arrive together. Here two experiments show that the
   most defensible procedure available for manufacturing independence —
   separate authors, separate institutions, separate languages, separate model
   families, one shared specification — leaves the errors correlated.
2. **The residual correlation has a locatable cause: the question.** Ron et al.
   trace co-occurring failures to the hard and ambiguous parts of the
   specification. Under that reading, a shared error among sources is a
   *measurement of the asker's own question*, not only of the sources. This
   reverses the usual inference from agreement and gives a use for it: cluster
   the inputs where the sources agree and are wrong, and you are pointing at
   the underspecified clauses.
3. **Diversity survives as a mechanism, not as an assumption.** Both papers
   keep redundancy: the 2026 numbers show triples cutting mean failures by
   roughly two thirds. What does not survive is computing the benefit from an
   independence model. An asker may still poll several sources; it may not
   multiply their error rates together.
4. **The result is one of the few replications in this part of the wiki.**
   Forty years apart, on human and machine producers of answers, with the same
   specification and the same scale of test.

## Transfer

| | |
|---|---|
| **Home field** | software versions produced from one written specification, executed on identical inputs, with an oracle available for a million inputs, and failure defined as a discrepancy from that oracle |
| **Here** | several sources answering one question, where the asker wants to treat their agreement as independent confirmation |
| **What survives** | the refutation and the experimental design behind it: the number of joint failures is comparable against what independence predicts, so "are these sources independent?" is an empirically decidable question rather than a stance; and the finding that deliberately maximised diversity of producer does not deliver it |
| **What does not** | the measurement itself, for want of an oracle. Knight & Leveson could count coincident failures because they had a gold program and a million labelled inputs; an asker with a question worth asking has neither, and therefore cannot estimate its own sources' correlation from the answers it is currently reading. A programming specification is also more explicit than most questions, which if anything makes the transferred estimate optimistic |

## Limits

- The claim here is "independently produced sources fail together more than
  independence predicts on these tasks", not a coefficient. Neither the
  correlation's size nor its dependence on the task is something this note
  states, and both experiments use a single specification — an unusual one
  (geometric predicates over radar input) chosen for its safety-critical
  flavour.
- Knight & Leveson has been contested in its own field; the authors published a
  reply to criticisms, which this note has not read. The numbers used above are
  the paper's **abstract** as reproduced in an archived copy; the full text was
  not read here and no per-test counts are claimed from it.
- `ron-2026` is a 2026 arXiv preprint; the figures quoted are its abstract's and
  are author-reported, with no independent replication. Its implementations were
  filtered by an admission step (48 admitted), so the population is
  post-selection.
- Both studies measure *programs*, which are deterministic and fail on a fixed
  input set. A source answering a question in prose has no comparable notion of
  a failing input, and the extension is this note's framing, not the authors'.
