ABSTRACT
Accurately predicting mortality risk in intensive care unit (ICU) patients is critical for clinical
decision-making. Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly explored for clinical prediction
using structured medical data, but their outputs may exhibit demographic disparities. Mitigating
such disparities without degrading predictive performance remains challenging.
We systematically investigate demographic bias in LLM-based ICU mortality prediction and
propose Case-Based Prompting (CAP), a training-free framework designed to improve the
empirical balance between predictive performance and subgroup fairness. CAP retrieves clinically
similar historical misprediction and demographic-sensitive cases with known outcomes and
incorporates them as case-level contextual evidence. We further evaluate model discrimination,
subgroup fairness, and feature-dependence consistency.
On MIMIC-IV, CAP improved AUROC from 0.806 to 0.873 and AUPRC from 0.497 to 0.694
compared with the Baseline Prompt. CAP also reduced several subgroup disparities, particularly
for sex and White–Black comparisons, although age-related disparities remained non-negligible.
Controlled demographic-information ablations showed that removing demographic information
did not uniformly improve both predictive performance and subgroup fairness, indicating distinct
performance–fairness patterns rather than a uniform benefit from demographic suppression.
Feature-dependence analysis showed generally consistent expressed clinical factor patterns
across demographic subgroups.
These findings suggest that CAP is a promising inference-time strategy for LLM-based clinical
prediction under the evaluated retrospective setting. Further validation across additional LLMs,
external datasets, and prospective clinical scenarios is required before deployment.

INTRODUCTION

In high-risk clinical environments, concerns regarding the fairness of artificial intelligence (AI)
systems have become a central ethical focus in both academia and industry[1]. As AI technologies
become increasingly integrated into healthcare, their role in supporting clinical decision-making
has grown rapidly[2, 3]. In intensive care units (ICUs), machine learning models have been widely
explored for mortality risk prediction and illness severity assessment[4, 5]. Traditional ICU mortality
prediction models, such as APACHE, SAPS, SOFA-based risk scores, and machine learning
models built on structured clinical variables, have demonstrated strong predictive performance in
risk stratification. However, these models usually rely on predefined feature representations and
fixed prediction pipelines, and they do not directly provide natural-language reasoning processes
that can be inspected for potential demographic shortcuts or clinically unsupported explanations.
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being explored as general-purpose clinical
reasoning interfaces. Although structured ICU mortality prediction remains a tabular-data task,
LLMs provide a useful setting for studying fairness because they can transform structured
clinical variables into natural-language risk assessments, generate explicit reasoning chains, and
incorporate retrieved examples through in-context learning. Therefore, our goal is not to claim that
LLMs are inherently superior to conventional tabular models for ICU mortality prediction. Rather,
we focus on whether LLM-based clinical prediction systems exhibit demographic disparities
when applied to structured ICU data, and whether such disparities can be mitigated through a
lightweight inference-time intervention.
Fairness concerns are particularly important in ICU mortality prediction because systematic
performance disparities associated with demographic attributes, such as sex, age, and race/ethnicity, may lead to inequitable allocation of monitoring intensity, clinical attention, or medical
resources, thereby exacerbating existing health disparities[6–8]. For example, two ICU patients
may have similar severity scores, vital signs, laboratory measurements, and treatment interventions, but differ in demographic attributes. In such cases, an LLM may produce different mortality
estimates or explanations by overemphasizing age, sex, or race/ethnicity rather than objective
physiological evidence. This type of demographic shortcut reasoning is especially concerning
in high-stakes clinical settings, where even small differences in estimated mortality risk may
influence clinical prioritization and trust in AI-assisted decision support.
Demographic bias in LLM-based clinical prediction may arise from several sources. First, LLMs
may inherit statistical associations, stereotypes, and uneven disease descriptions from pretraining
corpora, including imbalanced representations of risk across sex, age, and race/ethnicity groups.
Second, clinical notes and electronic health records may themselves reflect historical inequities in
care delivery, documentation practices, treatment access, and subgroup representation. Third,
when structured ICU variables are converted into natural-language prompts, demographic tokens
may become salient contextual cues and may be over-weighted by the model, especially when
clinical evidence is incomplete, noisy, or ambiguous. As a result, an LLM may rely on age,
sex, or race/ethnicity as convenient proxies for risk rather than sufficiently integrating objective
physiological evidence.
Given these potential sources of demographic shortcut reasoning, LLMs introduced into this
high-risk clinical scenario face two key challenges. First, it is necessary to detect demographic
bias across patient subgroups, including uneven performance for female, older, or racial/ethnic
minority patients[9]. Conventional fairness metrics primarily measure output-level disparities,
but they may fail to capture latent feature-dependence bias, namely the tendency of a model to
rely on different clinical factors or reasoning patterns across demographic groups. Second, bias
mitigation should be achieved without substantially sacrificing predictive performance. Existing
mitigation strategies often rely on reweighting, fairness constraints, or model retraining, which may

2

not be feasible when model parameters are inaccessible and may also degrade overall accuracy
in high-stakes clinical settings.
Therefore, this study aims to systematically investigate the following three research questions:
• Is bias present? Do LLMs exhibit measurable performance disparities across demographic
subgroups in ICU mortality prediction?
• Where is bias? Are disparities confined to prediction outputs, or are they also reflected in
feature reliance and reasoning patterns?
• Can bias be mitigated without indiscriminately suppressing demographic information? Can a training-free prompting strategy improve the empirical balance between predictive performance and subgroup fairness while preserving potentially useful demographic
context?
To address these questions, we construct a multidimensional bias assessment framework and
propose Case-Based Prompting (CAP), a training-free inference-time prompting framework for
LLM-based ICU mortality prediction. CAP retrieves clinically similar historical misprediction and
demographic-sensitive cases with known outcomes and incorporates them into the prompt as
case-level contextual evidence. By providing concrete examples of prior prediction errors and
known outcomes, CAP aims to guide the model toward more clinically grounded reasoning and
reduce unsupported demographic shortcuts.
The main contributions of this work are as follows:
• We develop a multidimensional bias evaluation framework that integrates model discrimination, subgroup fairness, and feature-dependence analysis, enabling systematic
assessment of both output-level disparities and reasoning-pattern differences across demographic groups.
• We propose Case-Based Prompting (CAP), a training-free prompting framework that retrieves clinically similar historical misprediction and demographic-sensitive cases to provide
case-level contextual evidence for LLM-based ICU mortality prediction.
• We conduct a retrospective evaluation on the real-world MIMIC-IV dataset to examine
whether CAP improves the empirical balance between predictive performance and subgroup
fairness, while also analyzing feature-dependence consistency and representative case-level
reasoning patterns.
• We conduct controlled demographic-information ablations to separately evaluate demographic information presented to the LLM and demographic filtering used during retrieval,
thereby empirically characterizing the predictive contribution of demographic context and
the fairness–performance trade-off.

Related Works

2.1

Bias and Fairness in Clinical AI

Predictive models in healthcare, especially those deployed in high-risk settings such as intensive
care units (ICUs), have been widely reported to exhibit disparities across demographic attributes
including race, sex, and age [10–12]. Such disparities may arise from insufficient subgroup
representation, historical inequities in healthcare delivery, and documentation bias embedded in
3

electronic health records. A recent scoping review reported that a substantial proportion of clinical
machine learning studies showed evidence of bias, often disproportionately affecting vulnerable
patient groups [13].
Fairness concerns are particularly important in ICU mortality prediction, where biased risk
estimates may affect monitoring intensity, treatment prioritization, and resource allocation. For
example, van Schaik et al. [14] examined ICU mortality prediction models and showed that
aggregate performance metrics alone are insufficient to reveal disparities across race, sex, and diagnostic subgroups. More broadly, fairness metrics such as demographic parity, equal opportunity,
equalized odds, false-positive-rate gaps, false-negative-rate gaps, and subgroup discrimination
gaps have been widely discussed in the machine learning fairness literature [15]. Recent work
further emphasizes that observed fairness gaps should be statistically validated, because subgroup differences may arise from sampling variability rather than systematic unfairness [16, 17].
These studies motivate our use of multiple fairness metrics together with statistical validation.

2.2

LLMs for Clinical Prediction and Medical Fairness

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being explored for healthcare tasks, including
medical question answering, clinical reasoning, and prediction. Brown et al. [18] evaluated
GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 on clinical prediction tasks using VUMC and MIMIC-IV data and found that
their predictive performance and calibration were generally inferior to locally trained machine
learning models, while fairness patterns differed across demographic subgroups. This suggests
that predictive performance and subgroup fairness may not improve simultaneously in LLM-based
clinical prediction.
Beyond structured clinical prediction, recent medical AI studies have highlighted the importance of reasoning ability, multimodal evidence integration, disease-specific model development,
and human-centered trustworthiness in healthcare applications. These include LLM reasoning in
healthcare and medicine [19], cross-modal knowledge diffusion for medical visual question answering [20], specialized medical imaging models such as FATE-YOLO for brain tumor detection [21],
and human-centered trustworthy AI frameworks emphasizing explainability, human-in-the-loop
design, hybrid AI, and uncertainty quantification [22]. These studies suggest that medical AI
systems should be evaluated not only by predictive performance, but also by reasoning reliability,
interpretability, and trustworthiness.
Recent studies have also begun to examine fairness and demographic bias in medical LLMs.
Lu et al. [23] investigated bias in LLM-based healthcare advice and explored mitigation strategies
through fine-tuning and prompt engineering. FairMedQA provides an adversarial benchmark
for evaluating demographic bias in medical question answering by constructing counterfactual
question pairs across sensitive attributes [24]. Similarly, counterfactual fairness alignment has
been explored for medical LLMs, showing that demographic perturbation can help reveal unfair
output patterns [25, 26]. Compared with medical question answering or advice generation, ICU
mortality prediction requires calibrated risk estimation from structured physiological variables.
Therefore, fairness evaluation in this setting should consider not only counterfactual output
changes, but also subgroup-level errors, false-negative rates, and feature-reliance patterns.

2.3

Retrieval-Augmented and Case-Based Prompting

Retrieval-augmented generation and case-based reasoning provide LLMs with external examples,
documents, or cases that are relevant to the current query. Foundational work on few-shot
prompting and in-context learning has shown that LLMs can adapt their behavior based on
demonstrations provided in the prompt [27]. Further studies on demonstration selection and
4

nearest-neighbor in-context learning suggest that the choice of retrieved examples can substantially influence model behavior, particularly in few-shot or zero-shot settings [28, 29]. In clinical
reasoning, case-based comparison is also intuitively aligned with how clinicians refer to similar
historical cases when making decisions.
However, most retrieval-augmented prompting methods primarily aim to improve factuality,
contextual relevance, or predictive accuracy, while their role in fairness-aware clinical prediction
remains underexplored. For ICU mortality prediction, retrieved cases may be useful not only
because they provide clinically similar examples, but also because historical misprediction and
demographic-sensitive cases can provide corrective contextual evidence. This motivates our
proposed Case-Based Prompting (CAP) framework, which retrieves clinically similar historical misprediction cases and demographic-sensitive cases with known outcomes to guide LLM
reasoning during inference.

2.4

Fairness-Aware Prompting and LLM Debiasing

Bias mitigation methods can be broadly categorized into data-level, model-level, and inferencetime interventions. Data- and model-level methods, such as reweighting, fairness constraints,
adversarial learning, group-robust optimization, and distillation-based debiasing, often require
access to training data or model parameters [30]. For deployed LLMs, such access may be
limited, making inference-time debiasing strategies more practical.
Prompt-based debiasing provides a lightweight alternative for regulating LLM behavior without
updating model parameters. Prompting Fairness integrates causal reasoning into prompt design
to reduce the influence of social information on LLM decisions [31]. Metric-Fair Prompting guides
LLMs to make decisions under metric-fairness constraints [32]. Self-Debias introduces a selfcorrection framework to interrupt bias propagation in LLM reasoning trajectories [33], while C2PO
diagnoses and suppresses bias shortcuts through causal-contrastive preference optimization
[34]. These studies demonstrate the potential of inference-time interventions for reducing biased
outputs.
Beyond prompt-level debiasing, recent studies have also explored training- or system-level
fairness interventions in related AI tasks, such as Dual-Teacher Debiasing for mitigating domain
bias through teacher–student distillation [35] and FairCRS for improving user-oriented fairness
in conversational recommendation systems [36]. Although these methods address different
task settings from structured ICU mortality prediction, they highlight the broader importance of
diagnosing and mitigating bias in AI-driven decision systems.
Nevertheless, most existing LLM debiasing and fairness-intervention studies focus on general
NLP, recommendation, or benchmark classification tasks. Their direct application to structured
clinical prediction remains limited. Moreover, simply instructing an LLM not to rely on protected
attributes may be insufficient in clinical settings, because demographic attributes can sometimes
reflect clinically meaningful risk differences rather than unfair bias. CAP differs from generic
fairness prompts by providing concrete, clinically similar historical cases with known outcomes,
allowing the model to use case-level contextual evidence rather than abstract fairness instructions
alone.

METHODS

3.1

Problem Definition

The objective of this study is to predict in-hospital mortality risk for ICU patients using structured
clinical variables collected during the first 24 hours after ICU admission. Let
D = {(xi , ai , yi )}ni=1
denote the study cohort, where xi ∈ Rd represents the non-demographic structured clinical
features of patient i, ai denotes demographic attributes, and yi ∈ {0, 1} is the ground-truth
in-hospital mortality label. The complete structured input includes demographic characteristics,
laboratory measurements, vital signs, clinical severity scores, and treatment interventions. In this
formulation, demographic attributes are represented separately by ai , whereas xi includes the
remaining clinical variables used for risk estimation.
Given the structured patient information, an LLM-based prediction function fθ (·) generates an
estimated mortality probability:
p̂i = fθ (xi , ai ) ∈ [0, 1],
where p̂i denotes the predicted probability of in-hospital death. A binary prediction can be obtained
as
ŷi = I(p̂i ≥ τ ),
where τ is the decision threshold. In addition to the mortality probability, the LLM is required to
output a confidence score, key contributing clinical factors, and a brief reasoning summary in a
predefined structured format. These outputs allow us to evaluate not only prediction performance,
but also whether the model relies on different clinical factors across demographic subgroups.
To assess subgroup-level disparities, we consider commonly used demographic dimensions
in clinical fairness evaluation, including sex, age group, and race/ethnicity:
Asex = {male, female},

Aage = {18–59, 60+},

Arace = {White, Black, Asian, Other}.

For each demographic dimension, we define subgroup sets such as Smale , Sfemale , S18−59 , S60+ ,
SWhite , SBlack , SAsian , and SOther . Our primary fairness analyses are conducted on these marginal
demographic subgroups, while intersectional subgroup patterns are considered as supplementary
analyses when sample size permits.
We evaluate each prompting strategy from three perspectives: predictive performance, subgroup fairness, and feature-dependence consistency. Predictive performance is assessed using
discrimination, calibration, and classification metrics. Subgroup fairness is assessed by comparing
error rates and discrimination performance across demographic groups. Feature-dependence
consistency is evaluated by analyzing whether the key clinical factors generated by the LLM remain consistent across patient subgroups. This formulation allows us to examine both output-level
disparities and potential reasoning-pattern differences in LLM-based ICU mortality prediction.

3.2

Overview of Case-Based Prompting

We propose Case-Based Prompting (CAP), a training-free inference-time prompting framework
for LLM-based ICU mortality prediction. The core idea of CAP is to provide the LLM with clinically
similar historical cases as contextual evidence, so that the model can make predictions by
comparing the current patient with prior cases that contain known outcomes and documented
baseline errors or demographic-sensitivity annotations.
6

As illustrated in Figure 1, CAP consists of three main steps. First, for a target ICU patient,
structured clinical variables from the first 24 hours after ICU admission are converted into a
standardized patient summary. Second, CAP retrieves clinically similar historical cases from a
curated case repository. The repository contains cases that are both misclassified by the baseline
prompting strategy and sensitive to counterfactual demographic perturbations, and the retrieval
procedure prioritizes clinical similarity after first using demographic attributes only to identify
comparable candidate cases. Third, the retrieved case information, including patient features
and known outcomes, is incorporated into the prompt together with the target patient information.
The LLM then generates a mortality probability, a binary risk prediction, key contributing clinical
factors, and a brief reasoning summary.
Different from generic fairness-aware prompts that only provide abstract instructions such
as avoiding protected-attribute-based decisions, CAP introduces case-level contextual evidence
into the reasoning process. This design is motivated by clinical decision-making practice, where
physicians often compare a current patient with similar historical cases when assessing risk. By
grounding the model’s reasoning in clinically similar examples with known outcomes, CAP aims to
reduce unsupported demographic shortcuts and encourage greater reliance on objective clinical
evidence. Importantly, CAP does not require model retraining or parameter updates, making it
applicable to settings where the underlying LLM is used as a frozen inference model.
The detailed construction of the case repository and the retrieval algorithm are described in
the following subsections.

Case Repository Construction

To support Case-Based Prompting, we constructed a repository of historical clinical cases
from the training set only. The test set was not used during repository construction, retrieval
hyperparameter selection, or case annotation, in order to avoid information leakage. The final
repository was designed to include cases that satisfied two criteria: (i) they were misclassified
by the baseline prompting strategy, and (ii) their predictions were sensitive to counterfactual
demographic perturbations.
First, we applied the baseline LLM prompting strategy to the training set and identified
misclassified samples by comparing the predicted binary label with the ground-truth in-hospital
mortality label. False-positive (FP) cases were defined as patients incorrectly predicted to die
during hospitalization, whereas false-negative (FN) cases were defined as patients incorrectly
predicted to survive. Both error types were retained because they reflect clinically distinct failure
modes: FP cases indicate risk overestimation, whereas FN cases correspond to missed high-risk
patients. To reduce the influence of class imbalance, we applied a mild stratified sampling strategy
to ensure that both FP and FN cases were represented in the repository.
Second, we performed counterfactual demographic perturbation on misclassified cases from
the training set. For each candidate case, counterfactual variants were generated by modifying
one demographic attribute at a time, such as sex, age group, or race/ethnicity, while keeping all
non-demographic clinical variables unchanged. GPT-4 was used only as an auxiliary screening
model to compare the outputs between the original and counterfactual versions of candidate
cases. It was not used for final test-set prediction, model evaluation, ground-truth label generation,
or direct mortality prediction. A case was considered demographic-sensitive if the predicted
binary label changed after the perturbation or if the absolute change in predicted mortality
probability exceeded 0.05. Formally, for a candidate case with original predicted probability p̂i and
counterfactual predicted probability p̂cf
i , the case was retained if
|p̂i − p̂cf
i | > 0.05

or if the predicted binary label changed between the original and counterfactual versions.
From these screened cases, we selected 400 representative cases to construct the final
repository. Therefore, all cases in the repository were both misclassified by the baseline prompting
strategy and sensitive to at least one demographic perturbation. Each case was standardized
into a structured format containing demographic attributes, vital signs, laboratory measurements,
clinical severity scores, treatment interventions, baseline prediction, ground-truth outcome, error
type, and demographic-sensitivity annotation. This standardized representation allowed retrieved
cases to be consistently inserted into the prompt during inference.

in Table 1. The repository composition was fixed before final evaluation on the independent test

mortality prediction.

Demographic attributes were retained in the structured prompt but were not designated as

only in the first-stage candidate-filtering process to identify demographically comparable cases.
Specifically, candidate cases were first restricted to those within an age window of ±5 years of
the query patient, with the same sex and race/ethnicity. Exact age was used internally only for the
±5-year retrieval filter, whereas age was represented to the LLM using the predefined age-group
categories of 18–59 and 60+. Among the filtered candidates, clinical similarity was then calculated
using the weighted cosine similarity and clinical-threshold penalty defined above. Candidate cases
with a final similarity score of simfinal ≥ 0.8 were retained, and the most similar case was inserted
into the CAP prompt when available. The threshold of 0.8 was selected based on preliminary
retrieval coverage analysis on the training set, which showed that this value provided a reasonable
balance between clinical similarity and retrieval availability. If no candidate case satisfied the

effects of demographic information presented to the LLM and demographic filtering used during

3.5

Role of Demographic Attributes in CAP

CAP is not intended to be demographic-blind. Rather, it is designed to be demographic-aware but
clinically grounded. Demographic attributes may contain clinically relevant contextual information
in ICU mortality prediction. Age may be associated with physiological reserve, comorbidity burden,
and baseline mortality risk, whereas sex may be relevant in specific disease or treatment contexts.
Race/ethnicity requires more cautious interpretation because observed associations may also
reflect social determinants of health, healthcare access, documentation practices, treatment
patterns, and historical inequities rather than direct biological risk.
To avoid conflating fairness with demographic information removal, demographic attributes
were retained in the structured patient representation and remained available in the targetpatient input across the four primary prompting strategies. Importantly, we did not assume that
demographic sensitivity itself constitutes unfair bias, nor did we attempt to infer causal clinical
justification from retrospective observational data. Instead, demographic effects were interpreted
at an empirical and operational level. A demographic effect was interpreted as potentially
clinically contextualized when its influence on prediction co-occurred with relevant objective clinical
evidence, such as severity scores, physiological instability, laboratory abnormalities, comorbidity
burden, or treatment intensity. This operational criterion was used for auditing purposes and
does not establish that the demographic effect was clinically or causally justified. Demographic
subgroup differences or demographic terms appearing in model-generated reasoning were not,
by themselves, considered evidence of either a clinically justified effect or unfair bias.
We separately defined demographic sensitivity as a material change in model output after
modifying one demographic attribute while holding all non-demographic clinical variables unchanged. A material change was defined as an absolute mortality-probability difference greater
than 0.05 or a change in the binary prediction. Counterfactual demographic sensitivity alone
was not considered sufficient evidence of an unsupported demographic shortcut. Instead, an
unsupported shortcut was interpreted as demographic-dependent prediction or reasoning that
was not adequately grounded in the available objective clinical context. This distinction was
used as an operational auditing framework and does not establish a causal judgment about the
demographic attribute.
Within CAP, demographic attributes have restricted and explicitly separated roles. Age, sex,
and race/ethnicity may be used during first-stage retrieval filtering to identify broadly comparable
candidate cases, with age restricted to a ±5-year window around the query patient. Final case
ranking is determined by weighted clinical similarity and clinical-threshold consistency rather
than demographic similarity alone. Demographic information is also retained in the target-patient
and retrieved-case descriptions for transparency and fairness auditing, but the prompt explicitly
instructs the model not to treat demographic attributes as standalone evidence for mortality risk.
We empirically evaluated these design choices using controlled demographic-information
ablation, counterfactual sensitivity analysis, and retrieval-strategy ablation. These analyses
quantified the effects of retaining versus removing demographic information from the model
input, characterized prediction changes under demographic perturbation while holding clinical
10

variables constant, and isolated the contribution of demographic filtering during retrieval. Predictive
performance and subgroup fairness were evaluated jointly to examine the predictive contribution
of demographic context, counterfactual demographic sensitivity, potential shortcut behavior, and
the fairness–performance trade-off.

3.6

Prompting Strategies

To evaluate how different prompting strategies affect both predictive performance and subgroup
fairness, we designed four prompting strategies with increasing levels of guidance: Baseline
Prompt, Fairness-Aware Prompt, System 2 Prompting, and Case-Based Prompting (CAP). All
prompts followed a unified structure consisting of role definition, task description, patient information, decision constraints, and required output format. This unified design was used to ensure
that differences across methods were primarily attributable to the prompting strategy rather than
unrelated formatting variations.
The LLM was required to output a structured response containing the predicted in-hospital
mortality probability, binary risk prediction, confidence score, key contributing clinical factors,
and a brief reasoning summary. Complete prompt templates for all strategies are provided in
Appendix A.
• Baseline Prompt. The baseline prompt includes only the basic instruction for ICU mortality
prediction and the structured patient information. It does not include fairness-related
instructions, step-by-step reasoning requirements, or retrieved case information.
• Fairness-Aware Prompt. The fairness-aware prompt adds an explicit instruction that
demographic attributes should not be used as standalone mortality-risk evidence. The
model may consider demographic information when clinically relevant, but differences in
predicted risk must be supported by the patient’s overall clinical context and objective clinical
evidence. This prompt serves as an instruction-based debiasing baseline.
• System 2 Prompting. System 2 prompting combines fairness-aware instruction with
structured step-by-step clinical reasoning. The model is guided to first evaluate physiological
indicators, laboratory measurements, clinical severity scores, and treatment interventions,
and then synthesize these clinical factors before generating the final mortality prediction.
This strategy is intended to reduce intuitive or shortcut-based reasoning by encouraging a
more explicit evidence-based reasoning process[37–40].
• Case-Based Prompting (CAP). CAP extends the structured reasoning prompt by incorporating retrieved historical cases from the case repository. Each retrieved case includes standardized clinical features, the baseline prediction, the ground-truth outcome,
and demographic-sensitivity annotation. By providing clinically similar historical errors with
known outcomes, CAP supplies case-level contextual evidence to support more clinically
grounded risk estimation.

target-patient and retrieved-case descriptions, but age, sex, and race/ethnicity were excluded
from first-stage candidate filtering. Candidate cases were instead ranked directly using the same
weighted clinical similarity and clinical-threshold penalty as in Full CAP. All subsequent CAP
components were kept unchanged. All ablation settings were evaluated on the same independent
test set using the same Qwen3-32B backbone, inference parameters, decision threshold, and
statistical procedures as the primary analysis. Predictive performance and subgroup fairness were
evaluated using the metrics defined in Section 3.8, and the corresponding results are reported in
Section 4.5.
Counterfactual demographic-sensitivity analysis. As a complementary patient-level audit
of demographic dependence, we performed counterfactual demographic perturbation on the
independent test set. For each evaluable patient, one demographic attribute was modified at a time
while all non-demographic clinical variables were kept unchanged. Sex was switched between the
male and female categories, age group was switched between 18–59 and 60+, and race/ethnicity
was perturbed across the predefined racial/ethnic categories used in this study. For race/ethnicity,
a prediction was considered race/ethnicity-sensitive if at least one valid counterfactual racial/ethnic
category resulted in a material output change. For Full CAP, the identity and content of the
historical case retrieved for the original patient were kept fixed during counterfactual perturbation.
12

Therefore, this analysis was designed to assess sensitivity to demographic information presented
to the LLM while avoiding changes introduced through the retrieval pathway. A prediction
was considered demographic-sensitive if the absolute change in predicted mortality probability
exceeded 0.05 or if the binary prediction changed. The reported sensitivity proportion was
calculated as the number of demographic-sensitive predictions divided by the number of evaluable
test patients with valid outputs under both the original and corresponding counterfactual conditions.
This analysis was treated as a descriptive behavioral audit rather than a causal test of whether a
demographic effect was clinically justified or unfair.

3.8

Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Analysis

To systematically evaluate LLM-based ICU mortality prediction, we assessed each prompting
strategy from three complementary perspectives: predictive performance, subgroup fairness, and
feature-dependence consistency. This design allowed us to examine both output-level disparities
and potential differences in reasoning patterns across demographic groups.
Predictive performance. Overall predictive performance was evaluated using discrimination,
classification, and calibration metrics. Discrimination was assessed by the area under the receiver
operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC).
Classification performance was measured using F1-score, precision, sensitivity, specificity, falsepositive rate (FPR), and false-negative rate (FNR). Probability calibration was assessed using the
Brier score. All metrics were computed on the independent test set.
Subgroup fairness. Subgroup fairness was evaluated across demographic groups defined by
sex, age group, and race/ethnicity. Given the clinical importance of missed high-risk patients in
mortality prediction, we used the FNR gap as a primary fairness metric:
∆FNR (g, r) = |FNRg − FNRr | ,

(4)

where g denotes the target subgroup and r denotes the reference subgroup. We also reported
the FPR gap:
∆FPR (g, r) = |FPRg − FPRr | ,
(5)
and the equalized odds gap:
∆EOdds (g, r) = max (|FNRg − FNRr | , |FPRg − FPRr |) .

(6)

In addition, subgroup AUROC, AUPRC, and Brier score gaps were reported to characterize
disparities in discrimination and calibration. Absolute subgroup gaps greater than 0.05 were
interpreted as practically relevant differences and were considered together with confidence
intervals and statistical tests, rather than as deterministic evidence of bias.
Feature-dependence consistency. To examine whether LLM reasoning patterns differed
across demographic subgroups, we analyzed the three key clinical factors generated by the model
for each patient. These factors were mapped to standardized clinical categories when necessary,
such as severity scores, oxygenation, renal function, hemodynamic status, inflammatory markers,
and treatment interventions. For each subgroup, we constructed a frequency distribution of the
extracted key factors and compared subgroup-level feature-dependence patterns using Top-K
Jaccard similarity, cosine similarity, and Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence. Higher Jaccard and
cosine similarity, together with lower JS divergence, indicate more consistent feature-dependence
patterns across subgroups. These metrics were interpreted as descriptive evidence of reasoningpattern consistency rather than definitive proof of fairness.
13

Demographic-information ablation analysis. For the controlled demographic-information
ablations, predictive performance and subgroup fairness were evaluated jointly using the same
metrics as in the primary analysis. Full CAP was compared with variants that masked demographic
information from the model input or removed demographic filtering from case retrieval. The mean
FNR gap was calculated across the sex, age, White–Black, and White–Other comparisons; the
White–Asian comparison was excluded from this aggregate measure because of the relatively
small Asian subgroup. These comparisons were used to determine whether modifying the use of
demographic information affected predictive performance, subgroup disparity, or both, thereby
characterizing the empirical fairness–performance trade-off.
Statistical analysis. To avoid over-interpreting subgroup differences caused by random variation, 95% confidence intervals for predictive performance and fairness metrics were estimated
using patient-level bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations. For subgroup fairness gaps,
permutation-based tests were performed by permuting demographic group labels while keeping
predictions and ground-truth labels fixed. These permutation-test p-values assess whether the
observed gap within each method is larger than expected under random group-label assignment,
rather than directly testing the gap reduction between methods. Paired bootstrap resampling with
1,000 iterations was used for between-method comparisons because the evaluated methods were
applied to the same test patients. For the controlled demographic-information analyses, Full CAP
was compared with each ablation variant using paired differences in AUROC and mean FNR gap,
with differences defined as the ablation value minus the Full CAP value. The 95% confidence
interval for each between-method difference was obtained from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles
of the paired-bootstrap distribution. A between-method difference was considered statistically
supported when its 95% confidence interval excluded zero. Permutation-test p-values were used
separately to assess whether within-method subgroup fairness gaps were larger than expected
under random group-label assignment.

3.9

Experimental Setup

All main LLM experiments were conducted using Qwen3-32B as the backbone model. The model
parameters were kept frozen throughout all experiments, and no fine-tuning or parameter updates
were performed. We evaluated four prompting strategies: Baseline Prompt, Fairness-Aware
Prompt, System 2 Prompting, and the proposed Case-Based Prompting (CAP). All strategies used
the same patient input format and structured output format to ensure comparability. Supplementary
evaluations using Qwen3-8B and BioMistral-7B were conducted as lightweight-model robustness
checks and are reported in Appendix B.
The LLM was required to output a structured JSON response containing the predicted inhospital mortality probability, binary prediction, confidence score, key contributing clinical factors,
and a brief reasoning summary. The predicted probability was extracted from the JSON field
probability, and the binary prediction was derived using a threshold of 0.50. Outputs that did not
conform to the predefined JSON format were re-queried once; if the second response remained
invalid, rule-based extraction was applied. The same parsing rules were used for all prompting
strategies.
To provide a conventional structured-data reference, we trained an XGBoost model on the
same training set using the same structured clinical variables. XGBoost was included as a
traditional tabular machine-learning baseline rather than as a direct LLM debiasing method.
The main evaluation was performed on an independent test set. Predictive performance
was assessed using AUROC, AUPRC, F1-score, precision, sensitivity, specificity, and Brier
score. Subgroup fairness and feature-dependence consistency were evaluated as described

4.3

Subgroup Fairness Evaluation with Statistical Validation

To evaluate subgroup-level fairness, we examined model performance across demographic dimensions, including sex, age group, and race/ethnicity. In addition to subgroup-specific AUROC,
we focused on false-negative-rate (FNR) gaps because missed high-risk patients are clinically important in ICU mortality prediction. In this setting, the FNR gap corresponds to Equal Opportunity
Difference (EOD), defined as the absolute difference in FNR between a target subgroup and a
reference subgroup.
Following prior clinical bias-mitigation work, we used an absolute FNR gap of 0.05, corresponding to five percentage points, as a practical reference threshold for potentially meaningful
subgroup disparity [41]. This threshold was used for interpretability and comparison with prior
work, but it was not treated as a statistical significance criterion. Therefore, subgroup fairness
gaps were interpreted together with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals and permutation-based
statistical tests.

4.4

Demographic-Use Retrieval Ablation

To characterize how different case-selection strategies affect predictive performance and subgroup
fairness, we conducted a retrieval-strategy ablation analysis. Five strategies were compared:
(i) Baseline Prompt without retrieval; (ii) Clinical-only Retrieval, which selected cases using
normalized clinical variables only; (iii) Demographic-only Retrieval, which selected cases using
age group, sex, and race/ethnicity only; (iv) Demographic-aware Retrieval, which applied demographic candidate filtering before clinical similarity ranking; and (v) Full CAP, which combined
demographic-aware retrieval with the complete CAP case context, including historical error type
and demographic-sensitivity annotation.
For Demographic-only Retrieval, each repository case received a demographic matching
19

score equal to the number of attributes shared with the query patient across age group, sex,
and race/ethnicity. The case with the highest score was selected, with ties resolved by random
selection using a fixed seed of 42. One case was retrieved for each query patient. No clinical
variables, feature-importance weights, clinical-similarity scores, or threshold penalties were used
in this strategy.
In all retrieval-based strategies, the selected case’s clinical features and known outcome were
provided as auxiliary contextual evidence. Full CAP additionally included historical error type
and demographic-sensitivity annotation. The term “Demographic-only” refers only to the criterion
used for case selection and does not imply that mortality prediction itself was based only on
demographic information. This strategy was included as a diagnostic comparison and was not
part of the proposed CAP framework. In Full CAP, demographic attributes were used only during
first-stage candidate filtering, whereas final case ranking was determined by weighted clinical
similarity and clinical-threshold consistency. White–Asian comparisons were reported separately
in the subgroup fairness analysis but were excluded from the aggregated race gap because of
the relatively small Asian subgroup size. The results are summarized in Table 7.

Trade-off

5

DISCUSSION

5.1

Principal Findings and Interpretation

This study investigated demographic disparities in LLM-based ICU mortality prediction and
evaluated whether a training-free case-based prompting strategy could improve the empirical
balance between predictive performance and subgroup fairness. In the retrospective MIMICIV evaluation, Case-Based Prompting (CAP) improved predictive performance among LLM
prompting strategies and reduced several subgroup fairness gaps compared with the Baseline
Prompt. These improvements were more evident in selected sex- and race-related comparisons,
whereas age-related disparities remained non-negligible. Controlled demographic-information
ablations further showed that the observed fairness improvements could not be explained by
simply removing demographic information, and that different uses of demographic attributes
produced distinct performance–fairness patterns. Therefore, the results should not be interpreted
as evidence that CAP eliminates demographic disparities, but rather as evidence that case-based
contextual prompting can improve the empirical balance between predictive performance and
subgroup fairness under the evaluated setting.
In addition to output-level metrics, we analyzed feature-dependence consistency based on the
key clinical factors generated by the LLM. CAP maintained generally high feature-dependence
consistency across demographic subgroups, although its advantage over other prompting strategies was not uniform across all feature-consistency metrics. This provides descriptive evidence
that case-based prompting did not introduce substantially divergent expressed reasoning patterns
while improving several output-level performance and fairness metrics. However, because these
factors are generated explanations rather than direct internal neural attention weights, they should
be interpreted as observable reasoning outputs rather than definitive evidence of internal model
mechanisms, consistent with broader cautions in the interpretation of post-hoc explanations for
black-box models [42].
The observed improvements may be related to the case-level contextual evidence introduced
by CAP. Unlike generic fairness-aware prompts that provide abstract instructions, CAP supplies
clinically similar historical misprediction and demographic-sensitive cases with known outcomes.
These retrieved cases allow the model to compare the current patient with prior examples in
which baseline predictions were incorrect or sensitive to demographic perturbation, potentially
encouraging reassessment of overestimation or underestimation patterns while grounding the
final prediction in objective clinical evidence.

5.2

Clinical Implications

ICU mortality prediction is a high-stakes task in which both predictive performance and subgroup
fairness are clinically important. False-negative predictions may lead to missed high-risk patients,
whereas false-positive predictions may contribute to unnecessary concern or inefficient resource
allocation. Therefore, subgroup differences in false-negative and false-positive rates should
be examined alongside aggregate discrimination metrics such as AUROC and AUPRC. By
incorporating historical error cases into the prompt, CAP provides a lightweight way to make LLM
predictions more aware of prior failure modes without requiring model retraining.
The proposed framework may be particularly relevant when the underlying LLM is used as
a frozen model and model parameters are not accessible. Compared with retraining-based
debiasing methods, CAP operates at inference time through modifications to prompting and
case retrieval. Importantly, the controlled ablation results suggest that demographic information
should not necessarily be removed indiscriminately, but rather used in a constrained and clinically

25

contextualized manner. Nevertheless, CAP should be viewed as an auxiliary risk-estimation
strategy rather than a standalone clinical decision-making system. Prospective validation, expert
review, continuous monitoring, and integration with clinical governance mechanisms would be
required before real-world deployment.

5.3

Demographic Signals and Shortcut Reasoning

A key methodological consideration in this study is that demographic attributes should not be
automatically interpreted as unfair bias. Age, sex, and race/ethnicity may be associated with
differences in disease epidemiology, comorbidity burden, treatment pathways, social determinants
of health, and documentation patterns. Accordingly, demographic sensitivity alone does not
establish that a model is relying on an unfair shortcut. CAP should therefore not be interpreted as
enforcing demographic blindness or suppressing all demographic information. Instead, its goal is
to constrain unsupported demographic dependence while requiring mortality estimates to remain
grounded in objective clinical evidence.
The controlled demographic-information ablations provide a more direct empirical assessment
of this issue. Masking demographic information from the model input altered both predictive
performance and subgroup fairness rather than producing a uniform improvement across both
dimensions. Similarly, removing demographic filtering during retrieval changed the performance–
fairness profile relative to Full CAP. These findings indicate that demographic information was
neither uniformly beneficial nor uniformly harmful, and that its effect depended on how it was
incorporated into the prediction pipeline. Importantly, simply removing demographic information
did not reproduce the overall performance–fairness profile of Full CAP, suggesting that the
observed fairness improvements were not achieved through demographic suppression alone.
The results are therefore consistent with a fairness–performance trade-off at the point-estimate
level rather than a monotonic benefit from reducing demographic information. However, because
the paired-bootstrap confidence intervals for the mean FNR-gap differences included zero, the
corresponding fairness changes should be interpreted cautiously.
The retrieval ablation provides complementary evidence. Demographic-only Retrieval performed worse than the Baseline Prompt and produced a larger mean FNR gap, indicating that
demographic similarity alone was not sufficient for reliable case selection. In contrast, Full CAP
combined demographic-aware candidate filtering with weighted clinical similarity, historical error
information, and demographic-sensitivity annotations. Thus, demographic attributes in CAP
function as auxiliary contextual and comparability information rather than standalone mortality-risk
evidence. These analyses do not establish whether a demographic association is biologically
or causally justified; instead, they empirically characterize how demographic information affects
prediction and subgroup disparity under controlled conditions. Clinical justification remains
context-dependent and would ultimately require disease-specific evidence and systematic expert
adjudication.

5.4

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the evaluation was conducted retrospectively using
the MIMIC-IV dataset, and external validation on independent ICU cohorts is needed. Second,
the main experiments were conducted using Qwen3-32B, with Qwen3-8B and BioMistral-7B
included only as supplementary lightweight-model analyses; therefore, the generalizability of
CAP across broader LLM families and medical LLMs remains to be established. Third, the case
repository was constructed from baseline misprediction cases that were sensitive to counterfactual
demographic perturbations, and its effectiveness may depend on the quality, representativeness,
26

perturbation threshold, updating strategy, and auxiliary screening model used for repository
construction. Fourth, although the controlled demographic-information ablations quantify how
demographic information affects prediction and subgroup fairness under the evaluated setting,
they cannot determine whether a demographic association is biologically, socially, or causally
justified. Counterfactual demographic sensitivity should therefore be interpreted as a behavioral
auditing signal rather than definitive evidence of unfair bias. Such clinical adjudication would
require disease-specific causal evidence and systematic expert review. Fifth, feature-dependence
analysis was based on key factors generated by the LLM rather than direct internal attention
or hidden representations. Sixth, code status may reflect treatment-limitation decisions and
care-context factors rather than physiological severity alone; therefore, its contribution should
be interpreted cautiously. Finally, this study did not include systematic clinical expert review or
prospective validation, and real-world deployment would require careful consideration of clinical
workflow, data privacy, latency, cost, monitoring, and clinician oversight.

6

CONCLUSION

This study investigated demographic disparities in LLM-based ICU mortality prediction and proposed Case-Based Prompting (CAP), a training-free inference-time framework that incorporates
clinically similar historical misprediction and demographic-sensitive cases into the prompt. In the
retrospective MIMIC-IV evaluation, CAP improved predictive performance among LLM prompting strategies and reduced several subgroup fairness gaps, while feature-dependence analysis
suggested that CAP maintained generally consistent expressed clinical factor patterns across
demographic groups. Controlled demographic-information ablations further showed that the observed fairness improvements were not attributable to simply removing demographic information
and revealed distinct performance–fairness patterns depending on how demographic attributes
were incorporated into prompting and retrieval.
These findings indicate that case-based prompting may help improve the empirical balance
between predictive performance and subgroup fairness in LLM-based clinical prediction, while
constraining demographic information to an auxiliary and clinically contextualized role rather
than using it as direct mortality-risk evidence. However, CAP should be viewed as an auxiliary
risk-estimation strategy rather than a standalone clinical decision-making system.

Additional Fairness Metrics

To complement the primary subgroup fairness analysis based on false-negative-rate (FNR) gaps,
we further evaluated additional fairness-related metrics, including false-positive-rate (FPR) gap,
equalized odds gap, maximum subgroup AUROC difference, subgroup AUPRC gap, and Brier
score gap. The equalized odds gap was defined as the maximum of the FNR gap and FPR gap
for each subgroup comparison. These supplementary metrics were used to assess whether
the observed fairness changes were consistent across classification-error, discrimination, and
calibration-related dimensions. The additional error-rate fairness metrics, maximum subgroup
AUROC differences, and AUPRC/calibration-related subgroup gaps are reported in Tables 13, 14,
and 15, respectively.
Overall, the supplementary fairness metrics showed a pattern broadly consistent with the
primary FNR-gap analysis. CAP reduced several subgroup disparities in FNR gap and equalized
odds gap, and also showed smaller maximum subgroup AUROC differences across the evaluated
demographic dimensions. AUPRC and Brier-score gaps were also reduced for several subgroup
comparisons, although the improvements were not uniform across all metrics. Age-related
disparities remained non-negligible, and the White–Asian comparison showed greater variability
in some supplementary metrics, likely reflecting the relatively small Asian subgroup size. These
supplementary results support the main finding that CAP reduced several subgroup disparities
under the evaluated setting, while demographic disparities were not fully eliminated.
