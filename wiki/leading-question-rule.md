# The leading-question rule

> The one field that regulates question wording by law does not ban suggestive
> questions. It **allocates** them by the answerer's alignment with the asker —
> forbidden when the answerer is friendly, permitted when hostile.

## Definition / Mechanism

Federal Rule of Evidence 611(c), as enacted (Pub. L. 93-595, 1975) and[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s4][^s5]
restyled:

> Leading questions should not be used on direct examination except as
> necessary to develop the witness's testimony. Ordinarily, the court should
> allow leading questions: (1) on cross-examination; and (2) when a party calls
> a hostile witness, an adverse party, or a witness identified with an adverse
> party.

The Advisory Committee's 1972 note gives the reason, and the reason is not
about wording:

> The basis of the objection to leading questions is the supposition that the
> witness is favorable to the examiner and thus needs protection from
> suggestive questions.

Three consequences follow from that sentence.

1. **The regulated object is a pair, not a question.** Suggestive power is
   constant across the two examinations; what changes is whether the answerer
   has a motive to resist it. The same words are a defect on direct and a right
   on cross.
2. **The test is actual alignment, not the formal role.** The Committee says
   "ordinarily" exists to let a court deny leading questions when the
   cross-examination is "in form only and not in fact" — a party questioned by
   his own counsel after being called by the opponent, or a nominally opposed
   witness who is in fact friendly. Symmetrically, "witness identified with an
   adverse party" enlarges the permission to answerers who merely *lean* the
   other way.
3. **No accuracy claim is made.** The rule is about admissibility. Its premise
   is that a friendly answerer will absorb the suggestion; it does not assert
   that an adverse one will not.

That last premise is where the psychology contradicts the law. Loftus & Palmer (1974) changed one verb in a post-event question ("smashed" for "hit") and moved
both speed estimates and the rate of reporting broken glass that was never in
the film — the friendly-answerer case, as predicted. But Zajac & Hayne (2003)
put 5–6-year-olds through cross-examination-style questioning and found they
changed previously *correct* answers as readily as incorrect ones; Valentine & Maras (2011) found the same pattern in adults cross-examined by a trainee
barrister. The adversarial half of the allocation is not protective; it is
destructive in the other direction. Wigmore's claim that cross-examination is
"the greatest legal engine ever invented for the discovery of truth", quoted by
the Supreme Court in *California v. Green* (1970), has no support in that
evidence.

## Why it matters

- **It supplies the variable the wiki's formulation notes leave out.**
  [[survey-response-model]] and [[presupposition-accommodation]] treat
  suggestive wording as uniformly bad. The law says its cost depends on a
  property of the *source*, which is why the same phrasing can be contamination
  or a tool.
- **It sharpens a live disagreement.** [[tacit-knowledge]] prescribes offering a
  deliberately wrong draft as bait. Under 611(c) that is a leading question, and
  it is licensed only against an answerer with a reason to resist. An agent's
  sub-agent, tool, or an instruction-following model is the paradigm friendly
  witness; a red team ([[dialectic-red-team]]) is the paradigm hostile one.
- **It gives a pre-ask check.** Before a suggestive phrasing: is this answerer
  adverse in fact, not merely in form? If no, the reply is partly the draft
  ([[constraint-as-information]]), and the ethnographic warning in
  [[grand-tour-question]] applies.

## Transfer

| | |
|---|---|
| **Home field** | adversarial trial: two opposed counsel, a witness under oath, a judge with discretion over each question, and a later chance to re-examine |
| **Here** | one agent formulating one question to a user, tool, or sub-agent whose interests it does not observe |
| **What survives** | the allocation rule (permission to lead is a function of the answerer's alignment, not of the question's form) and the in-form-only test (check actual alignment, not the nominal role) |
| **What does not** | the judge — no third party rules a question inadmissible, so nothing external stops an agent leading itself; the oath and perjury penalty that give a hostile witness a reason to resist; and the opposing counsel whose re-examination is the law's repair for a bad cross |

## Limits

- **The rule allocates a category it does not define.** Nothing in FRE 611 says
  what makes a question leading; the classification is left to the trial court's
  discretion. No operational classifier can be imported from it.
- **The rationale is a policy premise, not a finding.** "A friendly witness
  needs protection" was asserted by the Advisory Committee, not measured. The
  measured results (zajac-2003, valentine-2011) undercut the other half of the
  same premise.
- **The accuracy evidence is staged-event memory.** Films of car crashes and a
  visit to a police station, with a fact of the matter fixed in advance. Whether
  a designer asked a leading question about their own unformed intentions is
  equally malleable is untested by any source here.
- **Small and analogue.** Valentine & Maras (2011) ran 22 student witnesses with
  one trainee barrister; Zajac & Hayne (2003) used a cross-examination
  *analogue*, not a trial.
- **That an LLM answerer is a "friendly witness" is this wiki's inference.** No
  source cited here measures alignment in a model answerer.
