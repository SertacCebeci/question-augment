# Gradient-cluster mode discovery

Mode-balanced sampling takes the modes as given: the work's exposition assumes them known in advance, with each training trajectory tagged by its mode.[^g1] When no tags exist, the work asks whether the modes can be found in the data itself. Its method treats the training example as a point in gradient space. For each example, the gradient of the model's log-probability of the example's response is computed, and the gradients are reduced with a random projection, "to each gradient vector," in the words of the reduction step.[^g2] The projected vectors are then clustered, and "all samples in the same cluster belong to one mode," after which mode-conditioned training is applied as if the clusters were labels.[^g3] Gradient similarity has earlier uses in understanding training dynamics and in choosing data, which the work cites as its inspiration.[^g4]

The check on known data is the strongest evidence. On a multi-teacher distillation set, where the teacher of each example is known, clustering the gradients of a 1.5B base model "achieves 98.7% F1 score in recovering teacher assignments."[^g5] Training with those clusters gave "nearly identical test-time scaling benefits as using true teacher labels."[^g6] The mechanism here is simple: examples generated by the same teacher share a style, and that style moves the gradient in a shared direction. The work reads this as evidence that "gradient patterns effectively capture the underlying modes" — its own conclusion, from one dataset with one kind of mode.

The stronger claim is on data with no known modes. The work applies the same clustering to NuminaMath, where "we lack a clear sense of what modes exist," and reports that ModC on the discovered clusters gives "10% gains over standard training on the NuminaMath dataset with no additional side information."[^g7] The gain is measured against standard training. Nothing in the sections read checks that the clusters correspond to reasoning strategies, so the "meaningful" in the conclusion, "can automatedly uncover meaningful specializations," is a reading of the gain rather than a separate measurement.[^g8]

## Limits

- The only validation with ground truth is a mixture of two teachers. The claim that clusters match modes rests there; a dataset with several overlapping strategies from one teacher was not tested in the sections read.
- The number of clusters C is a free choice in the method. The sections read do not say how C was set for NuminaMath, and the result may depend on it. This is a gap in what was read, not a stated result.
- The gradients come from one base model (1.5B in the validation). Which clusters appear depends on the model used to compute them; the paper does not compare base models in the sections read. My inference.
- The random projection reduces dimension and so approximates the pairwise geometry of the full gradients. The work follows earlier papers in using it and does not measure the approximation error here.
- Gains on NuminaMath are shown against standard training, not against a clustering baseline such as clustering on surface features. Whether gradient clusters beat cheaper clusterings is not established in the parts read.
- The method is a training-time tool. It labels data before fine-tuning; it does not tell the model which mode to sample at test time, which still needs the prefix or specialist machinery of [[mode-balanced-sampling]].
