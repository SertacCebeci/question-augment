Summary
This is the fourth in a series of short reports that help business, education, and policy leaders
understand the technical details of working with AI through rigorous testing. Here, we ask
whether

assigning

personas to models improves performance on difficult objective

multiple-choice questions. We study both domain-specific expert personas and low-knowledge
personas, evaluating six models on GPQA Diamond (Rein et al. 2024) and MMLU-Pro (Wang et
al. 2024), graduate-level questions spanning science, engineering, and law.

We tested three approaches:
●​ In-Domain Experts: Assigning the model an expert persona (“you are a physics expert”)
matched to the problem type (physics problems) had no significant impact on
performance (with the exception of the Gemini 2.0 Flash model).
●​ Off-Domain Experts (Domain-Mismatched): Assigning the model an expert persona
(“you are a physics expert”) not matched to the problem type (law problems) resulted in
marginal differences.
●​ Low-Knowledge Personas: We assigned the model negative capability personas
(layperson, young child, toddler), which were generally harmful to benchmark accuracy.
Across both benchmarks, persona prompts generally did not improve accuracy relative to a
no-persona baseline. Expert personas showed no consistent benefit across models, with few
exceptions.

Domain-mismatched

expert

personas

sometimes

degraded

performance.

Low-knowledge personas often reduced accuracy. These results are about the accuracy of
answers only; personas may serve other purposes (such as altering the tone of outputs),
beyond improving factual performance.

Why Persona Prompts?​
The official documentation for many AI models recommends persona prompting, where the AI is
assigned a specific role (“act like a”). For example, Google’s Vertex AI prompt-design guide
says to “Assign a role” as a best practice and provides a sample template that begins, “You are
a [persona, such as a ‘math teacher’ or ‘automotive expert’].” (Google Cloud 2024) Anthropic’s
samples include, “You are an expert AI tax analyst. You help users understand the details of the
tax code.” (Anthropic 2024). OpenAI’s developer materials take a similar approach and include
“You are a world-class Python developer… (Sanders 2022).
Persona prompts may serve many purposes (such as making an AI respond in a particular style
or simulate a particular viewpoint), but many guides suggest that they may improve the quality
of output when models are given objective questions to answer. That is, being told to “act like a
physics professor” would improve the accuracy of a model when given physics questions, with
the underlying logic that these personas are statistically associated with approaches that are
more likely to yield the correct answer. Independent research on performance gains from
persona-based prompting is rare and inconsistent. One study showed improvements on
benchmarks (Kong et al. 2024). In another, adding personas to system prompts did not reliably
improve accuracy on factual questions (Zheng et al. 2024). We focus on hard factual questions
in order to shed additional light on this common practice.

How We Benchmark the AI
For our two benchmarks, we selected the commonly used GPQA Diamond (Graduate-Level
Google-Proof Q&A Benchmark, Rein et al. 2024) and MMLU-Pro (Wang et al. 2024). The GPQA
Diamond set comprises 198 multiple-choice PhD-level questions across biology, physics, and
chemistry. This is a challenging test: PhDs in the corresponding domains reach 65% accuracy
(74% when discounting clear mistakes the experts identified in retrospect), while highly skilled
non-expert validators only reach 34% accuracy, despite spending on average over 30 minutes
with unrestricted access to the web (Rein et al. 2024). MMLU-Pro provides another challenging
benchmark with similarly difficult questions but with 10 potential answers per question, lowering
the baseline performance achieved via pure guessing. For MMLU-Pro, we selected a subset of
300 questions: 100 from the dataset’s engineering category, 100 from its law category, and 100
from its chemistry category. These domains were selected to provide diversity for the domain
analysis and because they exhibit slightly lower benchmark scores than other categories,
allowing for greater potential gains.
We collected 25 independent responses per question for each model-prompt condition to
account for variability in LLM outputs (Miller 2024). We followed our previous work (Meincke et
al. 2025b), which showed that beyond 25 trials per question, additional trials (e.g., 100) do not

meaningfully improve accuracy or statistical power. We specifically looked at a variety of
models, including both reasoning and non-reasoning models: GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, o3-mini,
o4-mini, Gemini 2.0 Flash, and Gemini 2.5 Flash. Though specific models may vary by
performance, we are interested in the variability within models based on prompting techniques,
making specific model choices less critical.
This design yields 4,950 runs per model-prompt pair for GPQA Diamond (198 questions) and
7,500 runs per model-prompt pair for the MMLU-Pro subset (300 questions). Our primary
dependent variable, Average Rating, captures average model accuracy: it is the fraction of
questions the model answers correctly, averaged over 25 independent runs for each
model-prompt pair. We report additional metrics in the appendix, where we use stricter
thresholds, requiring the AI to answer correctly on all trials (25/25, see Figure S1), in 90% of
cases (23/25, see Figure S2), and in the majority of cases (13/25, see Figure S3).
We generally do not consider the more commonly used PASS@N (passing when one of N
answers is correct) and CONSENSUS (passing when the modal answer is correct) metrics
useful for benchmarking real-world applications.

How We Prompted the AI
We followed the GPQA evaluation protocol from Meincke et al. (2025b) and used the same
generic system prompt1 for all conditions in both benchmarks. For each trial, the user prompt
consisted of the persona text (when present) followed by the question and answer options in a
fixed template. In the Baseline condition, the user prompt was simply the question text, followed
by multiple choice options (four for GPQA and ten for MMLU-Pro) and a short instruction on how
to format the answer (example below shows GPQA baseline prompt):

[Potential Persona Manipulation]
What is the correct answer to this question:
[question text]
Choices:
A. [option A]
B. [option B]
C. [option C]
D. [option D]

We used a relatively high sampling temperature (1.0), together with 25 independent samples
per question per model-prompt pair, to obtain a more reliable central tendency (Wang et al.
2023) as well as more closely mirror real-world scenarios2. Unlike many reported MMLU-Pro
scores, we used zero-shot instead of few-shot prompting. We chose this method for two primary
reasons. First, it more closely mirrors how most users interact with AI in real-world scenarios,
where they typically ask questions directly without providing structured examples. Second, this
zero-shot approach ensures that our results measure the specific impact of the persona, rather
than the combined effect of the persona and the content of the provided examples.
For both datasets, we compared the effect of persona prompts on the overall benchmark
performance. In addition, we considered the impact of domain-specific personas. GPQA
Diamond is organized into three domains (Physics, Chemistry, and Biology), and we selected
three subdomains from MMLU-Pro (Engineering, Law, and Chemistry) for our domain analysis.
For each question, we assigned a domain-specific expert persona prompt that matched the
question domain (e.g., a physics professor answers a physics question). In addition, we
compared the performance of expert persona prompts from domains adjacent to the question
domain (for instance, math is adjacent to physics) and an unrelated expert (e.g., a physics
professor answering a biology question, see Table S4 for all domain relations). We chose more
elaborate persona prompts over simple prompts such as “You’re a professor of” to attempt to
make the model engage more deeply with frameworks and concepts used by real-world experts
in the domain. In total, we compared the following prompts (see Table S1 for example
questions):

Results
Prompt Analysis
Across both benchmarks, persona prompts usually had little to no effect on factual accuracy. For
a given model and dataset, most persona conditions produced performance that was
statistically indistinguishable from the Baseline (no persona) condition. When we did observe
significant changes, they were more often negative than positive: low-knowledge personas
tended to reduce accuracy in several models.
On the GPQA Diamond dataset, we did not find any expert or low-knowledge persona that
reliably improved performance over the baseline for any model (see Figure 1). The only
significant positive effect was a small gain for the “Young Child” prompt on Gemini 2.5 Flash (RD
= 0.098 [0.029, 0.164], p = 0.005; see Table S2 for more details), which appears to be a
model-specific quirk. In contrast, several low-knowledge personas reduced accuracy: on
o4-mini, all three low-knowledge personas (“Toddler,” “Young Child,” and “Layperson”)
performed worse than the baseline, and on GPT-4o the “Toddler” persona produced a
statistically significant negative difference.
On the MMLU-Pro subset, the pattern is similar. For five of the six models tested, no expert
persona showed a positive statistically significant improvement relative to the baseline, and we
observed nine statistically significant negative differences. Gemini 2.0 Flash is the main
exception: for this model, the five expert personas yielded modest positive differences relative to
the baseline (e.g., Engineering Expert vs. baseline RD = 0.089 [0.033, 0.148], p = 0.002), while
the “Toddler” and “Layperson” personas worsened results (see Table S3 for more details).

However, certain prompting variations consistently worsened performance: in four of the six
models tested, the “Toddler” prompt reduced performance.
Within the performance-reducing prompts, the degree of (lack of) knowledge implied by the
prompt persona type affected how poorly the models performed. Specifically, for five of the six

models tested on the MMLU-Pro dataset, the Toddler prompt was significantly worse than the
Layperson prompt, except for GPT-4o-mini, which had no statistically significant differences
between any pairs of prompting variations. Additionally, four of the six models (excluding
GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4o) yielded worse results in the Toddler prompt compared to the Young
Child prompt.
Correctness Thresholds
Results are overall comparable for both benchmarks when applying correctness thresholds, and
in general, the effects tend to be more pronounced at the higher threshold levels. We report
results for the 100%, 90% and 51% thresholds in Figures S1, S2, and S3.
Domain-Specific Persona Analysis
We next asked whether aligning expert personas with the question domain improves
performance (see Figure 2). For each dataset, we compared three persona types: in-domain
experts, domain-adjacent experts, and domain-unrelated experts (see How We Prompted the AI
and Table S4 for definitions and mappings).
We found that domain-tailored personas do not generally improve performance. Using the
GPQA Diamond dataset, there were no positive significant differences between the baseline
and any of the three prompting variations (see Table S5). Using the MMLU-Pro domain subset,
there were significant improvements for two models. Specifically, Gemini 2.0 Flash improved in
the Engineering and Chemistry domains for all three variations and GPT-4o improved in the Law
domain when comparing baseline to the in-domain expert (see Table S6). The Gemini 2.0 Flash
improvements may be outliers, as another model from the same family, Gemini 2.5 Flash,
exhibited no significant positive differences.

Discussion and Conclusion
Our findings indicate that, for difficult factual questions of the kind captured by GPQA Diamond
and MMLU-Pro, assigning an expert persona is not a reliable way to improve accuracy. Across
six models and two benchmarks, persona prompts leave performance unchanged relative to a
no-persona baseline in most cases. When persona prompts do matter, they are more likely to
reduce accuracy than to increase it.
The main exception is Gemini 2.0 Flash on MMLU-Pro, which shows improvements for all five
expert personas overall and, at the domain level, in Engineering and Chemistry; GPT-4o also
shows a small in-domain gain in Law. In contrast, low-knowledge personas (especially “Toddler”)
often reduce accuracy, with “Layperson” effects varying by model (harmful in several cases, but
not all). Overall, tailoring personas to match the question domain shows no consistent benefit

across models, with the limited improvements above appearing model and question-specific
rather than generalizable.
A notable failure mode emerges from the domain-mismatched expert conditions in the Gemini
Flash family. When instructed with an out-of-domain expert persona, Gemini 2.5 Flash
frequently declines to answer questions at all. Under the Unrelated Expert condition on GPQA
Diamond, the model refuses to answer an average of 10.56 out of the 25 trials per question,
typically asserting that it lacks the relevant expertise or “cannot, in good conscience” select an
answer. Gemini 2.0 Flash exhibits similar, but less extreme, behavior in some Physics and
Chemistry conditions. This refusal-driven behavior substantially depresses measured accuracy
and illustrates a broader risk: role instructions that are too narrow can cause models to
under-utilize their actual knowledge.
It is important to note that these findings focus on accuracy and personas may serve additional
purposes. For example, personas may change what factors the AI focuses on and how it
approaches reasoning about a problem. Personas may shift what the AI prioritizes (emphasizing
regulatory concerns for compliance officers vs. market opportunities for business developers)
and how it expresses outcomes (confident expert vs. cautious advisor). Personas certainly play
a role in shaping the tone and presentation of output (OpenAI 2025a). Beyond this, personas
may serve a dual function as a prompting aid for users. Naming a specific role or perspective
may help cue the user and help them better articulate and frame questions.
This study has several limitations, including testing only a subset of available models, focusing
on academic benchmarks that may not reflect all real-world use cases, and examining a specific
set of personas across a limited number of domains within those academic benchmarks. The
consistent null results across these models and two benchmarks suggest that the strategies of
assigning a persona when prompting models may be ineffective. When working on specific
problems, testing multiple prompt variations may still be worthwhile given the question-level
variability we have observed in previous work, but practitioners should be prepared for
unpredictable results and should not expect prompting variations to provide consistent benefits.
In practice, this suggests that organizations may get more value from iterating on task-specific
instructions, examples, or evaluation workflows than from simply adding expert personas to
prompts.

Supplementary Information
In addition to the Average Rating metric, we report the following:
●​ 100% Correct: The pass rate for a trial with the requirement that all answers are correct
on all trials (25/25) of a question to count that question as successful. This is our strictest
condition for tasks where there is no room for errors.
●​ 90% Correct: We relax the 100% condition and allow a few errors per question, but
23/25 answers must be correct. This might be comparable to human acceptable error
rates.
●​ 51% Correct: A simple majority vote where AI needs to get at least 13/25 answers on a
question correct.
