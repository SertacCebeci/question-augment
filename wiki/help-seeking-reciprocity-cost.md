# Help-seeking reciprocity cost

Asking is usually studied as a gain to the asker. In a field study of 291 employees in one division of a multinational, seeking help from teammates during creative problem solving predicted the seeker's own creativity: a multilevel model with controls gave a positive coefficient, and help seeking alone accounted for about 20% of variance.[^c1] The proposed reason is that help seeking widens the divergent information a person can combine, and that it requires treating the earlier framing of the problem as flawed (the paper's argument, not separately measured).

## The cost side

Asking creates a debt. Peer relationships follow norms of roughly equal exchange, so a seeker who never gives risks unfilled requests. In the data, seeking help was strongly and positively related to giving help.[^c2] Giving help in turn was negatively related to the giver's own creativity, even controlling for help received.[^c3] The paper offers two mechanisms for this, neither tested directly. One is lost time and energy. The other is that helping reinforces the helper's own problem representation, because the helper sees the asker as less competent and so does not absorb the asker's perspective.

## Net effect

Help giving moderated the benefit. The help seeking by help giving interaction was negative, and the slope of creativity on seeking was steepest when giving was low (.89) and flattest when giving was high (.56), yet positive at every level.[^c4] Asking therefore stayed net-positive; its benefit was reduced, not reversed.[^c5] The same pattern held for the indirect path from intrinsic motivation through help seeking to creativity, which was about 10% weaker for heavy givers.[^c6] Intrinsic motivation also predicted help seeking, and help seeking partially mediated its effect on creativity.

## Limits

- One cross-sectional field sample from a single organization, with self-reported or rated measures; causality is not established, and heavy askers may simply be more engaged.
- The mechanisms for the cost of giving (time loss, validated worldview) are argued, not measured.
- The moderation effect is modest (about 10% on the indirect effect), and it does not show the cost ever outweighs the gain.
- Concerns peer help in teams on creative tasks. It says nothing about asking an AI system or asking in routine tasks, where reciprocity does not apply.
- The reciprocation is inferred from correlation of seeking and giving, not from tracked exchanges.
