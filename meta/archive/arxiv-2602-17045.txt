Abstract
A growing body of work attempts to evaluate the theory of mind (ToM)
abilities of humans and large language models (LLMs) using static, noninteractive question-and-answer benchmarks. However, theoretical work in
the field suggests that first-personal interaction is a crucial part of ToM and
that such predictive, spectatorial tasks may fail to evaluate it. We address
this gap with a novel ToM task that requires an agent to persuade a target to
choose one of three policy proposals by strategically revealing information.
Success depends on a persuader’s sensitivity to a given target’s knowledge
states (what the target knows about the policies) and motivational states
(how much the target values different outcomes). We varied whether
these states were R EVEALED to persuaders or H IDDEN, in which case
persuaders had to inquire about or infer them. In Experiment 1, participants
persuaded a bot programmed to make only rational inferences. OpenAI’s
reasoning model o3 excelled in the R EVEALED condition but performed
no better than chance in the H IDDEN condition, suggesting difficulty with
the multi-step planning required to elicit and use mental state information.
Humans performed moderately well in both conditions, indicating an
ability to engage such planning. In Experiment 2, where a human target
role-played the bot, and in Experiment 3, where we measured whether
human targets’ real beliefs changed, o3 outperformed human persuaders,
significantly so in the R EVEALED conditions. These results suggest that
effective persuasion can occur without explicit ToM reasoning (e.g., through
rhetorical strategies) and that o3 excels at this form of persuasion. Overall,
our results caution against attributing human-like PToM to LLMs based on
predictive, spectatorial benchmarks while highlighting their potential to
influence people’s beliefs and behavior.
Keywords: theory of mind; planning; large language models; persuasion;
causal reasoning; mentalizing; social interaction

Introduction

Planning with Theory of Mind
Theory of Mind (ToM) describes the ability of an agent to understand behaviors of other
agents in terms of underlying mental states. This ability plays a crucial role in human social
interaction. Impressed with LLM performance on various benchmark tests for ToM, some
researchers suggest that LLMs may exhibit human-like reasoning about mental states. This
suggestion raises two questions. First, what constitutes human-like ToM? And second, how
do we best investigate this capacity in humans and LLMs?
ToM is often studied via behavioral prediction measures (Wimmer and Perner, 1983; BaronCohen et al., 1985; Wellman and Liu, 2004; Peterson et al., 2012). Classic ToM tasks have
participants predict the outcome of a story given some initial information about a character’s
mental states. In the false belief task, a character, Sally, places her marble in a basket. Where
will Sally look for the marble if she put it in the basket but did not see it get moved to a box
(Wimmer and Perner, 1983; Baron-Cohen et al., 1985)? Success in this task only requires
predicting Sally’s behavior, not a causal understanding how her beliefs generate her behavior;
a participant may succeed using only a heuristic such as ‘people look for things where they
last saw them’ without attributing any beliefs (Ho et al., 2022; Tamir and Thornton, 2018).
In addition, these tasks are spectatorial: participants predict the outcome of situations that
they are not themselves part of. This is the case for a range of common ToM tasks (Wimmer
and Perner, 1983; Baron-Cohen et al., 1985; Happé, 1994; Wellman and Liu, 2004; Peterson
et al., 2012).
Human ToM is not exhaustively described by predictive and spectatorial functions. Classic
accounts, such as the theory-theory, emphasize that ToM also supports explanation and
understanding of behaviors through a causal model of the relationship between actions and
mental states (Gopnik and Wellman, 1992; Gopnik and Meltzoff, 1997; Gopnik and Wellman,
2012). Moreover, many researchers highlight that the most natural context for ToM is in
active interaction with other agents (Hutto, 2012; Schilbach et al., 2013). More recent work
emphasizes planning and intervention as key elements of human ToM (Ho et al., 2022; Wu
et al., 2024; Slater et al., 2026). A causal understanding of the mind allows us to plan and
carry out interventions on other people’s mental states, e.g. as in persuasion. After Ho et al.
(2022), we call this capacity Planning Theory of Mind (PToM).
PToM captures both causal understanding and social interaction. To carry out an effective
intervention, one must be engaged in an interaction with an interlocutor one can intervene
on, and one must have a causal understanding of how the intervened-upon mental states
can bring about a desired behavior (Ho et al., 2022). In this paper, we use causal ToM to refer
to understanding mental states as causes of behavior, associative ToM to refer to predictive
mappings from mental state cues to behavior, and PToM to refer to using causal ToM in
interaction to plan interventions.

Causal and interactive ToM measures
In response to the limitations of classic predictive and spectatorial ToM tasks, researchers in
developmental and comparative psychology have developed several interactive adaptations
of classic tasks. Call and Tomasello (1999) developed a false belief task where a child
must not only rely on a character for cues about the location of a hidden reward, but
must also recognize when the character’s cues are unreliable: when the character could
not know the reward’s true location. In the active helping paradigm, the child must help
an experimenter access the toy by e.g., unlocking one of two boxes that the character is
struggling to open (Buttelmann et al., 2014). Similar interactive designs exist in comparative
psychology: the active helping paradigm has been adapted for great apes (Buttelmann et al.,
2017), and competitive games assess understanding of false beliefs in both chimpanzees and
children (Kaminski et al., 2008). Still, while interactive, these tasks do not target a causal
understanding of mental states.
2

Other paradigms integrate robust measures of causal understanding, but lack an interactive
component. The strange stories task measures childrens’ causal understanding of mental
states (Happé, 1994). Participants must explain why characters behaved as they did in
complicated social scenarios (e.g., from bluffs to sarcasm). To succeed, participants must
cite mental states as reasons or causes for behaviors. Similarly, the short stories task asks
adult participants to explain nuanced character behaviors in a story in terms of mental states
(Dodell-Feder et al., 2013). These tasks highlight the explanatory role of ToM and, in turn,
the causal understanding it involves. Nevertheless, they involve spectatorial contexts—not
interaction.
Persuasion paradigms in developmental psychology most clearly (1) measure a causal
understanding of mind in (2) an interactive context. In these tasks, children must select an
argument to persuade a target in a way that is sensitive to the targets beliefs and preferences
(Peterson et al., 2018; Bartsch and London, 2000; Bartsch et al., 2011). Good persuasion
skills plausibly require a causal model of the target, which the persuader can use to ask
questions such as “if I say X, what impact will that have on my target?” For example, if you
are trying to persuade someone to adopt a vegan diet, it might be valuable to know whether
they value animal welfare, are concerned about the environment, or dedicate lots of time to
cooking. A persuader could inquire about or infer these values and adapt their persuasion
strategy accordingly. Hence participants must tailor their interventions to the preferences of
the target to succeed. This intervention provides evidence of an underlying causal model
because the desired action (adopting a vegan diet) can be generated by intervening on the
beliefs or desires that jointly cause behaviors. Developmental persuasion paradigms tap
this causal understanding in interactive contexts. A clear limitation of these designs is that
they are circumscribed, often relying on forced choices between few options; given this,
they are too simple to illustrate meaningful differences in adult populations.

LLM performance on ToM tasks
A growing body of work has evaluated Large Language Models (LLMs) on ToM tasks, investigating the extent to which LLMs’ human-like behavior extends to classic psychological
tasks (Binz and Schulz, 2023; Binz et al., 2025). Some studies have focused on measuring the
capabilities of LLMs, treating ToM tasks as benchmarks for social reasoning (Sap et al., 2022;
Hu et al., 2025; Kosinski, 2024). Other studies have used LLMs as explicit computational
operationalizations of theories of human cognition and tested whether they can explain
human behavior (Trott et al., 2023; Binz et al., 2025).
While research on animal, child, and adult-human ToM has taken steps to address the
limitations of predictive and spectatorial tasks, research on LLMs has tended to focus
primarily on predictive, spectatorial measures. Early work established that LLMs can
produce correct answers in text-based versions of the false belief task (Trott et al., 2023;
Kosinski, 2024). However, later work suggested that LLMs showed inconsistent performance
on slightly modified versions of these stimuli, suggesting that their responses were not
based on robust causal models (Ullman, 2023; Rocca et al., 2026; Rooy, 2026). Many papers
have now assessed LLMs on batteries of ToM tasks reframed as “benchmarks”, including:
tracking the beliefs of multiple agents (Kim et al., 2023), tracking beliefs recursively (Jones
et al., 2024; Street et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024), detecting social norm violations (Strachan
et al., 2024), and interpreting non-literal language (Jones et al., 2023). However, these tasks
are largely predictive and spectatorial.
Some studies have used tasks that provide richer evidence for a causal model. Gandhi
et al. (2023) tested whether LLMs can infer beliefs “backward” from actions, rather than
“forward” from world-states to actions or beliefs. Yu et al. (2025) and Chan et al. (2024), used
pre-existing dialogues to test whether LLMs can predict how participants will behave and
respond in real persuasion and negotiation contexts. Jones et al. (2023) evaluated LLMs on
the short story and strange stories tasks. While these tasks make steps toward requiring
inferences about the mental causes of behavior, they remain fundamentally spectatorial:
they focus on passively inferring the beliefs of other agents rather than interacting with them.
To our knowledge, no research has tested LLMs’ PToM ability by having them interact over

Persuasion measures in LLMs
One of the most promising directions for measuring the causal and interactive PToM capacity
could be measuring persuasion ability. An emerging subfield tries to measure the extent
to which conversations with LLMs can change people’s beliefs (Rogiers et al., 2024; Jones
and Bergen, 2024; Bozdag et al., 2025). Although these tasks were not motivated as ToM
measures, but rather by the intrinsic benefits and risks of measuring the persuasion abilities
of LLMs (Jones and Bergen, 2024; El-Sayed et al., 2024), they nevertheless provide important
evidence of the abilities of models to influence people’s mental states.
These persuasion studies typically measure participants’ beliefs before and after interacting
with an LLM (Rogiers et al., 2024). Research suggests LLMs can alter people’s beliefs
and behavior toward policy issues (Salvi et al., 2024; Hackenburg et al., 2025; Potter et al.,
2024), factual questions (Schoenegger et al., 2025), conspiracy theories (Costello et al., 2024),
consumer choices (Werner et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024), charitable donations (Phuong et al.,
2024), and business decisions (Benton et al., 2024). Some studies have also found that LLMs
are more effective at persuading human participants than are other humans (Salvi et al.,
2024; Schoenegger et al., 2025).
However, while these results are impressive, they do not provide good estimates of ToM
abilities in general, nor PToM abilities in particular. Open-ended persuasion tasks allow
multiple paths to success that do not necessarily require sensitivity to interlocutors’ mental
states. LLMs may succeed through memorized persuasive arguments, classical rhetorical
techniques, or simply by providing a target with novel facts or statistics. Without experimental control over what information the persuader has access to—and thus what mental states
they could be modeling—existing tasks cannot distinguish between persuasion through
strategic mental state modeling versus persuasion through memorized rhetoric and generic
strategies.
The Present Studies
We address the gaps in these areas by extending the MindGames advanced PToM task
into a flexible task framework (Authors, 2025). Our task involves persuaders interacting with another agent (the “target”) through an online conversation game. Persuaders
must convince targets to choose the persuader’s preferred policy proposal—out of three
proposals—by strategically revealing information about the proposals. Each proposal affects
three attributes differently. For example, in a scenario about AI policy, each proposal might
increase or decrease one or all of safety and control of LLMs, development speed, and public
trust in LLMs. Importantly, persuaders and targets have different value functions over the
attributes—what the persuader favors may be neutral or unfavorable to the target.
Additional example dialogues and figures appear in the SI, including cross-experiment
conversation excerpts (SI Fig. S6).
In our task, the persuader always has complete information about all proposals, while the
target initially has partial information. This information asymmetry means persuaders can
make their preferred proposal attractive to the target by selectively disclosing favorable
information and, at the same time, they can retain other information that would make other
proposals more attractive. Success in the task depends on understanding both what the
target knows (informational states) and what they value (motivational states) and using this
understanding to plan interventions (in the form of disclosures).
We manipulate PToM demands through two conditions. In R EVEALED, persuaders can see
the target’s value function and existing knowledge of the proposals. In H IDDEN, persuaders
must infer these mental states through dialogue. While persuaders still must interact with
the target in R EVEALED, H IDDEN requires more complex counterfactual planning (reflecting
PToM (Ho et al., 2022)). In H IDDEN, persuaders must first inquire about the target’s mental

We use this framework to address a tradeoff between internal and external validity that has
plagued existing work on ToM. On one hand, tightly controlled studies, like those based on
the false belief task, allow clear inferences about specific cognitive capacities, but may poorly
reflect real-world use of such capacities. Conversely, externally valid tasks like open-ended
persuasion capture real-world complexity, but confound multiple factors, making it unclear
whether ToM or other abilities underlie performance.
We address this tradeoff by conducting three experiments that progressively sacrifice internal
for external validity. Experiment 1 maximizes internal validity by using a rational bot
as the target that responds only to appeals to its mental states, ensuring success depends
on PToM (§2). Experiment 2 introduces human targets role-playing assigned values,
introducing the potential for rhetorical and other strategies (§3). Finally, Experiment 3
allows human targets to act on their own values, in order to approximate real persuasion
where targets’ preferences are mutable (§4). Through these experiments we test both whether
the state-of-the-art reasoning model o3 possesses PToM abilities and whether such abilities
matter for naturalistic persuasion.
Our argument has three parts. First, the claim that o3 shows no evidence of PToM rests
primarily on Experiment 1 H IDDEN, where success requires PToM and o3 performs no
better than chance. Second, the claim that o3 can nevertheless be an effective persuader
without PToM rests primarily on Experiment 2, where o3 persuades human targets despite
lacking evidence of PToM. Third, Experiment 3 extends this pattern to a more naturalistic
but noisier setting.

Our contributions are as follows:
1. We introduce a family of experimental designs that trade off internal for external
validity to systematically explore the relationship between PToM and persuasion.
2. We conduct a comparison of adult human and o3 abilities across experimental conditions requiring different levels of mental state reasoning, addressing limitations
of prior evaluations of LLMs on ToM tasks.
3. We demonstrate that while o3 shows no evidence of human-like PToM abilities on
controlled tasks (Experiment 1, H IDDEN; Fig. 2), it can nevertheless be a highly effective persuader of human targets (Experiments 2 and 3; Figs. 3 and 5), suggesting
that PToM and persuasion success are dissociable.
4. We provide evidence that persuader performance is driven partly by properties of
human targets, such as cooperativeness and susceptibility to rhetoric, which creates
opportunities for persuasion success without PToM (§5.3).
5. We argue this comparison suggests that humans and o3 have different ToM styles—
causal ToM and associative ToM, respectively (§5.2).

Experiment 1: Rational Bot Persuasion

In Experiment 1, we sought to maximize internal validity over the target’s mental states,
robustly measuring PToM. To do so, the design screened off non-PToM persuasion strategies.
First, by randomly varying the target’s informational and motivational state, we ensured
a dynamic target that was unlikely to be persuaded by one general persuasion strategy.
Second, we designed the target of persuasion to be impervious to persuasion strategies
that do not rely on planned interventions on mental states. We accomplished this by
implementing the target as a “naively rational” automated bot. This target was assigned
a value function that specified how it valued different policies and was designed to only
change its preferred proposal if its value function was better satisfied by the information
revealed by the persuader. This meant that the target was immune to any appeal that was
not an intervention on its informational states. These two constraints ensure that success
in this experiment depends on the persuader’s ability to gather information about mental
states through interaction with the target, and then to use that information in interventions.
The R EVEALED and H IDDEN conditions allow us to contrast different PToM demands. By
design, a persuader can be successful in the R EVEALED condition with a single message
explaining that the target’s preferred proposal has drawbacks that do not align with the
target’s value function, and that the persuader’s preferred proposal has advantages that
align with the target’s value function.
Although a basic level of planned intervention is needed to do well in the R EVEALED
condition, the H IDDEN condition is far more demanding. Because the persuader does not
have full information about the target’s value function, they must seek out this information
and monitor when they have enough to intervene. This means that they must evaluate
whether their causal model of the interlocutor’s informational and motivational states is
sufficiently detailed for successful intervention.
An agent that performs well on the H IDDEN condition shows clear evidence of PToM. An
agent that only performs well on the R EVEALED condition displays only a limited capacity
for PToM. For this reason, we treat above-chance performance on the H IDDEN condition as
a signature of PToM.

Methods
Materials

We presented persuaders with five different scenarios each of which had a different payoff
matrix (see SI Table S1). A scenario involved a theme (such as AI policy) and three different
proposals. The persuader must persuade the target to choose their preferred proposal. Each
proposal had different effects on three different attributes unique to each scenario (such as
safety and control of LLMs, development speed, and public trust in LLMs). Persuaders and
6

targets valued attributes differently (e.g., preference for high development speed, neutrality
toward safety, preference for low public trust in LLMs) given different value functions.
The persuader and the target communicated via free-response messages. Fig. 1 depicts an
example of the MindGames interface with a scenario and a payoff matrix (a game).
Payoff Matrices. A player prefers a proposal when it has the highest utility (sum of attribute
preferences given its value function) relative to other proposals. We structured the payoff
matrices of all the games such that:
1. the persuader always prefers one proposal (e.g., “A”) and has complete information
about the effects of the proposals,
2. the target is missing the crucial information about four attributes across the three
proposals (of nine total),
3. therefore the target initially prefers a proposal dispreferred by the persuader (e.g.,
“C”),
4. if the target knew all of the information it would prefer a different proposal dispreferred by the persuader (e.g., “B”), and
5. if the target learns about two specific pieces of information it will prefer the persuader’s proposal (i.e., “A”).
We used a constraint solver to generate payoff matrices obeying these constraints and
selected a random sample of 100 of them. (See SI Section A.1.)
2.1.2

Procedure

In Experiment 1, persuaders had to persuade a rational bot. The rational bot updates its
preferences based on its assigned value function and available information, answers questions truthfully when asked, but never volunteers information. Accordingly, Experiment 1
measures PToM with a minimally cooperative interaction partner. We used gpt-4o to classify
whether any incoming persuader message: 1) discloses information about the proposals
or 2) inquires about the target’s (a) informational or (b) motivational states. The rational
bot’s decision rule is deterministic given its assigned value function and the set of disclosed
attribute values; we use the LLM only for message classification. A manual validation
audit of these binary classifier outputs is reported in SI Table S7. We then generated a
canned response from the rational bot to: 1) acknowledge information revealed, 2) reveal
states appealed to, or 3) respond generically (neither 1 nor 2). SI Fig. S5 reports how often
these exact generic fallback replies occurred by persuader type and condition. We call this
target rational because it always chooses the proposal that is maximally in line with its
preferences and current information, and naive because it freely discloses its preferences
and information and takes all disclosures of information at face value.
Disclosures and Appeals. A persuader had to strategically disclose information to persuade the rational bot. We operationalize a disclosure as stating a specific attribute effect for
a proposal (e.g., “Proposal A will increase development speed.”), and an appeal as asking
about what the target knows or values (e.g., “What do you know about the policies?”). If the
persuader discloses no information, the rational bot will never switch from a proposal the
persuader disprefers. If the persuader discloses all of the information, the bot will choose a
different proposal that the persuader also disprefers.
Persuasion in H IDDEN vs. R EVEALED. In H IDDEN, the persuader does not know the
target’s value function nor the information they have. In R EVEALED, we reveal the target’s
mental states to the persuader.
Hence, to know which pieces of information to disclose in H IDDEN, a persuader must appeal
to the target’s informational and motivational state with questions of the form, “what do you
know about the policies?” and “which attributes do you like?” Only with this information
may the persuader successfully disclose the relevant two pieces of information to persuade
the target more often than chance.

Participants

We elicited 200 trials from the LLM o3-2025-04-16 on five different scenarios, with 40
different payoff matrices per scenario, for a total of 200 games. We prompted o3 with default
parameters through the API with only the messages from the current conversation in context.
We chose this sample size to match the scale of the prior human dataset and to detect large
condition effects rather than marginal deviations from the chance baseline.2 In prior work
introducing MindGames (Authors (2025)), we also evaluated small prompt variants (e.g.,
adding a hint) and related game variants; see SI Fig. S4.
We compared LLM responses to data from 124 participants recruited through Prolific in
Authors (2025), including 199 R EVEALED condition trials and 202 H IDDEN condition trials,
from five different scenarios with randomized payoff matrices .
Participants were not told whether they were interacting with a human or bot. We debriefed
them afterward.
2.2

Hypotheses

In Authors (2025), we found that humans significantly outperformed OpenAI’s o1-preview
in the PToM experiment. Since this initial study, the more advanced reasoning model o3
was released. o1’s poor performance may be due to limitations in the model that have
since been improved upon. In this experiment, we ask whether the PToM deficit persists
in state-of-the-art reasoning models, specifically OpenAI’s o3. In doing so we establish
a baseline of LLM performance to compare to E2 and E3. At a high level, the core claim
yields a qualitative mapping from results to interpretation: if an agent relies on PToM, it
should perform well in H IDDEN and benefit little from R EVEALED; if it shows no evidence
of PToM but can exploit explicitly revealed mental-state information, it should show a large
advantage in R EVEALED. H1–H2 test this within o3, and H3–H5 compare the R EVEALED–
H IDDEN gap between humans and o3. Different ToM styles predict different types of failure.
An associative ToM should suffice when mental states are explicitly provided (R EVEALED)
but break down when they must be elicited and modeled through interaction (H IDDEN),
whereas a causal PToM should support performance in both conditions. We hypothesized
that:3
H1 o3 can discover the rational bot’s informational and motivational states in the H IDDEN condition
and use this information to successfully intervene on the target’s decisions—i.e., o3 success in the
H IDDEN condition is significantly greater than 0.1.
H2 o3 performs better on the task when the target’s informational and motivational states are
R EVEALED and do not need to be inferred—i.e., o3 success in the R EVEALED condition is
significantly greater than in the H IDDEN condition.

2.4

Discussion

Different ToM styles Experiment 1 shows that humans significantly outperform o3 in
the PToM-intensive H IDDEN condition of MindGames. This supports the claim that earlier
LLM successes on predictive ToM tasks, such as the false belief tasks, are not evidence
of human-like ToM. Human-like ToM characteristically bears the marks of both a causal
understanding of mental states and interaction as its primary context. This may reflect
fundamental differences in ToM styles between o3 and humans; o3’s ToM capacity may
merely involve a statistical model of mental states. Such a model would rely on associations
between different observable states, as opposed to causally-efficacious underlying mental
states that generate behaviors. Whereas o3 may possess an association-based understanding
of mental states that can support predictions in many common contexts, it may not possess a
causal understanding of mental states as underlying causes of behaviors, as is characteristic
of PToM. This contrasts with human ToM, which is characterized by both associative and
causal models of mental states.
The data on appeals and disclosures supports the interpretation that humans and o3 deploy different ToM styles. Humans are selectively information-seeking when faced with
an opaque interlocutor in H IDDEN and seemingly seek information to support surgical
interventions. This suggests that our human participants actively model the target’s informational and motivational states by seeking out information where this model is incomplete
10

and use this information to drive surgical interventions. In contrast, o3’s performance is
characterized by more disclosures on the basis of less information. Moreover, its behavior
does not change as markedly between the conditions as the behavior of human participants.
In short, o3 is not particularly sensitive to what it knows about the target. This pattern is
what we would expect if o3 was not relying on a model of the target’s mental states to guide
its disclosures and appeals.
Performance differences between conditions offer a final piece of support for the different
ToM styles interpretation. In earlier work, we found that humans perform equally well
on the H IDDEN condition and the R EVEALED condition (Authors, 2025). In contrast, the
difference between these conditions is very large for o3, with markedly better performance
in R EVEALED. Success in R EVEALED can be association-based: the persuader need not
monitor whether it has enough information about its interlocutor’s mental states to carry
out a successful intervention. Because all information about the target’s mental states is
immediately available in R EVEALED, an agent can perform well if they look for matches
between the target’s preferences and the information available to the persuader, using the
heuristic that targets will endorse proposals that best satisfy their preferences. o3 performs
poorly in H IDDEN, where the demand for causal modeling is high, but without the demand
for a causal model of mental states in R EVEALED, o3 improves. This is what we would
expect if o3 relies on an associative ToM, as opposed to the causal PToM. However, these
behavioral signatures do not uniquely identify an underlying mechanism. Performance differences could also reflect limitations in multi-step planning, working memory, or epistemic
tracking, as well as a pragmatic mismatch with a minimally cooperative target or noise in
the disclosure/appeal classifier (see the SI for robustness analyses and a classifier audit).
Planning interventions for persuasion We proposed that persuasion is a natural context
to use PToM. A subset of successful persuasion attempts (for humans in H IDDEN) seem
to rely on causal models of the target’s mental states. Experiment 1 shows that PToM is
relevant to persuasion, suggesting a relationship between them.
However, Experiment 1 is limited by its low external validity. MindGames is a significantly
more open-ended and more complex persuasion task than previous ToM tasks (Bartsch and
London, 2000; Bartsch et al., 2010; 2011; Peterson et al., 2018). Nonetheless, the rational bot
is a highly circumscribed interaction partner; it is only sensitive to exactly those persuasion
attempts that draw on PToM. This is by design. Because the bot never volunteers information
and provides sparse conversational feedback, success requires actively eliciting the target’s
informational and motivational states and planning disclosures accordingly. The cost of this
design is that it may also disadvantage persuaders that rely on cooperative conversational
cues rather than active elicitation of the target’s mental states. To provide the clearest
measure of PToM in our samples, Experiment 1 aims for a high degree of internal validity.
We can achieve higher external validity by using a less strict target—one susceptible to a
wider range of persuasion. Consequently, Experiment 1 points to a substantive difference in
ToM capacities between humans and o3, but fails to establish a general relationship between
persuasion and PToM.

3

Experiment 2: Role-Play Persuasion

In Experiment 1, we could entirely determine the information that the rational bot had
access to, the circumstances under which it would reveal information, and the inferences
that it would make from the persuader’s behavior. While this ensured that persuaders must
strategically inquire about and use mental state information to guide interventions, it differs
from persuading humans in many ways.
Human targets might be susceptible to rhetorical strategies (such as appeals to emotion
or authority) that do not depend on accurate mental state modeling (Cialdini et al., 2009).
They might provide more cues to their mental states—both explicitly and implicitly—than
the rational bot, making their beliefs and desires easier to infer without direct questioning.
Finally, humans might make decisions that deviate from strict utility maximization, either
through reasoning errors or by weighing considerations beyond the stated attributes.
11

These differences matter for interpreting our Experiment 1 results. Suppose we observe the
same pattern of results with humans role-playing a rational target. This would suggest that
LLMs’ lack of PToM means they are unlikely to persuade effectively in the real world when
facing the kind of information asymmetry that our task exhibits. Alternatively, suppose
LLMs persuade human targets in H IDDEN more effectively than they were able to persuade
the rational bot. This would suggest that LLMs are capable of persuading people by means
other than PToM. Moreover, it would suggest LLM success in this task is based on quite
different mechanisms than the ones that we theorize successful human persuaders use.
Experiment 2 sacrifices some internal validity for external validity by replacing the rational
bot with human participants. However, as with the rational bot in Experiment 1, target
participants were given a pre-specified value function over the attributes rather than using
their own values. They were also financially incentivized to select the optimal choice with
respect to these values. We called this ‘role-play’ persuasion because the human targets
were playing the role of someone else who had the values assigned to them.
If human targets behave as rational agents who simply communicate more naturally than
our automated bot, we should observe similar patterns to Experiment 1: human persuaders
should maintain their advantage over LLMs in the H IDDEN condition, and LLM persuaders
ought to perform better in the R EVEALED versus the H IDDEN condition. However, if human
targets are persuaded through rhetorical means that bypass mental state modeling, or if
they cooperatively disclose information without prompting, we may see LLMs narrow or
eliminate these performance gaps.

3.1.1

Materials

We used the same scenarios as in Experiment 1 except for some small clarifying changes
(see SI Table S2). These changes were made to more closely tie the labels for attributes to the
context of the scenarios, for example changing the abstract attribute “safety and control” to
“safety and control of LLMs”.
3.1.2

Procedure

We assigned some human participants to be targets, playing the role that the rational bot
played in Experiment 1. We assigned human targets a value function over the three attributes
(e.g., “You like safety and control of LLMs, dislike development speed of LLMs, and feel
indifferent about public trust in LLMs”) and showed them partial information about the
proposals, exactly as with the rational bot. Before receiving any messages from persuaders,
targets indicated which proposal they would initially choose. This pre-persuasion choice
allowed us to verify whether targets appropriately followed their assigned values and to
analyze how often persuasion changed their minds. We excluded targets if their choice did
not align with the optimal choice before any interaction with the persuader.
In Fig. 1, Human targets had a similar view to the persuaders but (1) only ever saw a “What
you know” section (which here would be the content of the “What the other player knows”
section), (2) were not told which proposal they ought to prefer, and (3) saw the text “You
should listen to the other player to help make your choice.” instead of “You must persuade
the other player of your preferred proposal.” (See SI Fig. S3.)
Analysis We analyzed our data in the same way as in Experiment 1, except we refined
our chance baseline from 10% (a nominal operationalization of chance performance) to
7.5% (based on a simulated analysis). This baseline is a chance benchmark under random
disclosure and does not rule out simple non-PToM heuristics. That is, if a persuader
randomly (with replacement) discloses six pieces of information (of the nine total), they will
persuade the rational bot about 7.5% of the time. (Revealing any more or fewer pieces of
information than six leads to an even lower success rate. See SI Section A.) This change in
12

threshold did not materially affect any of our conclusions in any of the experiments. We
report additional robustness analyses (exclusion rates, multiple-comparison corrections,
and volume controls) in the SI.
3.1.3

Participants

In Experiment 2, we recruited human participants to serve both as persuaders and targets.
We aimed to sample a total of 100 trials per condition.4 We recruited 152 participants who
were randomly assigned to one of three roles: Human Persuader, Human Target (for human
persuader), or Human Target (for o3 persuader). Each participant was assigned to complete
five trials in the same role, one for each of the five scenarios, however, due to matchmaking
issues and dropout participants often did not complete all 5 trials (2.7 trials on average).
Because each participant completed at most one trial per scenario, no participant saw the
same scenario in both H IDDEN and R EVEALED.
In total we collected 413 trials. 47 trials were excluded because the game was not completed
(e.g., a participant disconnected), and a further 182 trials were excluded because the human
target did not initially select the policy with the highest value under their assigned value
function. After exclusions, 184 trials were retained (human persuader: 44 H IDDEN, 62
R EVEALED; o3: 43 H IDDEN, 35 R EVEALED; see SI Table S3 for exclusions by condition and
persuader). The condition with the fewest number of trials was the o3 persuader R EVEALED,
with 35 trials.
We recruited participants from the platform Prolific, and paid $15/hr for their time, plus
a bonus of $1 for each trial in which they successfully persuaded the target to select their
preferred option (for persuaders) or successfully selected the optimal choice (for targets).
The average persuader bonus was $0.55, and the average target bonus was $0.09. This study
was approved by our IRB. Participants were not told prior to or during the experiment
whether they were interacting with a human or o3; we told them in a debrief afterward.

3.4

Discussion

In Experiment 1, we found that humans and LLMs display very different styles of ToM and
that this led to divergent levels of persuasive success in a tightly controlled task. Experiment
2 asked whether these conclusions would generalize to a more realistic setting where a
human plays the role of the persuasion target.
Do humans continue to outperform LLMs in the H IDDEN condition? In contrast to
Experiment 1, o3’s persuasive success was better than would be expected by chance (H1),
no worse than its performance in the R EVEALED condition (H2), and no worse than human
performance in the same condition (H3). Collectively, these results suggest that the LLM is
able to persuade human targets in ways that it was unable to persuade the rational bot. If
15

o3 shows no evidence of the PToM ability to persuade the rational bot, but can nevertheless
persuade human targets, this implies that interacting with humans bypasses some of the
need for PToM.
Why did o3’s persuasion success increase? There are several differences between Experiments 1 and 2 which could have led to o3’s increased persuasive success.
First, human targets may simply be more susceptible to persuasion than the bot. The success
of human persuaders was also higher in Experiment 2 than in Experiment 1, suggesting
it may simply have been easier to persuade people. More specifically, where two options
had apparently equal utility (given the information available to the target), the rational bot
would stubbornly maintain its preference for whichever option it had favored first, a policy
which would always disadvantage the persuader (see SI Fig. S2). By contrast, human targets
might have been sensitive to the motivations of the persuader, opting for the persuader’s
desired choice where this appeared to be costless.
In theory, humans may have been easier to persuade because they made irrational decisions
given the information available to them. However, our results suggest that humans virtually
never made decisions that had worse utility than the decision that the naively rational target
would have made (see SI Fig. 4). Thus, it appears that o3 was not performing better in E2
due to human error.
On the contrary, in many cases the human target made a choice that had a higher utility than
the one the rational bot would have chosen. Payoff matrices were sometimes set up such
that there was an option that benefited both the persuader and the target more than the
target’s initial choice. This nonzero-sum nature of the payoffs explains why games with
human targets led to both higher Persuasion Success and higher target utility compared to
the rational bot.
Third, humans were more cooperative communicators. The rational bot never revealed
information unless explicitly asked. Human targets, by contrast, might spontaneously
volunteer information about their value function (e.g. “Which option should I pick to
maximize environmental benefits?”) or informational state (e.g. “What impact will plan
A have on tech progress?”). This cooperativity might decrease the need for appeals about
mental states in H IDDEN, masking the PToM limitations of the LLM. Indeed, while the
number of target disclosures remained constant across experiments, the number of appeals
went down (see Fig. 7), suggesting that persuaders were acquiring information without
having to ask for it. Strikingly, o3 made almost no appeals in H IDDEN in Experiment 2, but
was nevertheless able to successfully persuade over 50% of the time.
Collectively, the results suggest that humans and o3 continue to show very distinct styles
of ToM in Experiment 2. However, in contrast to Experiment 1, this difference did not
lead to a large divergence in Persuasion Success. Nevertheless, the role-play persuasion
we investigated in Experiment 2 is still importantly different from real-world persuasion.
Participants were assigned values to defend, and may have behaved differently because
they were not reasoning about their real beliefs. We address this issue in Experiment 3 by
having persuaders attempt to change participants’ real preferences over the policies.

assigned nor immutable. In advertising, political campaigning, and interpersonal influence,
effective persuaders do not only convince interlocutors by strategically revealing information
that aligns with set values. Rather, persuaders often attempt to change what attributes their
interlocutors value.

targets’ real values rather than assigned value functions, but at the cost of additional noise
because targets’ value functions were inferred from survey responses rather than assigned

on survey-inferred preferences, it is also the noisiest experiment. Robustness analyses (SI
Table S4) indicate that two comparative inferences are sensitive to the analysis regime:
the within-o3 condition effect (H2) and the interaction (H5) are significant under the preregistered exclusions but not when exclusions are relaxed, and the interaction does not
survive Holm correction. By contrast, o3’s advantage over humans in R EVEALED (H4) is
significant under both exclusion regimes, and above-baseline success in H IDDEN (H1, H6)
is robust throughout.

4.3

Discussion

Experiment 3 investigated whether the patterns observed in the more controlled settings
of Experiments 1 and 2 generalized to real persuasion. We sought to understand how
persuaders’ strategies and performance changed when human targets could act on their
own mutable preferences rather than assigned value functions.
Overall, Persuasion Success peaked in this experiment. Both o3 (66%) and human persuaders
(45%) achieved higher Persuasion Success than in Experiment 2 (58% and 35%, respectively)
and substantially higher than in Experiment 1.
The open-ended environment of Experiment 3 proved particularly advantageous for o3.
The model outperformed human persuaders in both H IDDEN and R EVEALED (though
only significantly in the latter). This continues the trend from Experiment 2 and contrasts
Experiment 1, where o3 failed to perform above chance in H IDDEN.
Our exploratory analysis highlights a change in strategy for both human persuaders and
o3. Both human persuaders and o3 asked minimal diagnostic questions in the H IDDEN
condition, appealing to the target’s mental states only 1.3 times and 0.6 times per game,
respectively. This is a notable departure from Experiment 1, where humans made 6.1 appeals
and the model made 4.7 appeals per game. In Experiment 3, when constraints on the target’s
actions had significantly eased, both humans and o3 relied minimally on explicit inquiries
about mental states.
Instead, o3 succeeded in Experiment 3 when flooding conversations with information
disclosures. o3 made substantially more disclosures per game than humans in the H ID DEN condition across all experiments. One possible explanation is that the model may
have learned a heuristic association between verbosity (or high information disclosure)
and reward based on its training data, without a causal understanding of why acquiring
information about the target’s mental states is helpful. However, we note that possible information disclosure in this task is constrained relative to natural dialogue. Another possible
explanation is that the model may retrieve familiar arguments statistically associated with
the discussion topic (e.g., recycling arguments for action on AI policy), rather than tailoring
its points to the target’s belief or motivational states.
The efficacy of this high-information-disclosure strategy depended on the target. In Experiment 1, the strategy failed because the rational bot required specific information and could
penalize irrelevant disclosures. In Experiment 2, the strategy achieved moderate success; the
human targets were likely more tolerant of verbosity than the rational bot, yet the assigned

5

General Discussion

Our results suggest a dissociation between planning Theory of Mind (PToM) and persuasion capabilities, cautioning against premature attributions of human-like ToM to LLMs
while nonetheless highlighting the potential of models like o3 to influence people’s beliefs and behavior. o3 failed at causal mental state reasoning in controlled conditions yet
remained highly effective at persuading human targets.
The MindGames framework made this dissociation visible through three tasks that systematically trade off internal validity against external validity, with two conditions. Human
participants outperformed o3 in the tightly controlled H IDDEN condition of Experiment
1, yet o3 matched or exceeded human performance in the more externally valid settings
of Experiments 2 and 3. Importantly, o3 achieved this performance while making fewer
appeals to targets’ mental states than in Experiment 1. The dissociation between PToM
and persuasion capabilities has implications for both ToM research and research on the
persuasive capabilities of LLMs, which we explore below.

5.1

Humans are capable of PToM and persuasion

MindGames provides a first-personal, interactive measure of PToM that requires persuaders
to engage directly with a target to change their beliefs and behavior. In doing this, we
provide evidence of an advanced capacity for PToM in humans. We build on existing
persuasion paradigms used with children (§1), to create a rich, dynamic and interactive
task fit for use with even mature mindreaders. Success in Experiment 1 H IDDEN requires
counterfactual reasoning: persuaders must inquire about a target’s beliefs and values,
then simulate how a specific disclosure might update that target’s mental states, before
intervening to generate the desired behavior change.
Our human participants showed clear evidence of this capacity, performing significantly
above the baseline in H IDDEN. Nonetheless, human performance in Experiment 1 was far
from the ceiling. We observed substantial variability in performance between participants
(Fig. 2), and many participants found the task challenging. While average success was
moderate, a minority of participants succeeded in every game they played (Fig. 2), indicating
that the task is demanding but tractable for humans. This variability may be a strength.
21

Many ToM tasks are designed for children, which can elicit at-ceiling performance in human
adults. In contrast, MindGames provides a highly discriminative ToM task that could be
useful for downstream research. Whether this variability reflects individual differences in
PToM ability, task comprehension, or other factors remains an open question. Future work
could investigate whether Experiment 1 performance correlates with other ToM measures,
such as the Strange Stories task (Happé, 1994) or the Short Stories task (Dodell-Feder et al.,
2013).
Across experiments, human participants appeared to rely on mental state reasoning selectively. In Experiment 1, humans made substantially more appeals than disclosures in
H IDDEN; in Experiment 2, humans made roughly similar numbers of appeals and disclosures; and in Experiment 3, more disclosures than appeals (Fig. 7). This progression implies
that in the latter two settings, persuaders were not deploying PToM in the same way that
Experiment 1 required them to. In parallel, Rational Bot Success for human persuaders in
Experiment 2 was near zero, while Persuasion Success was substantially higher, and this
gap grew further in Experiment 3. These patterns across experiments suggest humans drew
on PToM when the task demanded it but shifted to other strategies when interacting with
more cooperative human targets—consistent with the theory that explicit ToM is cognitively
costly and deployed selectively (Apperly and Butterfill, 2009).
5.2

o3 shows no evidence of PToM but is still effective at persuasion

For o3, we observed a clear dissociation between performance by condition in Experiment 1.
In R EVEALED, o3 substantially outperformed human participants; in H IDDEN, o3 performed
no better than the chance baseline. These results extend those for earlier models (Authors,
2025), suggesting that the dissociation in performance persists in newer reasoning-focused
models.
We characterize the different strategies of o3 and humans in Experiment 1 H IDDEN as reflecting distinct “styles” of ToM. Humans appear to build a causal model of the target’s mental
states, while o3 relies on associative processes—pattern matching between observable game
states and persuasive strategies—without representing the target’s mental states in a causal
model. This associative approach succeeded when information about the target’s mental
states was provided to the persuader (Experiment 1 R EVEALED), or when familiar persuasive strategies happened to work (e.g., Experiment 2 and Experiment 3), but failed when
success required building a model of the target’s mental states through dialogue (Experiment
1 H IDDEN). Notably, o3’s improvement in Experiments 2 and 3 occurred despite using the
same low appeal, high disclosure strategy that failed in Experiment 1. The LLM succeeded
at persuading humans without relying on PToM.
5.3

Human targets allow multiple paths to Persuasion Success

Both o3 and human persuaders achieved significantly higher Persuasion Success in Experiments 2 and 3 than in Experiment 1 (in the H IDDEN condition). Several factors suggest why
persuasion became “easier” as the external validity of the experiments increased.
First, the rational bot and human targets differ by design. The rational bot provides information only when directly asked, updates preferences based only on disclosed information, and
cannot be moved from its value function. Human targets, on the other hand, can volunteer
information freely, update their preferences based on context they deem pertinent, and
update their value function (inadvertently in Experiment 2 or intentionally in Experiment
3).
Second, human targets were more cooperative than the rational bot. Appeals declined
significantly in Experiments 2 and 3 relative to Experiment 1 across both persuader types and
conditions. Yet target disclosures remained relatively constant (indeed, they progressively
increased for o3 in H IDDEN: Fig. 7). Human targets appeared to volunteer information to
persuaders, which reduced the need for persuaders to make explicit appeals.
Human conversation is structured by cooperative norms that our rational bot explicitly
violates: where Grice’s (1975) cooperative principle predicts that speakers will volunteer
22

relevant information and signal their communicative intentions, our bot responds only
when directly queried and never attempts to facilitate mutual understanding (cf. Clark
and Brennan, 1991). In short, human communicators cooperatively scaffold successful
communication.
The presence of this communicative scaffold in Experiment 2 may explain why LLMs perform much better here than in Experiment 1. LLMs, trained overwhelmingly on cooperative
human discourse, may be well-calibrated for exploiting this scaffold, but poorly equipped
for the genuinely adversarial epistemic environment that the bot created. A general consequence is that, if LLMs can persuade effectively through rhetoric and pragmatic exploitation
alone, then high performance on existing persuasion benchmarks may tell us little about
ToM—and much about the vulnerability of human interlocutors.
This may also help explain the asymmetry that o3 benefited more than humans from the
cooperative human-target settings. One explanation appeals to the two components of the
task: eliciting mental state information, and computing which disclosures will shift the target’s
choice given that information. Experiment 1 suggests that o3 excels at the second component
but fails at the first: it far outperformed humans in R EVEALED (65% vs. 22%), where no
elicitation was required. Cooperative targets who volunteer their mental states largely
remove the elicitation bottleneck, rendering H IDDEN functionally closer to R EVEALED: a
regime in which humans, not o3, are limited by the remaining utility-reasoning demands.
Cooperative targets therefore left o3 with more headroom for improvement than humans.
However, human cooperativeness alone cannot explain the relative ease of Experiments 2
and 3 compared to Experiment 1 (H IDDEN). If performance differences in these experiments
were driven solely by human targets volunteering more information, then this additional
information should have made it easier for persuaders to identify strategically optimal
disclosures, and those disclosures would also have convinced the rational bot. Yet Rational
Bot Success was substantially lower than Persuasion Success for both persuader types
in Experiments 2 and 3. Whether persuaders failed to translate volunteered information
into strategically optimal disclosures or never attempted to, cooperativeness alone cannot
account for their success. Persuaders must have also succeeded through means by which
the rational bot was immune. For example, in the transcripts in SI Fig. S9, o3 appeared to
try to reframe the target’s values rather than updating their information by arguing that
paradoxically, tight safety controls often hide problems. More broadly, persuaders in Experiments
2 and 3 may have succeeded not only by disclosing information (as in Experiment 1) but
also by exploiting reasoning errors or changing what targets valued (motivational states).
5.4

Implications for real-world persuasion

Our results suggest that even though LLMs can be effective persuaders, this is not evidence that LLMs possess advanced ToM capacities. More generally, real-world persuasion
may not call for much PToM at all. Persuaders may rely on a suite of alternative strategies.
Humans and language models alike may exploit observed associations between certain
persuasion strategies (such as verbosity) and desired outcomes, or may heuristically rely on
rehearsed general-purpose arguments rather than interventions tailored to their interlocutor’s specific beliefs and desires. A persuader who relies on association-based heuristics
may persuade very well using only associative ToM—creating associations between behavioral events—and not a causal one, that allows them to model the internal causes of such
behavioral events.
The split between tailored and heuristic persuasion strategies mirrors dual process theories
of persuasion, such as the Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty and Cacioppo, 1986). While
we have focused on the cognitive capacities of the persuader, the Elaboration Likelihood
Model is a cognitive model of the target’s information processing during a persuasion
attempt. Targets can be persuaded via two routes: the central route and the peripheral route.
If the persuasion attempt, in the form of e.g., an utterance, travels through the central
route and the target is motivated and capable of evaluating the content of the utterance
thoroughly, persuasion will likely result from a reflective process on the part of the target. If
the persuasion attempt travels through the peripheral route, it is not examined as thoroughly,
23

and successful persuasion will result from the target’s identification of positive or negative
cues in the utterance. In short, targets evaluate peripheral route messages heuristically. The
peripheral route is the default for humans when processing incoming messages, because
it is less effortful (Petty et al., 1981). For this reason, it is unsurprising that our human
participants likewise largely rely on heuristic persuasion strategies.
Our findings suggest that humans may be more effective persuaders than o3 when targets
process incoming messages through the central route, as humans can deploy PToM to
design arguments that specifically appeal to a motivated and thorough target. Although
tailored arguments are not strictly necessary for central route persuasion (e.g., the rehearsed
argument could still be compelling), it stands to reason that tailored arguments should
have greater success with reflective targets. Future work should compare persuasion in
models like o3 and humans, manipulating whether the target processes incoming messages
through the central or peripheral route. We hypothesize that humans may outperform o3
in persuading more reflective targets.
5.5

What is Theory of Mind, really?

In a seminal paper, Gopnik and Wellman (1992) argued that our everyday understanding of
the mind is structured like a scientific theory. Scientific theories posit unobservable entities
(gravity, viruses) to explain observable phenomena (the movement of bodies, the spread of
diseases). Gopnik and Wellman contend that to explain the phenomenon of behavior, our
ToM relies on unobservable mental states as its explanatory posits. These unseen mental
states explain observable behaviors because they cause these behaviors. Consequently, a
causal understanding of mental states props up human social interaction, rendering us
understandable to each other.
Gopnik and Wellman’s theory-theory has been very influential on the ToM literature. However, our findings suggest that in some social interaction contexts (i.e., persuasion), a
causal understanding of mental states is less and less important the more we approach
naturalistic contexts. This means that the causal understanding of mental states that we
have referred to as PToM is at once characteristic of human ToM and also rarely relied on by
humans.
The claim that sophisticated ToM capacities, such as PToM, are rare in social interaction has
been developed in the dual process theory of ToM. This theory distinguishes implicit and
explicit levels of ToM, each with its own associated system (Apperly and Butterfill, 2009;
Butterfill and Apperly, 2013). A fast, automatic system maintains implicit forms of ToM
and a slow, effortful system maintains explicit forms of ToM. According to the dual process
theory, explicit ToM is rarer than implicit ToM precisely because it is effortful.
PToM is likely also highly effortful, compared to associative ToM alternatives, suggesting
that the explicit/implicit distinction aligns with our distinction between associative and
causal ToM styles. The cognitive demands of explicit ToM also means that it is only
deployed in service of the agent’s goals (Apperly, 2012; Westra, 2017). Our design enforces
goal-dependent processing by rewarding successful persuaders. It is surprising, then,
that participants in Experiments 2 and 3 relied very little on PToM. This suggests either
that PToM is prohibitively expensive (deployed only when no alternative exists, as in
Experiment 1), or that persuasion simply calls for other strategies entirely. To examine the
relative contributions of the factors of costs and relevance, future work should investigate
the cognitive costs of PToM and its deployment across other contexts that may call for causal
models of mental states.
More fundamentally, why should we care about PToM if it is rarely deployed to alter people’s
opinions in naturalistic interaction?
The importance of PToM may not primarily lie in its ability to explain the behavior of participants in natural interaction, but rather in its ability to map the space of socio-cognitive
representations. Highly controlled designs are commonplace in psychology. Paradigms
such as the Stroop task (Stroop, 1935), garden-path sentences (Bever, 1970), and verbal
interference (Baddeley and Hitch, 1974) all involve creating artificially challenging circum24

stances precisely to uncover cognitive mechanisms. The scenarios presented in classical
false belief tasks are themselves highly artificial, as we have argued (§1). The importance
of the false belief task does not lie in its ability to adequately model the child’s everyday
cognitive challenges, but to reveal whether children understand the perspectival nature of
thought, which is a crucial building block of a mature understanding of the mind. Similarly,
although our identification of PToM in Experiment 1 may have only limited applicability in
explaining natural persuasion behaviors, it provides evidence that humans have a causal
understanding of mental states that they can use to plan and act out interventions on their
environments.
PToM is important for a variety of reasons. For example, it likely contributes to our explicit
understanding of behaviors (Cavallini et al., 2013). It may also enrich children’s developing
ToM, because it supports interventions to test specific hypotheses about the mental causes
of behavior. (Gopnik and Schulz, 2004; Gopnik, 2012; Gopnik and Wellman, 2012).
Since PToM is important at the levels of development and cognitive mechanisms, it raises
deeper questions about the relationship between ToM and persuasion. Even if adult humans
mostly get by on associative reasoning in persuasion, the capacity to identify and learn
the relevant associations may depend on an earlier ability to bring about evidence of such
associations through interventions. For this reason, participants who perform strongly
in measures of PToM (Experiment 1) may show surprising transfer effects to measures of
naturalistic persuasion (Experiments 2 and 3). Future work could investigate within-subject
correlations of performance in Experiment 1 and Experiment 3 to test this hypothesis.
The upshot of the above discussion is that real ToM is neither effortful deliberation nor
frugal heuristics but the broader capacity to deploy both of these capacities flexibly and
effectively. An understanding of human social cognition, requires that we can both identify
representational mechanisms in controlled conditions and evaluate how they are and are not
deployed in externally valid environments. Our work explicitly engages in this explanatory
project.

6

Conclusion

We used a persuasion-based measure to compare the capacity for Planning Theory of Mind
(PToM; §1) in adult humans and OpenAI’s reasoning model o3. We adapted this task for
three experiments that systematically traded off internal for external validity. Humans
significantly outperformed o3 in Experiment 1 H IDDEN (Fig. 2), where success required
PToM, while o3 outperformed humans on Experiments 2 (Fig. 3) and 3 (Fig. 5), which
gradually relaxed PToM demands. Our findings suggest that humans and o3 may fundamentally diverge in their ToM styles: humans can represent mental states as causes of
behaviors whereas o3 can succeed via associations between behaviors and strategies in
settings where PToM is not required by the task. Consequently, we recommend a cautious
attitude toward exaggerated claims about human-like ToM in LLMs. Nonetheless, o3 can
still be impressively persuasive.
Our findings raise further questions about the relationship between persuasion and PToM,
as well as foundational questions about the nature of ToM itself. Restricted persuasion
contexts (as in Experiment 1) are a powerful tool for revealing PToM capacities, and they
may even be especially useful for persuading more reflective targets—a target that can only
be evaluated through the central route (Petty and Cacioppo, 1986). We propose that the broad
capacity for ToM may be flexible: fast, efficient, and based largely on association when the
contextual scaffold is present, and slow, costly, and based on causal understanding when the
context is challenging. This paints a multifaceted picture of ToM that aligns with theories
that divide the capacity into the reflective and the heuristic, the explicit and the implicit.

Generating Payoff Matrices

We used a constraint solver to enumerate payoff matrices (outcomes for proposals), value
functions, and information hidden from the target such that the naively rational target
would initially choose one proposal, p; given all the information an optimal target would
choose another proposal, p∗; and given some of the revealed information an optimal target
would choose the persuader’s preferred proposal, p′ .
There need to be at least three choices (proposals) because the target has to have a different
preference than the persuader initially and there must be a confounding proposal to prevent
the persuader from convincing the target with simple heuristics (such as by revealing all of
the information). Additionally, the target must not know all of the available information
(they must have something hidden to them). There must also be at least two attributes for
each proposal and value function. We chose to have three attributes because this allowed
for more possible payoff matrices. (There are only 56 possible sets of information and value
functions with two attributes as compared to tens of thousands with three attributes.)

A.2

Selecting a Game

We avoid giving a participant a payoff matrix they have already seen. The scenario is chosen
first, then a payoff matrix is selected.
In the role-play persuasion experiment, we choose any unseen payoff matrix of the required
type.
In the real persuasion experiment, we infer the target’s value function from their initial survey
answers (binned to -1/0/+1) and select an unseen payoff matrix whose target coefficients
match that value function (using any 3-attribute subset when the scenario has more than
three). If no match is found, we fall back to the first available matrix. This can occur, e.g.,
when a participant answers “neutral” to more than two attributes.
