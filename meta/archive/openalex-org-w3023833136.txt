Abstract
Community question answering (CQA) platforms provide a social environment for users to share
knowledge online. Users can submit complex and subjective questions on CQA platforms and then
derive the desired answer from other community users. A large number of user-generated data
has been produced by various CQA sites (e.g., Quora, StackExchange) and been used in different
CQA researches. Question retrieval task is one of the popular CQA tasks aiming at solving the
overloading issue of CQA platforms and increasing user satisfaction by reducing their waiting
time. A question retrieval system is expected to automatically retrieve similar questions from the
CQA archives regarding a new question, and the answers to similar questions are returned to users
directly.
Different information retrieval (IR) approaches have been proposed for question retrieval task
ranging from the conventional retrieval models to the learning to rank models and neural ranking
models. However, the IR community is now facing the issue of overusing the weak baselines. Thus,
it is hard for researchers to identify the reported improvement of the newly-proposed methods,
which greatly impedes the development of the community. Some researchers have already proposed several competitive baselines for ad-hoc retrieval task, but currently, the proposals of strong
baselines for question retrieval are still not enough. Thus, this work targets on identifying the
suitable baselines for question retrieval task on different datasets.
We conduct an empirical comparison among different retrieval models on two representative
datasets and analyze the performance of models on different question sets. Analyzing on CQA
questions is challenging since the CQA questions are more diverse and complex, compared to the
questions on traditional question answering (QA) (e.g., Wikipedia) system as well as the queries
on traditional search engine (e.g., Google). Our work investigates the impact of the question from
two perspectives. We first display how retrieval performance changes on various question sets (e.g.,
questions with different lengths and different levels of specificity) and then explain the reasons
for the performance changes. Moreover, we conduct an error analysis to reveal the hard types of
questions for different retrieval models on two datasets. In order to overcome the existing weakness
of the retrieval models, we further select two techniques that have already proven effective in other
retrieval tasks. We hypothesize that the two techniques can also be useful on question retrieval
task. We then implement the two techniques on our datasets to validate the hypothesis. Our
findings show that one of them can not help to enhance the retrieval effectiveness of models due
to the different characteristics of task design while another technique successfully demonstrates
the additive effectiveness gains.
Based on our findings, we find out the suitable baseline models on different datasets as well as
emphasize their relative strength and limitation. We believe our work can provide useful guidance
on how to select an appropriate baseline for future works on question retrieval.

Chapter 1

Introduction
Community Question Answering (CQA) systems, such as Stack Overflow 1 , Quora 2 , and Zhihu 3 ,
provide a social environment for knowledge sharing and collaborative learning [138]. A large number of users have been attracted to different CQA systems, e.g., Stack Overflow has more than
9 million activate users [94], and Quora has over 300 million monthly users [169]. Traditionally,
we ask questions by web search engines (e.g., Google and Bing), in which the returned answers
come from the existing documents. In contrast, CQA systems exploit the collective intelligence
of the whole CQA community to return the high-quality answers to users. In Figure 1.1, we
give a toy example of the questioning and answering process in CQA systems. User A posts a
question in the CQA system and obtains a series of answers from other community users. We
denote the users who answer the question as community A. Then the other users in the system
may vote for the answers, we name them as community B. Therefore, user A may pick up the
best answer according to his/her own judgment as well as the voting from community B. Both the
question and the best answer are stored in the CQA archives. Compared to the web search engines,
users are encouraged to submit more complex, subjective and precise questions on CQA platforms.

Figure 1.1: The typical process of question-answering in CQA systems, in which the user A post
his/her question and receive a satisfying answer from other community users.
CQA systems can provide abundant answers to questions; however, providing the best answer
to the users in a short time is very difficult to achieve [138]. The first reason is that overloading
problem caused by a huge number of new questions are posted [138] may largely slow down the
best answer providing process. Secondly, the quality of answers varies a lot since CQA platforms

are open to anyone with different knowledge backgrounds [169]. In order to support the question
answering process, information technologies like information retrieval (IR) and natural language
processing (NLP) have been applied, yielding different tasks such as question retrieval, answer
retrieval and post quality assessment [138].
In this thesis, we solve the overloading and answer filtering problem of CQA platforms by
working on question retrieval (QR) task [138]. The question answering process is overloaded
due to an increasing number of new questions. In fact, some of the questions may be repeatedly
expressed in different ways by different users. For example, Table 1.1 shows ten repeated questions
that express the same meaning in different ways. The repeated questions cause redundancy, which
is a common issue for CQA archives. Take Yahoo! Answer as an example, 25% of the new
questions are detected as repeated questions [131]. Question retrieval (QR) works on automatically
returning answers by detecting similar questions from CQA archives [166]. The major benefits of
question retrieval systems include: (1) The redundancy of archived data can be largely decreased
by avoiding the same question being answered many times [169]; (2) User satisfaction can be
significantly increased by reducing the waiting time [169]. We describe the question retrieval
task as follows. For a new question, the question retrieval system takes it as input and retrieves
candidate questions from a CQA archive. Then the questions that are similar to the input question
are retrieved by the question retrieval system. The whole process is illustrated in Figure 1.2.
The detailed description of the two important question retrieval components, i.e., the index and
retrieval models, will be given in Chapter 2.

Figure 1.2: An overview of a question retrieval system, in which the retrieval model takes the
question and the index as input and output a ranked list of similar questions.

in different ways.

Motivation

Previous work has already proposed various retrieval methods on question retrieval task [166, 29,
42, 17, 74, 166, 167, 170, 164, 36, 40, 168, 121, 165, 85, 35, 110, 37]. However, it is still difficult
to identify the state-of-the-art approaches due to the usage of weak baselines, which has been
one of the main concerns for IR community. A weak baseline is any method that was poorly
selected by the authors for comparison with a proposed method that exhibits significantly lower
effectiveness compared to other published methods for the same task. In 2009, Armstrong et
al. [5] conducted a longitudinal survey over a large amount of literature on ad-hoc retrieval task4
and revealed the issue of overusing weak baselines in the IR community. The literature shows
the results of the retrieval techniques as well as the results of their corresponding baselines on
different Text Retrieval Conference (TREC) tracks5 . Figure 1.3 displays the finding on task of
TREC-8 Ad-Hoc6 that contains 32 result pairs (i.e., the results of proposed techniques vs the
results of corresponding baselines). We find that more than half of the reported baseline models
underperform the Median TREC (i.e., the median scores of all submissions on the track of TREC8 Ad-Hoc) and perform largely worse than the Best TREC (i.e., the best result of all submissions
on the track of TREC-8 Ad-Hoc), indicating that most of these approaches can not be treated as
suitable baselines. According to [5], the claimed improvement over such weak baselines for new
techniques is not always trustful. That is to say, comparing the newly-proposed methods with
weak baselines may lead to non-additive gains, which is further confirmed by [78].

documents.

1.2

Research Objective

In this thesis, in order to identify competitive approaches on question retrieval task, we systematically compare a set of selected methods on two datasets in a consistent experimental setting.
Then we conduct an analysis to better understand the relative strengths and weaknesses of each
retrieval model. Our work aims at solving the following research questions:
RQ1: Which retrieval model achieves the best retrieval performance on different datasets? Which
category of models (traditional models, learning to rank models and neural ranking models)
achieve the best overall retrieval performance on each dataset? Can a certain model or a
certain category of models offers a consistent retrieval performance gain over other models
on different datasets?
RQ2: What are the impacts of different question characteristics (e.g., lengths and the level of
specificity) on performance of different retrieval models? Are the conclusions consistent over
the different datasets?
RQ3: Which type of questions are hard for a certain categorized models to handle in terms of
two datasets?
RQ4: What approaches can be used to enhance the retrieval performance of each categorized
models further?
After answering above four research questions, we can draw a conclusion of which approaches can
be considered as strong baselines for different datasets or different question sets.

Related Work
In this chapter, we will first introduce the history and the current development of community
question answering (section 2.1). Then, we will provide readers with the background of information
retrieval and describe the different retrieval models used in this thesis (section 2.2).

2.1

Community Question Answering

2.1.1

Background

In recent years, CQA systems have gained a lot of popularity by providing a set of diverse social environments for knowledge sharing and collaborative learning. The Straight Dope 1 can be
considered as one of the earliest CQA platforms, which was built in 1973 on an American questionand-answer newspaper column [169]. Later in the 1990s, the number of CQA platforms grew in
popularity [169]. Most of them were initially designed for scientists or experts instead of general
users. For example, questions on Experts-Exchange 2 and Madsci Network 3 were mainly answered
by experts of a certain domain. After the 2000s, new CQA platforms such as Quora, Yahoo!
Answers 4 and Zhihu are designed for anyone who want to share knowledge with others online,
attracting great attention around the world. Some CQA systems focus on a specific domain (e.g.,
Apple is one of the sub-forums of StackExchange, dedicating to solving the software issues) while
other CQA sites do not have topic restriction (e.g., Quora, Yahoo! Answers). The main goal of the
CQA systems is to find an answer for users effectively. However, the overloading issue caused by
the growing number of new questions hampers the development of CQA systems. Thus, developers
have proposed various methodologies by leveraging the information technologies to support the
question-answering process. According to [138], different CQA approaches are classified into three
main categories: (1) Exploratory studies; (2) Content and user modeling; (3) Adaptive support.
They are introduced as follows:
1. Exploratory Studies
This group of studies concerned with the data that are collected during a process of question
answering. Such analyses provide us with an overview of how different users interact with
each other and how users interact with CQA platforms. The analyses are further divided
into three sub-groups [138]: (1) System-wide analyses; (2) Content-related analyses; (3)
User-related analyses. The system-wide analyses include the studies of the CQA systems
evolution [150] and the principles of CQA platforms design [98]. Besides, the studies on
how to extend the CQA concepts to other applications have attracted much attention. For
example, IP-QAT [99] is a community-based question answering system, in which users can

are two educational QA boards, in which the professors can support students with course
materials and collaborative learning is also allowed. The content-related analyses focus on
post strategies (i.e., how to guide users to formulate a proper question or answer in a CQA
context) [58, 141, 161] and personalization of posts which are used to predict the certain styles
of posting [160]. The user-related analyses mainly studied on user behavior (e.g., identifying
the user situation [48] and user motivation [52], analyzing the evolution of experts [113] and
how the users select the questions to answer [38]).
2. Content and User Modeling
The first important direction of this group of research is to assess the quality of the CQA
questions and answers. The question quality can be measured from different perspectives
and various frameworks have been proposed. Shah et al. [128] proposed a two-stage method,
in which the questions are firstly categorized into different groups and then their quality are
measured by utilizing the corresponding textual features constructed for a certain question
group. Tausczik et al. [143] illustrates that multiple forms of users’ reputation can be used
to estimate the quality of questions. Ravi et al. [122] combined the length of a question
with users’ reputation as features served as an input to a binary classifier to predict the
question quality. Additionally, the answerability of a question has been also treated as an
effective predictor of question quality. Dror et al. [41] employed several classification models
and regression models to predict the possibility of receiving answers as well as the actual
number of answers with respect to a question. Shah et .al [6] worked on summarizing the
reasons that why questions remain unanswered and using a classifier to predict the delay in
answering a question. Another definition of question quality is the complexity of a question,
which determines the level of user expertise required to answer the question [91]. Besides,
the question complexity can also be used to predict the maturity and robustness of a CQA
community [22]. Burel et al. [22] and Lin et al. applied the logistic regression models
and the probability models [91] respectively to identify the complex questions in CQA platforms. Some work derived the ground truth based on the community feedback. For example,
Ponzanelli et al. [118] advised us to use community judgment (e.g., the number of votes and
comments) for evaluation and Ravi et al. [122] suggested to normalize the obtained score
with the view count. However, the community judgement is unreliable due to the subjective information. Thus, later work such as [2] suggested hiring human annotators to do the
labeling manually. Besides the question quality, answer quality also plays a crucial role in
CQA research since unsatisfactory answers impede the process of knowledge sharing. Thus,
it is important to filter out low-quality answers and keep the most useful ones. Similar to
analyses of question quality, answer quality can be measured from different perspectives such
as accuracy and responsiveness [20]; relevance, objectivity and completeness [172]. Despite
the fact that various approaches have been proposed, such as using the linear regression
model [172] or a question-type-specific classifier [144], the evaluation of answer quality estimation is also biased since the most CQA questions are subjective and contain unique
context with respect to each user. Thus, eliminating the bias of judgment has also been
investigated [31].
Another important direction of the content and user modeling is to analyze the question
type and topic. Different taxonomies have been used for the classification of the question
type. Li et al. [88] categorized questions according to questions’ subjectivity and objectivity.
Harper et al. [57] classified questions into the informational group and the conversational
group. Another popularly used taxonomy is grouping questions based on their answer types
such as opinion, fact, confirmation and reason [100]. Besides, questions can also be classified
by their geographical dependency [31] or by their time-sensitivity [95]. The former work helps
to solve the question routing problem, and the latter work emphasizes the level of urgency
with respect to one question. Besides question type classification, the topic organization is
used to display a clear relation between a large number of questions and their categories.

Only the question text is utilized for the topic classification task. Various approaches have
been proposed to assign an appropriate topic to each question such as a category-based
language model [9], a k-nearest neighbor classifier [133], a maximum entropy classifier [24].
Topic hierarchy maintenance is an important task for tackling the issue of the growing size
of categories. The main idea of topic hierarchy maintenance is to distinguish a new category
that can complement the current topic organization. The related work is similar to topic
modeling. For instance, Miao et al. [103] applied the probabilistic latent semantic analysis
(PLSA) on Yahho! Answers to identify the new categories.
3. Adaptive Support
Adaptive support studies aim to directly enhance the process of knowledge sharing and
collaborative learning by using the outputs from exploratory studies and user modeling
studies. Question retrieval is one of adaptive support studies making use of the CQA
archives and it will be mainly discussed in section 2.1.3. Question suggestion aims at
displaying a recommendation that contains different aspects of information related to the
new question [28]. Different language models [151] and tree-based approaches [28] have
been adopted for question suggestion by utilizing the textual content features. Question
answering is defined as reusing the previous answers to solve the new submitted question
immediately [138]. Earlier related work such as [19], in which a learning to rank model
GBrank is proposed to rank answers; Xue et al. [154] used a hybrid architecture composed of a
translation model and a query likelihood language model, which can measure the similarity
between the new question and previous questions as well as the previous answers. One
of the recent work proposed a two-stage QA algorithm [114], in which a set of resolved
past questions that are similar to the new question are returned firstly, and then their
corresponding answers are ranked by a statistical classifier according to the similarity with
the new question. The approaches discussed so far utilize the CQA archives to help CQA
systems effectively return a suitable answer to users while question routing task direct
the questions to the community users who are most likely to provide a high quality answer.
More specifically, question routing is defined as given a new question q, a list of users should
be returned, and the list is ordered by users’ level of capabilities of answering the q [86].
Three main aspects have been studied in order to provide a suitable list [86, 87, 142, 75]: (1)
Creating a comprehensive question profile which can better represent question; (2) Creating a
comprehensive user profile which can better represent the interested domain and educational
level of one user; (3) Constructing an effective matching pattern between the question profile
and the user profile.

2.1.3

Question Retrieval

Question retrieval task is one of the main CQA tasks, aiming at retrieving a set of answered
questions from a question pool with respect to a new question (query question). This is a
sentence retrieval task, on which both the query question and retrieved question (candidate
question) are in the form of natural language. Retrieving a set of sentences is more challenging
in comparison to retrieving a document set due to the more severe issue of vocabulary mismatch.
For instance, the questions “how do I get knot out of my cat’s fur?” and “how can I remove
a tangle in my cat’s fur?” are semantically similar to each other but lexically different. Hence,
besides the exact word match, retrieval models are expected to capture the match at the semantic
and syntactic level between the query question and candidate question. There are mainly three
types of approaches aiming at tackling the vocabulary mismatch issue [67]:

• Translation-based models
The translation model [21] was initially used for learning relations between words in different languages by calculating their translation probabilities. The same idea is employed
on question retrieval by leveraging question-answer pair to learn the semantically related
words, which help to reduce the lexical gap [74, 154, 166, 16]. Later works also employed
external corpora (e.g., Wikipedia) as complementary, which has been proved to boost the
result [169, 132] further. However, the underlying assumption behind the translation model
is that the question and answer are “parallel text”. It is not true in practice since questions
and answers are highly asymmetric in terms of contained information. Besides, the performance of various translation models in works of literature highly depends on the quality of
the corpus, especially the quality of answers [169].
• Topic-based models
The second group of methods is based on topic modeling. It has been shown that semantic
similarity can be detected by using topic distribution. The reason for this is because the topic
of a text can be treated as a kind of semantic representation. Latent Dirichlet allocation
(LDA) [152] is one of the state-of-art topic models that assumes the topic distribution is
produced from a Dirichlet distribution. Consequently, LDA has been applied on question
retrieval task [166, 170]. The translation-based methods and topic-based methods measure
the semantic similarity at different levels (topic-based model capture meaning at a less finegrained level). Thus, some works combined the topic model with the translation model and
found that those two models complement each other [167, 171].
• Deep learning models
In recent years, deep learning techniques have been used in many related studies that aim
at learning distributed vector representations of two texts and compare them to determine
the matching degree [54]. Doris Hoogeveen et al. [169] summarize seven recently proposed
deep learning approaches (see Table 2.2). We first introduce four papers which applied
simple bag-of-words (BoW) representation [40, 168, 121, 165]. The BoW representation
describes the occurrence of a word within a document and ignores the order between words.
Query questions have two representations in [168], one is a weighted BoW representation, and
another one is a distributed representation generated by a convolutional neural network [116]
(CNN). A cosine similarity metric is then used to measure the relevance degree between the
query question and candidate question. This method only can beat simple baselines. Another
similar work included a tensor layer [136] to generalize the relation between question pair,
which has been considered as a stronger measurement than cosine similarity metric [121].
Zhou et al. [40] and Zhang et al. [165] both used word2vec [104] to learn word embedding
and measure the embedded word similarities rather than compare the word vectors. Das et
al. [35] ’s works included a topic model as well as a CNN model and using character trigrams
as word representation. This work demonstrated a better result compared to previous topic
models [167, 170]. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [65] have been viewed as a natural fit
for dealing with sequence inputs with different lengths. Only several works included RNNs
in question retrieval [35, 110]. It has been reported that Lei et al. [85] achieves the best
result to date, which integrates an RNN model and a gated CNN model. The model is
pre-trained in an end-to-end framework on a raw corpus. However, this model has not been
compared with any state-of-art non-neural-network approaches. Another well performing
RNNs-based work was proposed for SemEval Task3. It employed a long short-term memory
network (LSTM) [55], which is a modified version of RNNs.

Information Retrieval Models

According to Manning et al. [126], information retrieval can be defined as follows:
Information retrieval (IR) is finding material (usually are documents) of an unstructured nature
(usually text) that satisfies an information need from within large collections (usually stored on
computers).
The central goal of information retrieval is to provide documents from within a document
collection that are relevant to an arbitrary user information need. The information need refers to
the topic about which the user looks for and usually described by a query. The documents are
considered as relevant if they contain the information need. A traditional application of the IR
system is web search engine (e.g., Google, Baidu) in which the user submits a query that conveys
his/her information need and then obtains a set of relevant web pages. Figure 2.1 displays an
overview of a typical IR process. The IR systems utilize an index of a document collection to
search relevant documents and use various retrieval models to assign the relevance score between
a query and a document. The retrieval models aim to optimally rank the documents with respect to
a query, that is, ranking the relevant documents above the non-relevant documents. As mentioned
in Chapter 1, the retrieval models can be divided into three types and we will introduce them in
this section respectively.

with respect to a query and the retrieval model assigns the relevant score to each relevant document.

2.2.1

Traditional Retrieval Models

The group of traditional retrieval models cover boolean retrieval models, vector space models,
probabilistic retrieval models and language modeling [11]. The boolean retrieval model is an exactmatching model which treats documents either relevant or non-relevant without providing any
ranking information [126]. Thus, we do not consider boolean retrieval models. The vector space
models take ranking information into consideration by measuring the cosine similarity between a
query and the corresponding documents. Besides, the vector space models introduce TF-IDF [126],
which is a basic term weighting scheme (i.e.: a product of the frequency of the query terms in
the document and their inverse document frequency that measures how rare the terms are in the
corpus) that assigns more weights to the informative query terms. More sophisticated and effective
term weighting schemes have been applied by different types of traditional models such as BM25,
which will be introduced later. This section mainly introduces three covered traditional models:
one probabilistic retrieval model (BM25) and two language modeling approaches (QL and RM3).
Probabilistic Models
As mentioned above, an IR system is expected to estimate how well a document satisfies a certain
information need. Nevertheless, an IR system is likely to have an uncertain understanding of a
given query as well as an uncertain guess for the relevance between a query and a document. Thus,
probabilistic approaches have been applied to the reason for those uncertainties [126]. The main
idea of this kind of approaches comes from Probability ranking principle (PRP) [137]: ranking
documents by estimating the probability of being relevant with respect of an information need:
P (R = 1|d, q).
Binary independent model (BIM) is one of the basic probabilistic models that has been conventionally used with PRP [123]. Documents and queries are represented as binary vectors (1
means the presence of a term in one document, 0 otherwise). Besides, ’independence’ indicates
that IBM considers the different terms are independent with each other [123]. This assumption
simplifies the estimation of probability. In order to obtain a more accurate probability estimation,
we need to consider the relatedness between different terms as well as other queries/document
statistics. BM25 [123] utilizes an advanced term weighting scheme that considers term frequency
and document normalization. It has become one of the most widely-used probabilistic models [123]. Equation 2.1 shows the weight scheme of BM25. The parameter k1 controls the term
frequency scaling. The parameter b determines the scaling by document length and avoid model to
prefer long documents [45]. Inverse Document Frequency (IDF ) (see Equation 2.2) measures the
informativeness/importance of a term based on the number of documents contains the term [126].

Learning to Rank Models

Models introduced in section 2.2.1 need to tune the parameters manually (e.g., k and b for BM25;
λ for QL and RM3; f bdoc, f bterm and f bOriW eight for RM3). In this section, we introduce
another type of retrieval methods, of which parameters are learnt automatically. This group of
retrieval methods are mainly based on the framework of machine learning. Figure 2.2 describes
the process of learning to rank. Since L2R methods are based on a supervised learning. Thus, a
training set is needed and one training sample is composed of (1) Queries: qi (i = 1, ...n) ∈ Q; (2)

A ranking model h is learned by using a learning algorithm on the training set. More specifically, the goal of learning is to optimize the h by minimizing the difference between the given
relevance label and predicted label returned by h on the training set. And lastly, the performance
of an optimal model is tested on a test set (unseen data).

Various learning algorithms have been proposed. According to different training objectives,
the learning algorithms are divided into following groups [93]:
• Pointwise approach considers the relevance information between every question-document
pair. The main limitation of this approach is that relative ordering between different documents (with respect to the same query) is ignored.
• Pairwise approach considers the preference between two documents (with respect to the
same query) by taking the feature vectors of a document pair as input. Therefore, the
ranking task can be simplified as a classification task: predicting whether if one document
is more relevant to a query than another document.
17

CHAPTER 2. RELATED WORK

• Listwise approach takes a set of documents features as input and outputs a ranked list.
In this case, loss function aims at optimizing the evaluation metrics directly, which is harder
to realize in comparison to the one in pointwise approach and pairwise approach.
In this thesis, we select four state-of-art L2R models covering all categories:
• Random Forest [70] belongs to the group of pointwise approach. It is an ensemble method
that combines a collection of decision trees (learners or classifiers). Each decision tree is
trained on a subset of training data and tested on the left-out data. The optimal weights
are learned by the decision trees to build a robust prediction model. Random forest shows
competitive performance because of the inherent parallelizability and simplicity of tuning.
• LambdaMART [23] is one of the state-of-the-art pairwise approaches that combines a
tree-boosting algorithm MART (Multiple Additive Regression Trees) with LambdaRank
(extended from RankNet) [64]. MART is a boosted tree model combined with gradient
descent. Different from the Random Forest, MART generates a regression tree in each
round and assigns a weight for each of them. Then, MART combines all weak regression
trees to form a reliable model. The loss function is upgraded and minimized in RankNet for
a given ranking task.
• AdaRank [153] is also a boosting technique that combines weak learners to construct a
strong one. It belongs to the group of listwise approach, assigning the equal weight to
each training sample at first and increasing the weights of samples that obtained more error
based on the current ranker. Then the next weak learner will focus on the hard samples,
and samples are re-weighted iteratively. AdaRank usually runs several rounds, and the final
model is linearly combined by all weak learners.
• Coordinator Ascent [101] is an optimization method and also belongs to the listwise
approach. Ranking scores are calculated as weighted combinations of all feature values.
Several restarts at different positions of weight space can be used to tackle the issue of
getting stuck in local minima. Same as AdaRank, the measure-specific loss is minimized.

2.2.3

Neural Ranking Models

Unlike L2R models that employ machine learning techniques over handcrafted features, neural
ranking models learn the features of text representation and matching patterns from the raw text
automatically by using the neural networks [106]. Current neural ranking models are divided into
three groups, which will be introduced first in this section. Later, we will discuss the five covered
neural ranking models in detail.
Categories of deep learning models
According to [54], we group neural ranking models into three categorizes based on the manner
they model the query q and document d (as shown in Figure 2.3).
• Representation-focused architectures include two complex representation functions
(i.e., deep neural networks), which are used to map a query q and a document d into a
query vector and a document vector respectively. Then, a simple matching function (e.g.,
cosine function) is applied to match the two vectors and generate the relevant score. Some
popular representation-focused models are DSSM (Deep Structured Semantic Model) [69],
C-DSSM (Convolutional Deep Structured Semantic Model) [130] and ARC-I (Convolutional
Neural Network Architectures I) [68].
• Interaction-focused architectures firstly build a local interaction between a query-document
pair and then employ neural networks to extract the higher matching pattern based on the
local interaction. Some popular neural ranking models in this group are DRRM (Deep
Relevance Matching Model) [53], KNRM (Kernel Neural Ranking Model) [32], ARC-II

Now, we will introduce the input/output representation of BERT, which is the reason why
BERT can handle a large range of downstream tasks. Compared to other introduced neural
ranking models (see Figure 2.3, 2.4, 2.5, 2.6, 2.7), BERT only has one input sequence which
can be a single sentence or a sentence pair. The input sequence will be pass through three
embedding layers. The first layer is the same as any embedding layer in other neural ranking
models. It transforms a word representation into a vector representation. The only difference
is that BERT includes two more special tokens. Every input sequence is required to begin
with a classification token [CLS] and is separated by the token [SEP]. The second layer
is the segment embedding layer. Since different downstream tasks contain different forms
of input (a single sentence or a sentence pair), thus, the segment embedding layer aims to
distinguish the paired sentence inputs from the single sentence inputs. Lastly, the third layer
is a position embedding layer indicating the unique position information for each word in the
sequence. For instance, for a given input sequence I think, therefore I am, the two words ‘I’
will have the same representation without using the position embedding layer. As a result,
the position-specific representation can be produced by the position embedding layer and
then flow through multiple transformer layers, generating a new contextual representation
at each layer.

Methodology
3.1

Datasets

In this thesis, we select one benchmark dataset SemEval introduced in section 2.1.2 and construct
another dataset based on Quora. The reasons are given as follows:
1. There are not enough benchmark datasets available for the current question retrieval community. SemEval is a commonly-used benchmark for SemEval Task that attracts a number
of work [47, 13, 46, 108].
2. Compared to SemEval, the size of Quora is much bigger, which is good for training the L2R
models and neural ranking models. Besides, Quora is one of the most popular sources of
data nowadays, which is worth analyzing. The reason why we chose Quora from several
popular CQA datasets (e.g., Yahoo! Answer and StackExchange) is because: (1) Quora
contains relevant information between different questions while Yahoo! Answer does not.
(2) Quora only contains information of questions while StackExchange also includes other
thread information and metadata such as tags and dates, which are not needed for our task.
Also, it has been reported that only some sub-forums are suitable for CQA research [66],
and it is hard for us to recognize good quality ones from all sub-forums.

SemEval

As we mentioned in section 2.1.2, SemEval consists of two different languages of questions. Besides,
the thread information like comments is also included regarding each question (both original
question and candidate question). In this thesis, we only consider questions in English. SemEval

Question type
Next, we take a deep look at different types of questions. According to [105], we classify all
questions into the following categories:
1. Factoid questions can be answered with brief and consice facts in a single phrase or
a sentence (e.g., Who is Jordan?, What was the name of three brothers in Harry Potter? ).

Most of factoid questions start with wh-words (who, when, where, which) and contain named
entities (person name, location, time, etc.).
2. Non-factoid questions can be further divided into four sub-groups:
• Causal questions usually start with why or how, looking for an explanatory answer
(e.g., Why should someone buy an iPhone?, Why do answers on Quora get collapsed? ).
• Confirmation questions need to be inferred by leveraging the high level of knowledge, requiring a yes/no answer (e.g., Can eigen values be negative?, Are human beings
naturally monogamous? ).
• Opinion questions are commonly asked on CQA platforms, seeking open answers such
as suggestions and solutions. Opinion questions are subjective and unique regarding
different users and contexts (e.g., How do I develop good sense of humor?,I feel sleepy
and dull all of the time. What can I do to be energetic all of the time? )
• Mixed questions are more complex than the above types of questions since they
contain several subquestions. Those subquestions sometimes can be answered independently (e.g., I am a US citizen and flying next week from New York to Doha. I
intend to travel to Morocco and back in three weeks. Is it possible and safe? Should
I rent a car or buy one in Doha? I have a driving license from New Jersey. Do I
have to have a driving license from Qatar to drive? ), and sometimes the answer to one
subquestion is based on another subquestion (e.g., Would Donald Trump make a good
US president? Why or why not? ).

we observe that most mixed questions in SemEval actually consist of the types of confirmation

Quality of questions and relevance judgments
CQA data can be classified as user-generated content and thus faces two main issues: (1) The
quality of questions varies from very informative to spam due to the diverse backgrounds of
users [2]; (2) The relevance judgment sometimes is biased and unreliable. Regarding to the two
used datasets, we find that questions of Quora are usually formed in a formal text while informal
language using such as typos and grammatical mistakes can be found in SemEval frequently (e.g.,
Hey mates; Hope you guyz are doing well :) Wanted to know if the Penalty for Running the Red
27

CHAPTER 3. METHODOLOGY

Light has already been reduced or still under Discussion? grr!!! ). Such informal questions are
semantically poor and hard for the system to pre-process as well as to retrieve a similar text for
them.
As we mentioned above (section 3.1.3), the type of CQA questions covering from the factoid
questions to non-factoid questions. In terms of a factoid question, it is unlikely to lead to a bias
of understanding among different users. However, non-factoid questions are usually subjective
and can be perceived from various perspectives. Thus, it is difficult to determine the quality of
the relevance judgment between two non-factoid questions, indicating that some of the current
relevance annotations may become unreliable. This biased issue is found to be severer in SemEval,
which can be verified by Table 3.4. We see that the query question is seeking the experience of
mixed marriages, while the first two given relevant questions are looking for opinions on mixed
marriages. Some people may think they are different due to the distinctive information need.
Nevertheless, some people may consider they are similar since they all discuss the same concept.
An unreliable annotation is found in the third given relevant question. It is denoted as relevant
to query question by crowd workers but actually describes another concept: special care for the
expat.

3.2

Experimental Pipeline

Figure 3.1 depicts an overview of our experimental pipeline. We test different categorized retrieval
models in a consistent setup with the same pre-processing and evaluation platform. Besides, two
additional techniques (query expansion technique and pre-training technique) are included in our
retrieval test.

3.2.1

Data Preparation

Before conducting our retrieval test, we first clean our used datasets by lemmatizing all questions
with the help of WordNetLemmatizer and lowercasing all contained words. For traditional models,
we use Indri 2 to build the index. Indri is one of the commonly-used IR toolkits for over a
decade [139], which is a part of the Lemur [112] project3 . The inputs of the L2R models and
neural ranking models are presented as instances in Figure 3.1. Each instance consists of a query
question (after being pre-processed), a candidate question (after being pre-processed) and the
given relevance information between them (the relevance information is also referred to as the
ground truth).

3.2.2

Feature Extraction

Regarding L2R models, a set of features are extracted before the retrieval test. According
to [54], we categorize all used features into three groups: query-dependent features, queryindependent features and query-level features. Most of our features belong to the former
two groups, and only one query-independent feature is included that is the length of the candidate
question. All the features are numeric and are combined to train the ranking model.
Query-dependent Features
We consider employed query-dependent features within LETOR datasets [120] since they have been
shown to be useful in various L2R applications [93]. Features in this category include similarity
scores calculated by different popular weighting functions (feature 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 in Table 3.8)
and various matching statistics between query question and candidate question (feature 6 ∼ 19 in

for SemEval. The evaluation metrics are measured on trec eval 7 . Moreover, we conduct a pairwise
t-test [135] for statistical significance testing on all experiments with p-value lower than 0.05.

Quantitative Analyses

In this section, we are trying to demonstrate the performance of models on different query characteristics in order to answer the second research question (section 1.2).
Query Length Analysis
The impact of query length on models’ performance has been explored by previous work. Different
conclusions have been derived in terms of various IR tasks. It has been shown that query length is
positively related to retrieval performance for relevance feedback experiments [147] and operational
interactive IR systems [14]. More specifically, in the case of relevance feedback experiments, it is
found that retrieval effectiveness can be improved by adding more terms that relate to query and
suitably weighted by the relevance models [147]. Also, the prior work on interactive IR systems
concludes that the longer queries are more likely to increase user satisfaction [14]. However,
Kumaran and Carvalho [80] found that models’ performance is deteriorated on longer queries (used
queries come from Robust 2004 Track long description queries) due to their more sophisticated
information need. Kumaran and Carvalho also showed that longer queries are more likely to
contain ambiguous and noisy terms. Relevant work on investigating query length’s impact on
question retrieval tasks has not been deeply studied. Thus, we decide to display the changes in
models’ performance on questions (also termed as queries in this thesis) with different lengths.
We have already reported the length statistics of two datasets in Table 3.3. The distribution of the
question length can be found in Figure 4.1 (test set). We can observe that questions in Quora have
a smaller length range (ranging from 3 words to 33 words), compared to the questions in SemEval

long queries. In terms of SemEval, queries with 3 to 40 words are treated as short queries, and

less than 15 as short queries, and the rests are long queries.

Quora: We conjecture that retrieval models can achieve better performance on short questions because the short questions contain more clear and simple information need. In order to

the set of matching candidates and thus easy to identify the true relevant questions.
Regarding the long questions, they contain more keywords and sub-sentences, specifying an
ambiguous information need and a complicate question structure (see Table 4.2). Retrieval models
have to infer the underlying relation between different keywords and different sub-sentences in
order to find a correct relevant question. Five examples of long questions are also displayed in
Table 4.2. Let us take a look at long question Q3. The retrieval models are expected to capture
the opposite relation between Navy, Air Force and Army due to the fact that Navy and Air Force
belong to the United Kingdom Royal but Army does not. As for long question Q5, retrieval models
need to know that the first sentence (I feel sleep and dull ) is a primitive condition to answer the
question of the second sentence (be energetic). Therefore, only capturing the keywords is not
enough to infer the conveying relation of the questions, which explains why retrieval models are
more likely to fail to demonstrate good performance on the long questions.
SemEval : In previous section, we mentioned that the average length of SemEval questions is
longer than one of the Quora questions (see Table 3.3). We also summarized three characteristics
of the relative longer questions in SemEval : (1) Questions contain multiple information needs; (2)
The contents of questions are detailed and unique; (3) Questions contain redundant contents. The
former two characteristics indicate that it is hard to find a similar question that contains all the
same topics and entities with the query questions due to the unique context. Thus, the goal of the
user is to find the relevant questions that partly answer his/her question. Therefore, we conjecture
that the reason for the better performance on longer questions is because the probabilities of
including one of their keywords or topics are higher than the cases in shorter questions. Table
4.4 and 4.3 can verify our conclusion. We first look at the long question Q1 in Table 4.4. Long
question Q1 includes six entities and one topic. The first relevant question contains one same entity
bayt.com, which is the name of a job website. In spite of the different user backgrounds, the answer
of R1 can still provide useful information to answer the long question Q1. The second relevant
only question contains another entity Qatar, which also can provide the desired information that
the user wants. Let us take a look at an example of the short question (as shown in Table 4.3).
The number of entities in short question Q1-Q5 is limited. As a result, it is much harder to find
the same entity in a relevant question, compared to the long questions.
Analysis of Query Specificity
As we introduced in section 3.9, IDF score can be used to measure the specificity of a question.
The MaxIDF is the largest IDF of all question terms. Previous work has shown that MaxIDF
positively correlates with the retrieval effectiveness of models on ad-hoc retrieval task [60]. More
specifically, a higher MaxIDF can help the retrieval models to select a particular candidate set
while lower MaxIDF is hard for models to pick out relevant candidates from a large number of
documents. The AvIDF is the average of IDF scores of all query terms considering the IDF scores
of all terms. It has also been considered as another useful query performance predictor on ad-hoc
retrieval but less effective than AvIDF [60]. However, no previous studies have shown which one
is more effective on question retrieval task. Thus, we decide to analyze the impact of MaxIDF

4.2

Query Expansion for Traditional Models

According to the above analysis, we find that traditional models face the issue of lexical mismatch.
In order to answer the RQ4 (see section 1.2), we now explore some useful techniques that help
to overcome the lexical mismatch problem to improve the effectiveness of traditional models.
We assume that it would be easy for traditional models to solve the vocabulary mismatch by
utilizing more context-related terms. Take the question How can you rapidly lose weight in 14days?
(Quora) as an example, some related words such as fast and quickly help traditional models pick
out a much smaller relevant candidate set. Let’s take the question Are Spanish and Italian mutually
intelligible? as another example, context-related words such as language and symmetrically are
expected to help models narrow down the search scope. Thus, we consider employing the query
expansion technique on traditional models. The query expansion technique aims at alleviating the
vocabulary mismatch problem by refining the initial query with additional meaningful terms [1].
The new augmented questions are expected to be more informative compared with the original
question. Various methods have been proposed such as relevance feedback based approaches,
interactive query filtration and corpus dependent knowledge models [7]. In this thesis, we decide
to implement a main PRF (pseudo relevance feedback) based method: RM3. The mathematical
details can be found in section 2.2.1. RM3 has already demonstrated additive effectiveness gains
on ad-hoc retrieval task [1, 89], but no related work has been conducted on the question retrieval
task.

Conclusion

In the task of question retrieval on datasets SemEval and Quora, RM3 shows little effectiveness
in improving the retrieval performance of two traditional models. The reason is that RM3 is more
likely to benefit the models’ performance on short factoid questions and harm the one on nonfactoid questions. As we mentioned in section 3.1.3, most questions in Quora and SemEval are
non-factoid questions and the non-factoid question is also the main question type for most CQA
datasets. Thus, adding a relevance model maybe not a useful approach to enhance the traditional
models for question retrieval task, thereby answering the RQ4 (see section 1.2).

4.3.2

Evaluation of Feature Importance

We now measure the importance of each applied feature by conducting a tree-based L2R model
combined with the Gini coefficient [96]. This thesis includes two tree-based L2R models: Random
Forest and LambdaMART. We choose to use Random Forest for analyzing the feature importance
since it shows comparative effectiveness on two datasets while LambdaMART performs poorly on
SemEval (see Table 4.1). All feature importances are calculated by Gini importance. As we can see
from Figure 4.11 and Figure 4.12, the score-based features (e.g., BM25 scores and TFIDF scores)
derived from the ranking models are shown to be most effective due to their abilities to measure the
relevance between question pairs. Besides, meanTFIDF, maxTFIDF and varTFIDF are shown to
be important to L2R models’ learning on SemEval. Regarding to Quora, the prominent features are
sumTFIDF, maxTFIDF, CoveredQueryTermTokens, sumQueryTermTF and normSumQueryTermTF. The different prominent features on two datasets specify their distinctive characteristics.
To be more specific, features such as CoveredQueryTermTokens, sumQueryTermTF and normSumQueryTermTF are more useful for Quora, indicating the question pairs of Quora have more
word overlaps than ones of SemEval (see Figure 3.1 and Figure 3.2). Regarding to other features
not mentioned above, they make little contribution to models’ learning and may harm the final
performance. Thus, a feature selection is expected to filter out the useless features regarding to
different datasets.
In terms of query-level features, we find that they do not have obvious impacts on models’
performance, verifying the derived conclusion for ablation study. Nevertheless, we still want to
analyze which specific query-level feature is more useful. We find that the performance of L2R
models on SemEval is influenced by the query length, maxIDF, gamma1 and SCS while ones on
Quora only sensitive to AvIDF and SCS. Therefore, SCS can be treated as a useful feature among
all query-level features on different datasets. We mentioned that SCS is used to measure the
ambiguity of a question by employing the Kullback-Leibler divergence (see Equation 3.7), and
now we can prove it as a relatively stronger predictor on CQA datasets, compared to other used
query-level features.

4.3.3

Conclusion

After conducting the feature analysis on different feature groups as well as different individual
features, we find out the most useful feature group is the query-dependent group on two datasets.
The performance of L2R models can not be improved by only utilized the two features extracted
based on BM25 and QL. Also, the useful features for different L2R models as well as the different
datasets are varying. All the findings suggest we conduct a feature selection instead of using all
the extracted features.

Conclusions and Future Work
In this chapter, we will draw several conclusions from our work and then briefly describe several
aspects that worth to be explored more in future work.

5.1

Conclusion

Our work mainly focused on question retrieval task based on CQA systems. A question retrieval
system aims at returning similar questions from the CQA archives regarding a new question on
CQA sites. As a result, the system helps to reduce the time-lag between posting a question and
deriving an answer. Besides, the system helps to solve the redundancy issue of the CQA archives.
Most previous work focuses on mitigating the vocabulary mismatch problem between the question
pairs but ignores the issue of overusing the weak baselines, which has become an urgent concern
in the IR community. Thus, our work targeted on finding out the suitable baselines for question
retrieval task on different datasets.
Our work selected one commonly used CQA benchmark SemEval and constructed another
dataset based on one popular CQA platform, Quora. The two used datasets are representative
regarding the question length and type. We picked our tested models from three different types of
retrieval models (traditional model, learning to rank models and neural ranking models). BM25
and QL come from the group of traditional models, exhibiting the competitive and robust performance on two datasets. Despite the effective performance, we found that it is hard for traditional
models to capture the right context of the non-factoid questions. Besides, traditional models show
their ineffectiveness on questions with complex sentence structure. In order to further improve the
performance of traditional models, we combined them with a relevance model RM3 and derived a
list of expansion terms for refining the original questions. However, our results showed that RM3
could not help to improve the overall performance of two traditional models on two datasets.
We also tested the retrieval effectiveness of four L2R models (AdaRank, Coordinate Ascent,
LambdaMART and Random Forest) and found that their performance is not consistent. Compared
to traditional models, Coordinate Ascent and Random Forest can achieve competitive results on
two datasets while LambdaMART only shows the comparative result on Quora. The performance
of AdaRank is relative worse on two datasets in comparison to the other three L2R models.
Since L2R models and traditional models both utilize the statistics of question terms; thus, L2R
models suffer the same retrieval problem as the traditional models do. We further investigated the
employed features and found that the query-dependent features are prominent and contribute more
to the promising results on two datasets. Besides, we conducted an analysis of feature importance
and found that two datasets benefit from different features due to the distinctive characteristics
of the dataset.
In terms of neural ranking models, we selected aNMM, ArcII, MV-LSTM and Duet, which
are specifically designed for the short text matching task. We found that they all fail to achieve
competitive results as other groups of models achieve. Compared to the traditional models and
65

CHAPTER 5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

L2R models, neural ranking models are more sensitive to the noise and suffer the issue of overgeneralization. In order to bridge the performance gap between the neural ranking models and
the other two types of models, we then applied BERT and found the results of BERT are better
than the results of all previously tested models.
Therefore, we draw the following conclusions of the baseline selection on question retrieval
task:
1. Despite the fact that the issue of vocabulary mismatch is more likely to occur between
the complex, diverse and subjective CQA questions, BM25 and QL can still be treated as
suitable baselines due to the comparative effectiveness, simplicity and the robustness to the
variance of datasets. However, they should be given together with other baselines that can
capture the relevance signal on the level of linguistic and syntactic.
2. Regarding the L2R models, we showed that AdaRank performs relative worse than the other
three L2R models. The reason may be attributed to the size of the dataset. Thus, we can not
safely draw the conclusion of AdaRank. As for the other three L2R models (LambdaMART,
Coordinate Ascent and Random Forest), the retrieval effectiveness is largely dependent on
the employed features. We showed that statistics-based features could not help L2R models
lead to a big improvement over the traditional models due to the various characteristics of
CQA datasets, emphasizing the importance of designing the linguistic features as well as
the syntactic features. However, feature engineering on different datasets is time-consuming.
Thus, the conclusion of the L2R models is similar to the one of the traditional models:
LambdaMART, Coordinate Ascent and Random Forest (by using our extracted features) are
good choices for baseline section, but a more effective baseline should be provided together
with them.
3. Regarding the neural ranking models, the poor retrieval performance of aNMM, MV-LSTM,
ArcII and Duet may be caused by the small size of the dataset. But still, we can not safely
conclude that the four popular neural ranking models can provide fair comparison conditions
for the work of question retrieval. Another neural ranking model BERT should receive more
attention. Our work illustrated that BERT could show the effectiveness on different datasets
as BM25, QL, Coordinate Ascent and Random Forest. Moreover, BERT also displayed the
capability of generalizing the correct context of non-factoid questions. Besides, training a
pre-trained BERT is simple and efficient. Thus, BERT should be used as a competitive
baseline in future work.

5.2

Future Work

1. We see that RM3 fails to help enhance the traditional model due to the poor quality of
some expansion terms. Previous work has tried to involve additional criteria such as query
length [82] and linguistic features [134] to filter out the useless expansion terms. Another
work integrates a term classification process with RM3 to select the good quality expansion
terms [25]. Moreover, some work also applies the word embedding technique on term selection [71]. Thus, we believe that the mentioned techniques can alleviate our current query
drift issue and help to reduce the ambiguity of long expanded questions.
2. We mentioned that feature selection should be conducted to filter out the less influential
features. Moreover, dataset-specific features are expected to be designed due to the large
variance among CQA datasets in terms of the question length and the question type. Previous works have already investigated a rich set of features based on some popular CQA
datasets (e.g., Yahoo! Answer, Stackexchange) but for different tasks (e.g., answer selection) [140, 156]. But still, we expect the idea of designing high-quality features from CQA
datasets can shed light on our future work.
66

CHAPTER 5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

3. In our experiments, BERT demonstrates the competitive performance on two datasets. However, our analysis also points out that questions with long lengths or containing the specific
entities are hard for BERT to handle. Since traditional models (BM25 and QL) are capable
of distinguishing the specific entities, thus, we believe that the performance of BERT can
be improved by interpolating with BM25 and QL. We can find similar work on the ad-hoc
retrieval task in [159].
