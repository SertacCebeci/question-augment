# Real-query distribution transfer failure

Retrieval benchmarks built from academic or bar-exam questions do not describe how practitioners actually ask. In a study with the New Jersey Office of the Public Defender, defender queries were visibly separable from the queries of two public legal datasets (LePaRD, BarExam-QA) in both t-SNE and PCA projections of query embeddings.[^c1] The consequence was measured by fine-tuning four small embedding models on different training sets and testing on real defender queries.

## What was measured

Fine-tuning on BarExam-QA raised in-benchmark Recall@5 by 1.7 points, yet recall on the proprietary defender set and the released public-defense set fell.[^c2] A "naive" synthetic set (one LLM-generated query per corpus paragraph) helped the two public benchmarks but substantially hurt both public-defense test sets.[^c3] The authors attribute this to domain shift, a speculation rather than a tested mechanism.[^c4]

What worked was making training queries look like real ones. An LLM was fine-tuned on annotated (query, paragraph) pairs to write more realistic queries, the output was filtered with a reranker trained on the defender data, and paragraphs of facts, tables of contents or procedural content, which rarely appear as real targets, were removed.[^c5] Fine-tuning on this optimized synthetic set improved public-defense recall. Expanding queries with an IRAC (issue, rule, application, conclusion) legal-reasoning trace before fine-tuning also helped; a small E5-large model approached the much larger Qwen3-Embedding-8B.[^c6] Zero-shot, expansion was mixed: it raised recall for smaller models and lowered it for larger ones.[^c7]

## A proxy benchmark for private data

The released public dataset tracks the private, defender-annotated one: Spearman R 0.79 across eight zero-shot runs and 0.82 across 20 fine-tuning runs.[^c8] One confound: the first production system returned the top five of one model, which inflates that model's score on the private set.[^c9] So a benchmark can be validated as a stand-in by rank correlation of system scores, not by looking similar.

## The same query-source gap in medical benchmarks

The query source is a variable in clinical question answering too, though there it is argued rather than measured. PanCanBench is built from de-identified questions "collected from the Pancreatic Cancer Action Network (PanCAN) Patient Services Help Line".[^c11] Its authors hold that simulated queries miss what real ones carry: "a reliance on simulated patient queries which may not reflect real clinical complexity".[^c12] HealthBench, in their account, "relies on synthetic questions generated by GPT and Claude".[^c13] In my reading the paper shows the gap by construction and by the benchmark's own rubrics; it does not measure a score or recall loss on the synthetic queries, so it is weaker evidence than the retrieval result above.

## Limits

- One domain (public defense, one office, appellate-brief paragraphs); whether the gap generalizes to other fields is not tested.
- The domain-shift explanation is speculation; the paper shows embedding separability, not that separability causes the loss.
- Expansion results depend on model size and were mixed zero-shot, so "domain knowledge helps" holds mainly after fine-tuning.
- The optimized synthetic data used annotated real queries and a reranker trained on the target set, so it is not free of target-domain supervision.
- Annotation agreement with an independent annotator was only fair (Cohen's kappa 0.36), so labels are noisy.[^c10]
- Correlation of 0.79 rests on eight systems.
