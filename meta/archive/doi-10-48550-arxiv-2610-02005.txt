Counting Moves, Weighing Voices: Bayesian Dialectical Argumentation
for Calibrated Multi-LLM Councils under Persistent Adversaries
Ionel Eduard Stan∗ , Paolo Napoletano
Department of Informatics, Systems and Communication (DISCo),
University of Milano–Bicocca, Milan, Italy
ioneleduard.stan@unimib.it, paolo.napoletano@unimib.it

Abstract
A multi-LLM council lets several large language models
(LLMs) deliberate on a question and return an answer together with a confidence estimate. As these systems become
increasingly used for reasoning, that confidence should represent a calibrated probability of being correct, and the decision should remain robust when some agents are persistently unreliable. Existing council aggregation methods fail
on both fronts: their confidence estimates measure decisiveness rather than correctness, and they cannot identify or discount persistently unreliable agents. We introduce Bayesian
Dialectical Argumentation (BDA), which treats the council’s
typed moves—who proposed, challenged, or conceded which
answer—as observations of a classical annotator model with
per-agent reliabilities. This formulation recasts multi-agent
deliberation as a reliability estimation problem, using the deliberation trace to infer agent reliability under persistent adversarial behavior. By weighting evidence according to inferred
agent reliability, BDA yields calibrated posterior probabilities
over candidate answers while allowing persistently unreliable
agents to be inverted rather than merely outvoted. Across binary and multi-class benchmarks, BDA achieves the best calibration among zero-cost council aggregation methods, requiring no additional LLM calls, and improves robustness under
persistent adversarial coalitions while remaining competitive
in clean settings.

Introduction

Consider a panel of three large language models (LLMs)
asked to verify a claim: they deliberate—proposing, challenging, conceding—and the system returns a verdict with a
confidence. Two requirements make that verdict trustworthy:
calibration—when the panel says “0.9” it should be right
nine times in ten (Dawid 1982)—and robustness: one persistently unreliable member, weak, stale, poorly prompted,
or compromised, should not silently capture the answer. Fail
the first and the confidence misleads; fail the second and the
council is only as trustworthy as its worst seat.
Deployed aggregators fail both requirements, each for a
structural reason. Their confidences are decisiveness signals,
not probabilities: a unanimous vote reports 1.0, yet unanimous councils in our data are wrong 18% of the time; an
argumentation margin reports nearly 0 even when correct

The idea. Both failures have one fix: treat the deliberation as evidence. This changes the role of deliberation in the
aggregation pipeline: rather than treating the exchange primarily as prose to be judged or synthesized, we treat each
protocol-governed move as an attributable probabilistic observation about the candidate answers. Councils emit typed
moves—Propose, Challenge, Concede—whose legality a
protocol governs, the speech acts of formal dialogue theory
(Hamblin 1970; Walton and Krabbe 1995). We read each informative move by agent i as a draw from a categorical noise
model governed by that agent’s latent reliability θi , with a
conjugate prior fitted on labeled calibration data, and apply
Bayes’ rule—Bayesian Dialectical Argumentation (BDA).
The posterior is closed-form (Eq. 2), costs no model calls,
and meets both requirements: a probability whose conjugate prior doubles as a cross-validated recalibrator (Property 1), deciding by a weighted majority whose weights are
the log-odds of each speaker’s reliability (Proposition 1)—
the committee rule of Nitzan and Paroush (1982), plurality
the equal-weight case.
When a persistent adversary’s fitted reliability falls below
chance, its weight becomes negative and its endorsements
become evidence for the opposite answer—BDA does not
out-argue a persuasive adversary, it learns not to believe that
seat; the council’s signal without its attack surface (Figure 1).

Background and Related Work

Multi-LLM councils. MoA (Wang et al. 2025), Self-MoA
(Li et al. 2026), and multi-agent debate (Khan et al. 2024)
aggregate deliberation through different mechanisms, while
prior work has exposed fragile gains, poorly calibrated confidence, conformity effects, and the importance of the full
interaction trajectory (Smit et al. 2024; Cui and Du 2025;
Hao et al. 2026; Cui et al. 2026). We use the MoA family and
plurality as baselines (Section 5) and address the remaining
gap with a calibrated per-seat interpretation of deliberation
under persistent adversaries.
Argumentation-based verification. A quantitative bipolar argumentation framework scores arguments and propagates their strengths through attack and support edges (Dung
1995; Baroni, Rago, and Toni 2019). The argumentativeLLM pipeline (ArgLLMs) of Freedman et al. (2025) has one
model generate and score an argument tree for a claim and
returns the tree’s gradual strength as its confidence. We evaluate ArgLLMs as the strongest published claim-verification
baseline: its strength-as-confidence is a margin, not a probability, and LLM argument mining and judging are themselves
unreliable (Sanayei et al. 2025)—exactly why BDA reads
typed moves rather than prose: public, verifiable communication semantics, the classical agent-communication principle
(Singh 1998; Wooldridge 2000).
Reliability-weighted aggregation and calibration.
Weighting votes by competence is classical—the optimal
rule for independent binary voters uses log-reliability-odds
(Nitzan and Paroush 1982; Grofman, Owen, and Feld 1983),
generalizing Condorcet’s jury theorem. BDA is this lineage
on a new carrier: protocol-governed typed moves, per-agent
priors fitted from data, a K-candidate treatment, and an
adversarial evaluation (concurrent work attacks LLM debate
empirically Amayuelas et al. 2024; Khan et al. 2024; Cui
and Du 2025; we add the aggregation defense). We measure
calibration (Dawid 1982) by ECE (Guo et al. 2017) and
the proper Brier (Brier 1950), comparing against post-hoc
recalibration (Zadrozny and Elkan 2002)—a comparison

that changes our own clean headline (Section 6).
Label aggregation and annotator models. Inferring a
hidden truth from noisy annotators is a long crowdsourcing
literature: the one-coin model of Dawid and Skene (1979) fits
one reliability per annotator by expectation–maximization
(EM); Raykar et al. (2010) learn per-class confusion matrices; Raykar and Yu (2012) down-weight and invert adversarial annotators exactly as our negative weights do; and a parallel line estimates reliabilities without labels (Whitehill et al.
2009; Zhang et al. 2016; Hovy et al. 2013; Ratner et al. 2020).
BDA is this annotator model on a new carrier—typed deliberation moves under a protocol, per-agent reliabilities and,
in our generalization, full confusion matrices—stress-tested
against a live adversarial coalition rather than independent
noise, where the unsupervised estimator’s label-switching
ambiguity is exactly what a supervised prior resolves (Section 7).

Bayesian Dialectical Argumentation

The name of the method summarizes it. We count moves:
the record reduces to who endorsed which answer, prose
discarded. We weigh voices: each agent’s counts enter the
posterior through its own fitted reliability—an adversarial
voice, we will see, weighs negatively. The central modeling
choice is this reduction from deliberative traces to signed endorsements. It defines how protocol-governed dialogue acts

binary carrier is in effect weighted round-0 voting; the dialectics there earns its keep in the reliability estimator (ECEem
0.117 → 0.080), its contribution to the final decision grows
with K (fix/break 1.10 → 1.61).
Traces, moves, and endorsements. A deliberation trace
T = (m1 , . . . , mN ) is an ordered sequence of N typed
moves. Each move carries a force, the identity of the agent
that made it (agents are indexed i = 1, . . . , I; here I=3), a
round index, and—depending on the force—an answer and a
target move. The move algebra has five forces; the dialoguegame protocol (Prakken 2005) used in all our experiments
emits three (Propose, Challenge, Concede; Appendix D).
We write K = {c1 , . . . , cK } for the K candidate answers, g
for a candidate under consideration, g ⋆ ∈ K for the unknown
correct answer (the datasets’ gold label), and π0 for a prior
over candidates (uniform in every experiment).
Each move reduces to a signed endorsement. This abstraction is intentionally minimal: aggregation depends on
who publicly supported or opposed which candidate, not on
how that support was linguistically expressed. A Propose
endorses its own answer; a Concede to another agent’s proposal endorses that proposal’s answer; a Challenge rejects
its target’s answer; self-concedes and abstentions carry no information. The extractor reads only the force, the agent, the
target, and the answer—never the move’s free-text surface,
never its self-reported confidence. Discarding the surface is

deliberate (judging argument text is exactly the attackable
step we set out to avoid); discarding the confidence gives

Properties

Five properties say why the model behaves as it does and
where it stops; statements, proofs, and numerical verification
are in Appendix C.
Property 1 (Calibration, scoped). If the prior and likelihood
match the data-generating process, the BDA confidence is
calibrated in Dawid’s sense.
In plain words: a posterior is the one kind of number that can
be calibrated, and the CV-selected prior is what earns it—a
one-parameter recalibrator, not a theorem’s gift. The qualifier
binds harder for the confusion model: with challenges at
K>2, Eq. (4) is a composite likelihood even at τ =1, with a
plug-in Dirichlet mean, so its calibration is a cross-validated
correction, never “by construction” (Appendix C).
Property 2 (The K=2 ceiling and collapse of the
finite-strength construction dissolve for K>2). At K=2,
when every agent shares one true reliability θ⋆ ∈ ( 12 , 1)
and θi is integrated under the CV-selected finite-strength
prior of Eq. (3), the posterior on g ⋆ converges to a ceiling
strictly below one, and with a symmetric prior (α0 =β0 ) it
never leaves the prior at all. Both are artifacts of the Betafunction symmetry at K=2; for K>2 the (K−1)−(Ni −ei (g))
factor of Eq. (1) makes the log-odds grow without bound, so
the posterior learns even under a symmetric prior.
In plain words: binary BDA is honestly humble and a symmetric prior is a trap; for K>2 the same growth makes the
one-coin over-confident, and the confusion model’s temperature absorbs it (the clean-K>2 fix, Section 6). The ceiling is
a property of our finite-strength construction, not of binary
aggregation—a hierarchical, growing-strength θi posterior
would pin the orientation; we keep the CV-selected s because it is the recalibrator (Appendix C).
Property 3 (Confidence invariance). The posterior is invariant, bit for bit, to any rewriting of the per-move self-reported
confidences.
Sycophantic confidence inflation (Fanous et al. 2025) cannot
move BDA: permuting every confidence across all 4,500
traces changes no posterior by a bit; an adversary who
changes what the moves endorse is Section 7’s subject.
Property 4 (Bounded prior sensitivity, regime-dependent).
The posterior is Lipschitz in the prior parameters. At the
uniform-error K=2 ceiling the Lipschitz constant does not
vanish as evidence grows; for the confusion model at K>2
the Dirichlet prior instead washes out at the per-agent rate
O(λ/Ni ), dominating only in the sparse regime.
A misspecified prior is damped, not always forgotten: not
at the K=2 ceiling (refit across task families), while the
confusion’s washes out—predicting that at light contamination, where the off-diagonal is under-determined, the fewerparameter one-coin recovers faster (Property 5).
Property 5 (Scope of the robustness mechanism). Reliability weighting requires the unreliability to be identifiable:
present in the labeled calibration window, stationary into
deployment, and attached to a stable seat.

Experimental Setup

Data. We use three binary claim-verification datasets
(MedClaim, StrategyClaim, TruthfulClaim; the ArgLLMs
evaluation family of Freedman et al. 2025), n=500
each; PubMedQA (K=3; yes/no/maybe) (Jin et al. 2019),
class-balanced n=300; and a four-way subset of MMLU
(Hendrycks et al. 2021), n=500. Each dataset is generated
under three seeds (proposers at temperature 0.7, the MoA
convention): the binary pool is 4,500 trials; results are seed
means, with paired bootstrap for head-to-head tests (dispersion in the appendix).
Council and models. The primary council is three
heterogeneous chat models—gemma-3-27b-it, phi-4
(14B), gpt-4.1-nano, fitted binary reliabilities ≈
0.74/0.68/0.68; the sub-8B open-weight council is Appendix H. All deliberate for three rounds with full visibility, so a persuasive seat can sway every trace reader: a
round-0 Propose, then two rounds of {Propose, Concede,
Challenge} under a protocol-automaton schema, identities
stripped; every aggregator sees the same traces.
Aggregators. We select baselines to span the principal
methodological alternatives to BDA rather than variants of
one approach: direct vote aggregation (plurality majority);
deliberation-based aggregation (MoA and MoA-Lite (Wang
et al. 2025), which synthesize the proposer surfaces; SelfMoA (Li et al. 2026), six samples of one model; ArgLLMs
(Freedman et al. 2025), which verifies each of the task’s own
candidates (fn. 1), never reading the council); supervised discriminative aggregation (logistic stacking, an ℓ2 regression
on the same per-seat tallies ci (g), di (g) BDA reads, and a
gated vote, plurality restricted to seats whose calibration accuracy clears chance); and latent reliability estimation (unsupervised Dawid–Skene EM, one-coin EM on the deployment
pool with no labels). BDA enters as shared, per-agent, and
confusion. One asymmetry favors the baselines: their evaluator/synthesizer runs on a stronger model (gpt-4.1-mini)
than any council seat; the MoA-family confidences are nearconstant (calibration N/A).
Metrics and model selection. We report accuracy; the
ECE under ten equal-width and fifteen equal-mass bins
(ECEew , ECEem ; Appendix B); the Brier score; and a
10,000-resample paired bootstrap giving confidence intervals and pwin . All supervised aggregators’ hyperparameters are chosen by nested, leak-free CV on the same label
budget—outer folds produce the reported numbers, inner
folds select the hyperparameters, no test label influences a
choice that scores it—on the proper Brier score (ECE selection is degenerate; Appendix D). Free-text K>2 answers
are scored by a candidate-restricted extractor with a single
frozen LLM judge for prose-only commitments; parse rates
and the judge ledger are in Appendix D.

Clean Data: Honest Accounting

Table 2 holds three clean-data findings—one against us, reported with equal prominence.
Calibration at zero cost, and a decision gain. BDA peragent reaches ECEew 0.016 against majority’s 0.160 (the
confusion posts the best Brier), at no marginal cost and
no recalibration step—the cross-validated conjugate prior is
the recalibrator (Appendix E). And weighing voices changes
which answer wins: per-agent BDA beats plurality by 1.71
points in every paired resample, a gain a monotone recalibration of a fixed decision cannot touch.
What BDA does not win. Granting the baselines the same
label budget, leak-free isotonic recalibration (Zadrozny and
Elkan 2002) collapses everyone’s calibration error, and a
recalibrated ArgLLMs then leads on Brier atop its accuracy
lead. On clean data BDA is not the best aggregator money
can buy—it is the best it need not buy: the only free arm with
a decision gain and no recalibration stage. The case rests on
what happens next.

calibration (0.051/0.037, matching the full model), because
a seat’s several moves per task are correlated and counting

8

Discussion and Limitations

What the results support. The best-calibrated zero-cost
confidence, a decision gain recalibration cannot produce, and

leading accuracy under a persistent adversary on two of three
domains. Claims we do not make: clean accuracy dominance
(recalibrated ArgLLMs wins, at a price); positional-K>2
under attack (stacking); post-recalibration calibration dominance (recalibrated majority); unconditional robustness.
Which arm to deploy. Ship the confusion model where
answer labels carry stable cross-task identity, re-fitting periodically: it matches the one-coin clean, dominates every zerocost arm under validation-exposed attack, and is calibrated
under fire; positional options pass its edge to the stacker,
open answer spaces to the one-coin. Its two exposures—light
sleeper contamination and a between-fit policy shift—are
measurably announced by row drift (twice the noise floor at
a fifth of a window; Appendix F); the policy-robust fallback
is the one-coin.
Scope of the formulation. Although evaluated on multiLLM councils, BDA is not tied to that architecture. The formulation applies to repeated multi-agent decision processes
that expose protocol-typed, agent-attributed commitments
over a shared candidate space and permit agent reliability to
be estimated across tasks. The council setting is one instantiation of this broader inference problem.
Limitations, stated as assumptions. Supervision. BDA
consumes labeled calibration data (≈ 25 labels, amortized;
baselines get the same budget); label-free settings exclude
it. Persistence. The adversary must misbehave in the calibration window and keep misbehaving; a sleeper inherits our
trust—a narrow exposure (a fifth of a window recovers it;
Appendix F), mitigated by re-fitting. The confusion and the
stacker need strictly more: the error structure must persist,
and a policy shift costs both their entire edge (Appendix F).
Identifiability. Moderate-dose rotation defeats every tracereading arm. Heterogeneity. Near-homogeneous reliabilities
reduce the per-agent model to a noisier shared BDA (Appendix H). Model. Move independence is an idealization the
temperature absorbs; abstention is untested.

9

Conclusion

A council’s deliberation record, interpreted as agentattributed probabilistic evidence over candidate answers, becomes a problem of joint inference over answer correctness and per-agent reliability. BDA realizes this formulation
through the classical annotator model of Dawid and Skene
(1979); with its confusion refinement, it provides what current aggregators lack: a probability calibrated as the answer
set grows and a decision that learns to discount, even invert,
an unreliable voice. Open are aggregation-aware adversaries
(policy shift and rotation bound them), online re-fitting, hierarchical and semi-supervised estimation, and—the trace
being typed—temporal-logic verification of the deliberation
itself. Pay a small, amortized supervision cost to know whom
to believe.

B.2

Reading the council, and how each method is
attacked

This subsection makes two things precise that the rest of the
study rests on: what it means for a method to read the council,
and why injecting the adversary role ρ of Appendix F into
each method’s own generation is the sound way to attack it.
Every method is aggregation over LLM evidence. Fix a
task: a question q, a candidate set K, and an unobserved gold
g ⋆ . The council is the three agents A1 , A2 , A3 whose threeround deliberation produces the typed trace T . A method—
any aggregator or baseline—is a map

Council-reading. A method Φ reads the council if its verdict depends on the trace: some T ̸= T ′ (same q) give
Φ(T, q) ̸= Φ(T ′ , q). It is non-reading if it factors through the

the council, not a defense of it.

The redundancy asymmetry, and its control. The one
quantity ρ does not equalize is the fraction γΦ of a method’s
evidence it corrupts. For the readers γ=2/3 and a clean seat
survives; for Self-MoA γ=1, because its “ensemble” is six
draws of one model, so compromising the model corrupts every sample; for ArgLLMs the whole generated tree. This is
forced by architecture—a single-pipeline method has no redundant channel—and it is exactly the property under study,
so a bare “Self-MoA collapses, BDA survives” would conflate redundancy with reliability weighting. We separate the
two by holding both fixed: MoA is also a three-model reader,
attacked at the same γ=2/3 and also keeping one clean seat,
yet it falls from 0.72 to 0.48 because its synthesizer trusts the
persuasive arguments, while BDA holds (0.75 to 0.73). The
BDA−MoA gap at identical architecture and identical budget
is the reliability-weighting effect with the fraction asymmetry removed; the single-pipeline collapses are the separate,
architectural point.
Two modeling choices. (i) For MoA, ArgLLMs, and SelfMoA the aggregation stage is itself an LLM—a synthesizer,
a strength evaluator—and ρ leaves it clean, touching only
the proposers, generator, or samples. This is generous to
those baselines: it grants them an uncompromised internal
check, which is precisely why ArgLLMs degrades rather
than collapsing. BDA needs no such grant, its Agg being the
closed-form posterior, with no LLM to compromise. (ii) On
K>2 the ArgLLMs attack is gold-routed: it uses g ⋆ to undermine the correct option and bolster each wrong one, a
per-option-optimal attack stronger than the commit-to-one
council adversary (both are gold-aware; the optimization is
not). This cuts against ArgLLMs, and we flag it so the K>2
comparison is read with the asymmetry in view.

Methodology, Implementation, and
Reproducibility

This section explains model selection in plain terms, documents four ways an evaluation of this kind can fool itself, and
records the implementation, storage, and cost ledger.

D.1

Leak-free nested selection, on a proper score

Every BDA hyperparameter is chosen by nested crossvalidation, and the two nesting levels do different jobs. The
outer level splits the data into five folds stratified by the gold
label; each outer fold is held out in turn, and the numbers we
report are computed only on these held-out rows. The inner
level operates entirely within an outer fold’s training part: it
runs its own five-fold split to choose (b, κ, s) for the per-agent
model, or (µ, s) for the shared model (the κ=0 slice), by the
Brier score. Because the inner level never sees the outer heldout rows, no test label ever influences a choice that later scores
it. The reliabilities ri are re-fitted on each outer training part
alone. The per-agent grid is b ∈ {0.52, 0.55, 0.58, 0.60},
κ ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}, s ∈ {4, 8, 16, 32}.
Why the Brier score, and not ECE. On a class-balanced
binary task the constant forecast p̂ ≡ 0.5 has an expected calibration error of 0: minimizing ECE is satisfied by refusing to
say anything. An ECE-selected grid duly chooses the degenerate symmetric prior—the collapse of Property 2—precisely
because that collapse produces a constant 0.5 confidence. The
Brier score is proper: it penalizes the lack of resolution and
avoids the trap. We flag this because ECE-selected calibration methods are common and inherit the pathology; the grid
also excludes b=0.50, the collapse boundary.

D.2

Run variance

LLM serving is stochastic even at temperature 0, so we draw
each dataset under three independent generation seeds (proposers at temperature 0.7, the MoA convention) and report
means over seeds, with a 10,000-resample paired bootstrap
on the pooled per-task records for head-to-head deltas. The
full tables carry the standard deviation across seeds.

D.3

Scoring pitfall: extracting a committed
answer

The free-text baselines (the MoA family, and—before the
faithful multi-option fix below—ArgLLMs) emit verbose answers on the K>2 tasks. The common “last capital letter”
heuristic is correct for a terse answer (“The answer is C.”)
but reads the final prose letter of a long justification, and
it scored all three baselines below the 0.25 chance floor on
four-way MMLU—a parsing artifact, not model failure. We
re-extract every stored answer with a candidate-restricted
parser (a bolded letter, then “answer is X”, then a leading
“X.”, then the first standalone candidate; for word labels,
the first word-boundary candidate). A residual of MMLU
answers commit to an option only in prose: “the authority

to coin money” names the option “Coining money” without
ever writing its letter. Scoring these as wrong understates the
baseline, so we pass exactly this residual to one frozen LLM
judge (gpt-4.1-nano, temperature 0), which reads the option texts and the answer and returns the committed letter or
NONE; genuine non-commitments (empty answers, refusals,
off-topic text) return NONE and stay wrong. Every judge verdict is cached, so the scoring is reproducible from the raw
answers at no cost. On clean MMLU the judge maps 65 of
MoA’s 227 unmapped answers to a committed option, raising
its accuracy from 0.605 to 0.640; the other 162 are genuine
non-commitments and stay wrong. Parse rates are disclosed
with every table (Table 5); sub-chance baseline accuracy is
a useful tripwire, and we recommend it as a standard check.

D.4

Implementation pitfall: the faithful
multi-option baseline

Freedman et al. (2025), footnote 1, extends claim verification
to multiple-option questions by “generating a set of candidate answers and applying our method to each of these.” The
candidate answers are the question’s own options; ArgLLMs
generates its argument tree for each and never consults an
external council. Feeding ArgLLMs the council’s proposer
surfaces as its candidate set instead produces two artifacts.
It returns a proposer paragraph as the answer (the parse
problem above), and it made ArgLLMs appear attackable on
K>2, because corrupting the proposals corrupted its candidate set. The faithful implementation—verify the task’s own
options—restores 100% parse and makes ArgLLMs councilinvariant on every K, exactly as it already was on binary. Its
clean K>2 accuracy changes in both directions: PubMedQA
rises from an understated 0.369 to 0.516, and MMLU falls
from an overstated 0.682 to 0.565 (it had been borrowing
signal from the council’s proposals). We report the faithful
numbers throughout, and its true attack surface is the fair
attack of Appendix F.

D.5

Design pitfall: the vocal adversary

An adversary instructed to maintain its wrong position every round complies by re-proposing—about 2.9 moves per
task against the genuine members’ one—and under per-move
counting that volume alone over-weights it (plurality falls to

0.29; per-agent BDA is inflated to 0.996 by a thrice-repeated
inverted signal)—an artifact of move volume, not persuasion.
The stealthy adversary of Appendix F deliberates naturally—
one move per round, conceding to allies—which removes the
artifact at the source: full-trace and one-vote-per-agent analyses agree. Volume effects are an easy way to fool oneself
when evaluating a trace aggregator.

D.6

Scoring pitfall: degenerate ties under attack

The label-permutation control of Appendix F.11 exposed
a subtler artifact in our own baseline. Under the live k=2
coalition, up to 22% of the stacker’s PubMedQA predictions
presented identical feature rows for two candidates (both
un-endorsed once the genuine seat is flipped), so its top
two scores tied exactly; a lexicographic tie-break by candidate string happens to correlate with the coalition’s wronganswer choices, depressing the stacker there by about 0.11.
The seeded-random tie-break we use throughout removes the
artifact (0.643 → 0.75 on live PubMedQA k=2), shrinking
the confusion-versus-stacking gap accordingly. The confusion model has zero exact ties on any live cell (its train-fitted
candidate prior and asymmetric rows break every symmetry), and the one-coin’s exposure is bounded by its measured
permutation invariance (|∆| ≤ 0.007). The general lesson:
under an adversary that empties a candidate’s endorsement
profile, argmax tie-breaking is part of the aggregator, and a
deterministic rule can silently correlate with the attack.

E.5

What fixes clean K>2: temperature, not the
matrix

The confusion model selects its Dirichlet concentration λ
and temperature τ jointly by the same inner cross-validation
on Brier (Table 10, left). On clean K>2 it drives λ high—
shrinking the learned confusion back toward the uniformerror one-coin—while τ falls well below one. The control
confirms the reading (Table 10, right): a per-agent one-coin
with a single cross-validated τ and no learned off-diagonal
already recovers the calibration (ECEew 0.30 → 0.05 on
PubMedQA, 0.18 → 0.04 on MMLU), matching the full
confusion model, at accuracy unchanged from the one-coin
(tempering is argmax-preserving). The temperature is the
clean-K>2 calibration fix. Under the live k=2 coalition
the story inverts: the tempered one-coin cannot match the
confusion’s accuracy (0.734/0.038/0.672 on binary/PubMedQA/MMLU against 0.892/0.840/0.721), because that
gain is the learned matrix inverting a systematic coalition,
and—being τ -invariant—no temperature can produce it.

E.6

Label efficiency of the supervised family

The confusion model carries K(K−1) free off-diagonal parameters per seat against the one-coin’s one, so does “about
25 labels” still hold? On clean pools, yes, and for the stacker

and gated vote too (Table 11): accuracy is flat from n=10
at every K (the one-coin-centered Dirichlet shrinkage does
its job), and at n=25 the confusion’s K>2 calibration is already several times better than the one-coin ever gets (ECEew
0.091 against 0.355 on PubMedQA), with MMLU settling
by n≈50–100. The caveat is adversarial, not clean: under attack the off-diagonal must be estimated from attacked data,
and the sleeper axis (Appendix F.8) already prices that—the
confusion needs the turn in more of its window than the
one-coin.

F

The Adversarial Study

This section documents the adversary end to end: the conditions, the verbatim role prompts, the full synthetic grid,
the calibration picture under the live attack, the invariance
checks, and the fair attack in full.

F.1

Threat model and conditions

Of the I=3 seats, k ∈ {1, 2} are adversarial. A persistent adversary occupies the same seats on every task, including the
calibration folds; a rotating adversary corrupts freshly random seats per task. The synthetic (count-level) attack redirects a corrupted seat’s endorsement to a random wrong answer with probability pwrong (full grid in Table 12). The live
attack makes the adversarial seats real LLMs with a stealth
role, on all five datasets and three seeds (13,800 traces).

F.2

The stealthy coalition adversary

The two role prompts are reproduced verbatim—beside the
honest prompts they piggyback on—in Appendix I; each is
prepended to the standard council prompt, so the adversary
sees everything a genuine member sees.
Three design decisions, each forced by a measured failure.
(i) Anti-answer, not fixed-wrong: forcing a specific wrong letter is ignored by the model on about 75% of MMLU tasks (it
argues its own preferred wrong instead); the anti-{correct}
framing lets it choose a semantically defensible wrong, keeping answer and reasoning coherent. (ii) Coalition compatibility: two adversaries with different chosen wrongs still recognize each other as allies against {correct} and concede
rather than fight. (iii) Natural volume: one move per round
(Appendix D.5).
Measured compliance. The fraction of adversary proposals that are anti-correct (round 0 / maintained across rounds,
pooled over datasets) is: binary 91.6/90.4% at k=1 and
87.0/86.5% at k=2; PubMedQA 100% at k=1 and 99.9%
at k=2; MMLU 99.8/99.8% at k=1 and 94.3/94.3% at
k=2. Targeting is strategic: on MMLU at k=2, adversaries
challenged truth-supporting moves 56 times against 18 challenges of allies, and conceded to allies 34 times against 6
truth-concessions. On binary tasks adversaries occasionally
get persuaded by a strong correct argument (8 to 13% round-0
non-compliance)—a realistic property we report rather than
suppress.

F.3

Dose–response (synthetic), full grid

This synthetic study isolates the count-level mechanism
behind the live results: an adversary corrupts a fraction

pwrong of a seat’s informative moves (from pwrong =0, clean,
to pwrong =1, always wrong), and Table 12 reports accuracy on the binary pool across three regimes. Majority and
shared BDA degrade monotonically as pwrong rises—neither
can single out the corrupted seat. Per-agent BDA is nonmonotone: it dips when the adversary is only mildly unreliable (near chance, where the bad seat is least identifiable) and
then rises as the adversary becomes reliably wrong, its fitted
weight turning negative so its corrupted votes are inverted
into evidence for the truth. The confusion model tracks it
under the persistent coalition and ends higher at k=2 (0.947
at pwrong =1). Rotation (bottom block) splits the two BDA
parameterizations instructively. The per-agent one-coin falls
monotonically—its prior map is centered on the council
mean (µi = b + κ(ri − r̄)), and a rotating adversary depresses every seat equally, so the centering erases the signal.
The confusion model fits absolute per-seat rows, and at high
dose every seat’s row is genuinely anti-diagonal (each seat
is corrupted on k/I of tasks), so it inverts the whole council
and recovers (0.554 at the pwrong =0.4 valley, back to 0.811
at pwrong =1): rotation defeats per-seat identification, but a
strong rotating coalition is still council-level identifiable. The
honest unlearnable regime is moderate-dose rotation, where
no trace-reading arm beats plurality.

F.4

Live attack: calibration under fire

Accuracy is in main Table 2; the calibration picture (Table 13)
is equally one-sided among the council-reading arms—and
it is where the confusion model shines. The tempered confusion posterior is genuinely calibrated under attack (ECEem
0.045–0.109 across all six cells, with no recalibration step),
where the one-coin per-agent is merely least-bad and plurality’s confidence becomes badly over-confident. The discriminative stacker’s softmax scores are also reasonable but
trail the confusion on four of six cells. ArgLLMs, read faithfully, is invariant on every K and keeps its clean calibration;
its multi-option confidence is a margin, already poorly calibrated on clean K>2.

F.5

Rounds under attack: the gain is round-0
weighting

Does the deliberation itself contribute to robustness? For every attacked cell we truncate each trace to round 0 and re-run
the identical pipeline with leak-free refitting on the truncated
evidence (the truncation of Appendix E’s rounds diagnostic,
the same collapse basis); round-0 one-coin is the classical
supervised weighted vote of main Proposition 1, so Table 14
is the weighted-vote / full-trace / confusion comparison. The
answer is unambiguous: round-0 aggregation is more accurate under attack in eleven of twelve cells—the one-coin by up
to 20 points (binary k=2), the confusion by 3 to 13—because
rounds 1–2 are where contagion lives (Appendix F.14): the
coalition’s challenges flip genuine seats, and reading those
rounds imports the corrupted evidence. The live-attack defense is therefore carried by round-0 reliability weighting; the
typed trace earns its keep in the reliability estimator, the calibration, and the clean-K>2 decision value (main Section 3),

The sleeper: a contamination axis

Per-agent BDA is supervised, so its sharpest threat is a
sleeper: a seat clean throughout the labeled calibration window that turns hostile only at deployment inherits a positive
weight and is trusted. We measure the exposure along the
natural axis—the fraction f of the calibration window in
which the seat has already turned, i.e. how far a periodic
re-fit has caught up with a seat that turned partway through
(Figure 7). Deployment is the full persistent k=2 coalition;
the calibration set is a disjoint split in which each task carries
the adversarial trace with probability f and the clean trace
otherwise, and we fit the reliabilities and hyperparameters
on it and predict the held-out deployment. At f =0, the pure
sleeper, per-agent BDA collapses to 0.17—below majority’s
0.37: it confidently follows the seats it learned to trust, and
the confident weighting makes it worse than a plain vote. But
the exposure window is narrow. Once the calibration captures
20% of the adversary’s persistence the seat’s fitted reliability crosses the council mean, its vote weight flips negative,
and accuracy jumps to 0.45; 30% gives 0.56, and the curve
climbs to 0.63 by f =0.4, plateaus near 0.66, and reaches the
aware 0.72 at f =1. The assumption BDA needs is therefore
not that the seat misbehaved throughout calibration but that
the window is re-fit often enough to see it turn—and periodic
re-fitting is the cheap mitigation, bounding the exposure to
the interval before the next fit rather than the damage within
it.
The same axis, swept per arm (Figure 7), exhibits the bias–
variance trade-off that the Dirichlet washout of Appendix C.5
predicts. At light contamination the fewer-parameter onecoin recovers fastest (0.45 at f =0.2 against the confusion model’s 0.26): the adversary’s off-diagonal is underdetermined there, and the concentration prior dominates.
Once the turn is well inside the window the ordering inverts—
the confusion model overtakes from f ≈0.6 and reaches 0.90

F.9

Recalibration erases the calibration edge, not
the accuracy

Given the same 25-label budget, leak-free 5-fold isotonic recalibration (Zadrozny and Elkan 2002) is applied to every
confidence-emitting arm (Table 15). On clean data it collapses everyone’s calibration error to a common floor—a
recalibrated majority reaches ECEew 0.006 on binary, erasing BDA’s edge. It cannot recalibrate accuracy: under the
persistent coalition a recalibrated majority is perfectly calibrated at ECEew ≈ 0 but only 0.37/0.21/0.43 accurate, while
BDA confusion holds 0.89/0.84/0.72. This is why we do not
headline clean calibration: BDA’s durable advantages are accuracy under attack and zero cost.

F.13

The monitoring result: row drift announces
the change

The deployment recommendation leans on an alarm—
“exposures announce themselves as drift in the fitted rows”—
which we now measure rather than assert. The statistic is the
mean per-(seat, class) total variation between confusion rows
fitted (fixed λ=10, so drift is not confounded with hyperparameter selection) on two labeled windows; its noise floor is
the same statistic between two disjoint clean half-windows
(conservative: half-size windows overstate sampling noise).
Along the sleeper axis the drift is monotone in the contaminated fraction f —0.036, 0.074, 0.105, up to 0.358 at
f =0.1, 0.2, 0.3, . . . , 1—against a floor of 0.033: a refit window carrying a fifth of the turn already raises the alarm at
more than twice the floor, at exactly the exposure where the
confusion cannot yet exploit the data (deployment accuracy
0.26 at f =0.2, Figure 7). The alarm precedes the recovery,
which is what makes “fall back to the one-coin and re-fit”
actionable. Across the policy boundary the single-refit drift
is 0.202 (PubMedQA) and 0.193 (MMLU) against floors
of 0.068 and 0.110—detected at the first labeled refit. One
caveat is honest: at f =0.1 the drift (0.036) sits at the floor,
so a turn occupying less than roughly a tenth of the window
can hide; the exposure of Appendix F.8 is bounded by refit
frequency, not eliminated.
