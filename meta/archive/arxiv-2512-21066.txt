Abstract
Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) reveals how explanatory variables relate to a response variable, yet
communicating XAI outputs to laypersons remains difficult, limiting trust in AI-based predictions. Large
language models (LLMs) can translate technical explanations into accessible narratives, but iterative
refinement of XAI explanations by an autonomous LLM agent remains unexplored. This study proposes an
agentic XAI framework that combines SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) with iterative refinement
by a multimodal LLM and tests it as an agricultural recommendation system on rice yield data from 28
fields in Japan. From a SHAP result, the agent explored additional analyses across 11 refinement rounds
(Rounds 0–10). Crop scientists (n = 12) and LLM judges (n = 14) scored every round on seven criteria:
Specificity, Clarity, Conciseness, Practicality, Contextual Relevance, Cost Consideration, and Crop Science
Credibility. Both groups found that refinement raised the average score by 30–33% over Round 0, peaking
at Rounds 3–4, after which quality declined, below the starting point for crop scientists. Refinement
therefore requires strategic early stopping, which challenges assumptions of monotonic improvement.
Criterion-level trajectories indicate a bias–variance trade-off. Early rounds lacked Specificity (bias),
whereas excessive iteration eroded Conciseness and raised Cost Consideration through ungrounded
economic reasoning (variance). The LLM judges overscored every criterion by 1.4–2.3 points but largely
preserved the experts’ ranking of rounds (Spearman ρ = 0.58–0.90), so screened LLM judges can flag the
quality peak despite unreliable absolute scores. Trustworthy agentic XAI also needs expert-anchored
screening of LLM judges and transparent, verifiable refinement records.

1.

Introduction

Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) has become a standard framework for AI-driven data analysis in
science. By quantifying the importance of explanatory variables and their relationships with the target,
through feature importance and dependence plots, XAI lets researchers move beyond predictive accuracy
to understand which factors are associated with the response variable (Rajbongshi et al., 2026; Manafi
Varkiani et al., 2025; Ryo, 2022; Molnar, 2020; Lundberg & Lee, 2017; Ribeiro et al., 2016). Yet
communicating XAI outcomes to laypersons remains difficult, which hinders trust in AI-based predictions
(Heider et al., 2026; Schiller et al., 2025; Lipton, 2018; Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2017). In agriculture in
particular, this communication gap directly limits the trust that growers and agronomists place in datadriven decision support, and thus the adoption of otherwise capable AI tools (Edet & Chowdhury, 2026;
Gardezi et al., 2023).
Large language models (LLMs) can, in contrast, foster science communication with laypersons (Galaz et
al., 2025). LLMs can transform technical explanations into natural-language narratives, generate
counterfactual scenarios, and hold interactive dialogues that align with users’ knowledge structures (Chen
et al., 2025; De Clercq et al., 2024). LLM-based advisory systems have proved practical in applied domains.
For instance, an agricultural advisory system has served over 15,000 farmers with an approximately 75%
success rate in providing actionable guidance (Singh et al., 2024). Evaluations of conversational LLMs in
agricultural extension roles show that LLMs can provide high-quality, structured responses that often
surpass those of human extension agents in consistency and comprehensiveness, although they need
grounding in local data and validation protocols before they can be relied on for critical decisions (Ibrahim
et al., 2024; Tzachor et al., 2022). Recent advisory systems address the need for grounding by retrieving
agricultural knowledge before generating an answer (Sawant et al., 2026), which on a benchmark of
agricultural questions reduced hallucinations and improved factual consistency (Ravindran et al., 2026).
Generative AI use has also been linked to greater advisory effectiveness among extension agents
(Gouroubera et al., 2026), and LLMs have been proposed as a means of translating the outputs of geospatial
AI into actionable advice under human oversight (Gakhar & Singh, 2026).
Given this narrative capability, a few studies have combined LLMs with XAI to explain data-driven
discoveries in plain language (Bilal et al., 2025). Key predictors found by a SHapley Additive exPlanations
(SHAP) analysis, for instance, can be described in natural language. However, no previous study has had
domain experts assess XAI–LLM integration, so its usefulness remains unknown. Nor is it clear how far
the AI should elaborate its explanation of the XAI results. We argue that elaboration generally improves
explanations but faces a trade-off between simplicity and comprehensiveness. A simple explanation of the
system is easier for laypersons or domain experts to digest, but it may discard critical information.
Conversely, an overly comprehensive description may blur important points. We therefore hypothesize that
explanation comprehensiveness has an optimum, by analogy with the bias–variance trade-off in machine
learning. A simplistic explanation gives the user biased information, whereas an overly extensive
explanation overwhelms the user’s capacity to digest it.
To test this hypothesis, we introduce an ‘agentic XAI’ approach. Among possible definitions, we define
agentic XAI as an approach in which an AI agent searches for the best explanation of modeled patterns by
iteratively refining a natural-language description. Agentic AI is an emerging technique in which a
multimodal LLM operates as an autonomous agent capable of reasoning through multiple steps, retrieving
external knowledge, and validating its outputs through feedback loops (Acharya et al., 2025; Hosseini et
al., 2025; Sapkota et al., 2025). Recent implementations such as ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) and AutoGen
(Wu et al., 2023) have shown that agentic architectures work across diverse domains, and frameworks for
agriculture now organize agents into closed loops of perception, decision-making, and action (Davcev et
al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). One agentic decision support system for smart agriculture has also applied SHAP
and LIME to make its models transparent (Swati et al., 2026). To our knowledge, however, no study has

had an agent iteratively refine its explanation of XAI results, or examined how the quality of that
explanation changes as refinement proceeds. Our ambition is to enable smoother knowledge transfer from
data science to domain experts and practitioners by providing explanations that are both technically rigorous
and practically meaningful.
As a case study, we investigated agricultural systems, where complex interactions between soil properties,
meteorological conditions, and management practices make it hard to translate AI outputs into actionable
guidance for farmers (Holzinger et al., 2022; Gardezi et al., 2023). We generated recommendations through
11 refinement rounds (Rounds 0–10) and had them scored on seven quality metrics by crop scientists (n =
12) and by LLM-as-judge evaluators (Zheng et al., 2023) (n = 14). The first question was whether overall
quality follows the inverted U-shaped pattern that the bias–variance analogy predicts as refinement
progresses. The two evaluator panels also let us ask a second question with direct practical stakes, namely
to what extent scalable LLM judges can reproduce expert judgment in absolute scores, in the ranking of
rounds, and in the location of the quality peak.
Three findings emerge. First, refinement is not monotonic. Expert-judged quality rises by roughly one third
to a peak at Rounds 3–4 and then deteriorates below the unrefined baseline, tracing the hypothesized
inverted U. Second, the seven quality criteria follow distinct trajectories, in which five trace the inverted U
while conciseness declines from the outset and cost consideration improves without grounding data,
showing that the trade-off cannot be escaped by simple iteration. Third, LLM judges overscore all criteria
yet largely preserve the experts’ ranking of rounds, so a screened LLM judge can flag the quality peak even
though its absolute scores cannot be trusted. Together, these findings yield evidence-based design principles
for trustworthy agentic XAI systems, foremost among them disciplined early stopping.

2.

Materials and methods

2.1

Proposed framework

The proposed agentic XAI framework comprises three interconnected components that enable iterative
refinement of agricultural recommendations: (1) XAI analysis to elucidate yield drivers from a tabular
dataset (Fig. 1a), (2) multimodal LLM (MLLM)-driven iterative refinement of recommendations for
farmers (Fig. 1b), and (3) systematic evaluation across multiple metrics (Fig. 1c). In this architecture,
the MLLM behaves as an agent, progressively improving its outputs through self-reflection,
supplementary analyses, and iterative refinement.
All materials for reproducing the agentic XAI workflow are available on Zenodo:
https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17876329 (Yamaguchi et al., 2026). The repository includes the
“Prompts” folder and “PythonCode.ipynb”, which chronicles the code generation process from the initial
baseline (Round 0) through all refinement stages (Rounds 1–10). Additionally, the “Figures” and
“Recommendations” folders contain the complete sequence of visualization outputs and farmer
recommendations, documenting the step-by-step expansion of the agent’s analytical scope.
The theoretical foundation draws from cybernetic principles of feedback control adapted to knowledge
translation (Holzinger et al., 2022), and conceptually applies the ReAct paradigm (Yao et al., 2023)
wherein reasoning traces and analytical actions are interleaved to improve outputs. The framework
implements a self-reflective mechanism inspired by recent advances in iterative refinement
methodologies (Madaan et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2022). At each iteration, the MLLM analyzes its
previous outputs, identifies analytical gaps, generates supplementary visualizations and statistical
analyses through code generation, and produces enhanced recommendations. This approach exploits the
reasoning capabilities shown by chain-of-thought prompting, while domain-specific constraints keep the
outputs practically grounded (Pang et al., 2024).

Fig. 1. Framework architecture of agentic explainable AI (XAI). The system integrates machine learning

2.2

Dataset

We used a dataset collected from a Japanese farm that combined yield data with associated soil properties,
rice varieties, cultivation management practices, and meteorological data. The dataset was collected in
a single farm operation growing four rice varieties over three years (2021–2023) and comprises 28 fields,
of which 16, 24, and 26 were observed in 2021, 2022, and 2023, respectively. The fields are located in
Tomioka-machi, Futaba-gun, Fukushima Prefecture (37.34°N, 141.00°E) and cover approximately 7.5
ha in total (0.12–0.41 ha per field). We used four local rice varieties: Fukunoka (medium-height, used
for sake production), Gohyakugawa (very early-ripening, with short stems), Sakurafukuhime (earlymaturing, tall with strong stems), and Tennotsubu (medium-height, lodging-resistant).
Yield maps were generated from UAV imagery using a deep learning model (Xception) trained on
maturity-stage images with three spectral bands (green, near-infrared, red edge), followed by spatial
interpolation using the Inverse Distance Weighting method. The detailed methodology is described in
Yamaguchi et al. (2025a). Soil properties were measured annually using a tractor-mounted real-time
sensor system with hyperspectral capabilities (Kodaira and Shibusawa, 2013). Meteorological data were
obtained from a gridded climatological database provided by the National Agriculture and Food
Research Organization (NARO) (Ohno, 2014), with variables averaged across four growth periods: two
equal periods between transplanting and 30 days before heading (early/late vegetative stage), the 30-day
pre-heading period (reproductive stage), and the 35-day post-heading period (ripening stage).
All variables, including soil properties and yield, were averaged at the field level, resulting in 66 fieldyear observations across the three-year study period (n = 66). This dataset structure reflects typical
smallholder farming contexts in East Asia, where field-level variation in soil properties, management
practices, and environmental conditions creates complex yield-determining patterns that challenge
conventional analytical approaches. The variables used in this study are summarized in Table 1, and
their distributions are shown in Fig. S1.

2.3

XAI analysis to elucidate yield drivers from a tabular dataset

To establish the foundation for iterative refinement, we developed a Random Forest model for rice yield
prediction from tabular agricultural data and applied SHAP analysis to generate explainability
visualizations (Fig. 1a). These initial outputs served as the starting point for the LLM-driven iterative
refinement process described in Section 2.4.
2.3.1

Model development and configuration

All yield prediction models were implemented in Python 3.9.13 using scikit-learn 1.5.2, SHAP 0.46.0,
NumPy 1.26.4, pandas 2.2.0, and matplotlib 3.8.2. The complete package environment is available in
the accompanying repository, as described in the Data availability statement, to ensure reproducibility.
We employed a Random Forest regressor as the base model because of its robustness, interpretability,
and proven effectiveness in agricultural applications (Raharimanana et al., 2023; Yamaguchi et al.,
2025b). Model performance was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV) across six
hyperparameter configurations, systematically varying the number of estimators (100 or 200) and
maximum tree depth (None, 10, or 20): (1) 100 estimators with unlimited depth, (2) 200 estimators with
unlimited depth, (3) 100 estimators with a maximum depth of 10, (4) 200 estimators with a maximum
depth of 10, (5) 100 estimators with a maximum depth of 20, and (6) 200 estimators with a maximum
depth of 20. All models used a fixed random state of 42 as the seed for the pseudorandom number
generator to ensure reproducibility. We selected the configuration with the highest LOO-CV R² and
refitted it on the whole dataset as the final ML model.
The optimized Random Forest model, configured with 100 estimators and a maximum depth of 10,
achieved an R² score of 0.749 in the LOO-CV setting. The R² score on the training dataset for the final
ML model was 0.962 (Fig. S2). The gap between training and cross-validation performance indicates
some overfitting, but the cross-validation score shows adequate predictive capability for generating
SHAP-based explanations in this framework.
2.3.2

SHAP-based interpretation

SHAP (TreeExplainer) was applied to the final ML model to generate feature importance scores and
interaction effects (Lundberg & Lee, 2017). In agricultural contexts, this method has outperformed
alternative XAI approaches, providing global and local interpretability at low computational cost (Ryo,
2022; Kaler & Kaur, 2025). We analyzed the top 20 most influential features based on mean absolute
SHAP values. The primary visualization was a beeswarm plot showing the distribution of SHAP values
for each feature, providing insights into both feature importance and directional effects on yield
prediction.
SHAP analysis identified management timing variables, meteorological conditions during critical
growth phases, and soil nutrient parameters as primary determinants of yield (Fig. 2). This SHAP
beeswarm plot served as the input for the subsequent agentic XAI process, wherein the MLLM
iteratively analyzed and refined cultivation management recommendations across multiple refinement
rounds (Rounds 0–10).

2.4

MLLM-driven iterative refinement of recommendations for farmers

The agentic XAI framework uses a multimodal LLM to run an iterative analysis loop (Fig. 1b) that
implements principles from recent work on self-refinement and iterative reasoning optimization
(Madaan et al., 2023; Kumar et al., 2024). We employed Claude Sonnet 4 (Anthropic) with standard
settings: web search disabled, normal response style, and extended thinking disabled. In this sequential
workflow, the MLLM alternates between interpreting data, generating Python code that extracts new
statistical evidence, and updating its recommendations on that evidence.
The workflow consists of an initialization phase followed by a defined number of refinement iterations
(Rounds 1–10). The specific inputs and prompts for each stage are detailed below, with the three prompts
reproduced in full in Text S1:

2.5

Quality evaluation of recommendations

2.5.1

Evaluation design and protocol

Because agricultural advisory quality is hard to evaluate, we used two evaluator groups, human experts
(crop scientists) and AI systems (LLMs) (Fig. 1c). Twelve crop scientists evaluated the
recommendations so that domain knowledge, including Japanese farming conditions, practical
constraints, and established agronomic principles, entered the evaluation. These crop scientists were
PhD holders in agricultural sciences and current or former members of the Crop Science Society of
Japan. Participation was voluntary, and consent was obtained after the crop scientists had been informed
of the purpose of the study and of how their evaluation data would be used. The evaluations were
recorded and analyzed anonymously. This human expert evaluation was complemented by assessments
from 14 LLMs representing five major AI providers: OpenAI (ChatGPT 4o, ChatGPT 5.1 Instant,
ChatGPT 5.1 Thinking), Anthropic (Claude Sonnet 4, Claude Opus 4.1, Claude Sonnet 4.5), xAI (Grok
3 Fast, Grok 3 Expert, Grok 4.1), DeepSeek (DeepSeek, DeepSeek with DeepThink), and Google
(Gemini 2.5 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 3.0 Pro). Recent studies have demonstrated that properly
implemented LLM evaluators can achieve 75–85% agreement with human expert judgments (LLM-asjudge), particularly when using multiple models to mitigate individual biases (Zheng et al., 2023; Liu et
al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024a, 2024b).

Every evaluator, crop scientist or LLM, independently scored the recommendations of all 11 rounds
(Rounds 0–10) based on seven evaluation metrics. Drawing on agricultural decision support system
evaluation frameworks that emphasize accessibility, usability, and practical implementation (Zhai et al.,
2020), user acceptance and workload considerations (Htun et al., 2022), and economic value assessment
(Helps et al., 2024), we developed the seven evaluation metrics (Table 2), with scores ranging from 1
for very poor to 7 for excellent. Although each criterion in Table 2 is described by several guiding
questions, evaluators weighed these aspects holistically and assigned a single integer rating per criterion,
so that each round received exactly seven criterion-level scores from every evaluator.
Table 2. Evaluation metrics for assessing recommendation quality.
Metrics

Definition

Clarity

Is the response easy to understand, well-structured, and free from ambiguity? Is the logical flow clear?
Is technical terminology explained when necessary? Can farmers grasp the main points without
confusion?

Conciseness

Is the response succinct and to the point without unnecessary verbosity? Does it avoid redundant
information or excessive repetition while still being complete? Can busy farmers read and understand it
quickly during their work breaks?

Contextual Relevance

Does it avoid over-generalization or excessive theoretical discussion that doesn't fit the given
background? Does the response align with the actual conditions and context provided in the dataset
(location, varieties, field scale, management practices)? Is the advice appropriate for the specific
farming scenario described?

Cost Consideration

Does the response address economic factors such as costs, budget requirements, return on investment,
or cost-effectiveness? Does it consider the financial constraints that farmers typically face?

Crop Science Credibility

Is the response agronomically sound and reliable from a crop science perspective? Does it reflect
established scientific principles in agronomy, plant physiology, soil science, and crop management? Is
the information accurate and credible based on current agricultural research and best practices?

Practicality

Does the response offer actionable and feasible advice or solutions that can be realistically
implemented? Are the suggestions achievable with typical farm resources, skills, and time constraints?

Specificity

Does the response provide detailed and precise information rather than being vague or general? Does it
include specific numbers, measurements, varieties, methods, or step-by-step instructions that farmers
can directly reference?

Before the evaluation, the crop scientists received background information in writing and orally in an
online meeting, and the LLMs received the same information in writing. The instructions, the criteria as
they were presented and this background are reproduced in Text S2. This background described the
dataset context (Section 2.2) and the 37 input variables categorized into soil properties (7 variables),
agricultural management practices (10 variables), and meteorological conditions (20 variables across
four growth stages), with yield as the target variable (Table 1). Evaluators were told that the
recommendations came from an iterative process in which the LLM analyzed the tabular data and revised
its outputs over 11 cycles (Rounds 0–10), and that each response contained agronomic insights into the
relationships between explanatory variables and rice yield together with practical recommendations for
local farmers.
To minimize systematic biases such as position effects and verbosity bias documented in LLM
evaluation studies (Ye et al., 2024; Chern et al., 2024), we applied the same evaluation protocol to both
crop scientists and LLMs. For each evaluator, the sequence in which the 11 recommendations (Rounds
0–10) were presented was randomized, and the order used is given in Text S2. Each evaluator provided
independent assessments without knowledge of other evaluators’ scores or the iteration number being
evaluated, ensuring blind evaluation.

To keep the LLM evaluations independent of one another, all LLM evaluations were conducted in
isolated conversation contexts. For ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok models, evaluations were performed in
incognito/private mode to prevent any influence from previous conversation history. For DeepSeek and
Gemini models, which do not offer an incognito mode, evaluations were conducted in completely new
conversation threads with no prior conversation history.
2.5.2

Statistical analysis of quality trajectories

After collecting the assessments, we organized the evaluation data separately for crop scientist
evaluations (n = 12) and LLM evaluations (n = 14), treating each evaluator (or repetition) as an
independent replicate within its group. For each round (0–10), we computed an overall quality score for
each evaluator by averaging the seven evaluation metrics. For each metric and the overall score, we then
calculated descriptive statistics across evaluators within each group for each round (mean and standard
error of the mean, SEM).
To test whether recommendation quality differed across rounds, we conducted one-way ANOVA for
each metric and the overall score. The null hypothesis assumed equal means across rounds, and statistical
significance was evaluated at α = 0.05.
To assess whether recommendation quality exhibited an inverted U-shaped trajectory across rounds, we
fitted a generalized additive model (GAM) relating round number (X) to the round-level mean score
(Y):
Y = β₀ + f(X) + ε
where f(X) represents a smooth function estimated via penalized regression splines with smoothing
parameters selected by internal grid search.
Detection of an inverted U-shape was based on the first derivative of the fitted smooth, following
derivative-based change detection approaches for GAMs (Simpson, 2018; von Brömssen et al., 2021).
The derivative was evaluated by numerical differentiation over a dense grid of round values. We
classified a trajectory as inverted U-shaped when all of the following criteria were met: (i) exactly one
zero-crossing occurred within the observed range at which the derivative changed sign from positive to
negative; (ii) the derivative was positive on average in early rounds and negative on average in later
rounds; and (iii) the 95% confidence interval for the derivative excluded zero in both regions, indicating
a statistically robust increase followed by a robust decrease. The peak round was defined as the zerocrossing location, and the peak score was obtained from the fitted GAM curve at that round.
In our implementation, 95% confidence intervals for the GAM mean function were computed as
pointwise intervals from the fitted penalized spline model, using the estimated coefficient covariance
matrix derived from the (penalized) design matrix and the residual variance. Confidence intervals for
the first derivative were then approximated by numerically differentiating the lower and upper 95%
confidence interval curves for the mean function across the evaluation grid.
Additionally, to evaluate whether the nonlinear GAM provided a better description of the observed
trajectories than a simple linear trend, we fitted a linear ordinary least squares regression model as a
baseline:
Y = α₀ + α₁X + ε
Model fit was summarized using the coefficient of determination (R²). To formally compare model
performance while accounting for model complexity, we computed Akaike’s Information Criterion
(AIC) (Akaike, 1973) under a Gaussian error assumption for both the GAM and the linear model:
AIC = n[log(2π) + 1 + log(RSS/n)] + 2k

where n denotes the number of observations (rounds), RSS represents the residual sum of squares, and
k indicates the number of estimated parameters (effective degrees of freedom for GAM; k = 2 for the
linear model).
Following the widely cited guidelines of Burnham and Anderson (2002), models with ΔAIC < 2 are
considered to have substantial empirical support. We required the GAM to outperform the linear model
by at least 2 AIC units (ΔAIC = AICLinear − AICGAM > 2) to justify adopting the more complex nonlinear
model. The GAM was therefore adopted only when it both satisfied the derivative-based criteria for an
inverted U-shaped pattern and demonstrated substantially improved model fit relative to the linear
baseline. When this criterion was not met, the linear model was considered sufficient to describe the
trend and was used as the reference representation. This comparison-based procedure ensures that
nonlinear structure was adopted only when strongly supported by the data, thereby reducing the risk of
overfitting and avoiding circular inference regarding peak detection.
All analyses were conducted in Python 3.12.12 within the Google Colab environment. Data processing
and statistical analyses were performed using NumPy (v2.0.2), SciPy (v1.16.3), and Pandas (v2.2.2),
with visualization implemented in Matplotlib (v3.10.0). Generalized additive models were estimated
using pyGAM (v0.12.0). The analysis code archived in the Zenodo record re-runs every analysis
reported here, including the yield model of Section 2.3.1, in a single pinned environment (Python 3.11.9)
and reproduces the reported values.
2.5.3

LLM–expert agreement analysis

To quantify whether, and at which level of measurement, the LLM evaluators can stand in for the crop
scientists, we compared the two evaluator groups on each of the eight score series (seven criteria plus
their average), using the round-level group means (n = 11 rounds). Absolute agreement was assessed by
the mean score difference (LLM − crop scientists) with paired t tests and Cohen’s d, and by Lin’s
concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) (Lin, 1989) with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals;
Pearson’s r is reported alongside the CCC to separate linear association from absolute concordance.
Rank agreement, defined as whether the two groups order the 11 rounds consistently, was assessed by
Spearman’s rank correlation (ρ) of the round means, with block-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals.
Peak agreement was assessed by comparing the round at which each group’s observed mean score
reached its maximum.
Because a deployed advisory system would more realistically rely on one or a few LLM judges than on
a 14-model panel, we additionally benchmarked each LLM individually. For every LLM and criterion,
we computed the absolute difference between that LLM’s peak round and the crop-scientist consensus
peak round, and summarized each LLM by the proportion of criteria for which its peak lay within ±1
round of the expert consensus.

Results

3.1

Overall quality dynamics across refinement rounds

The overall average score (mean of seven evaluation metrics) showed a consistent inverted U-shaped
pattern across the 11 refinement rounds (Rounds 0–10) in both evaluator groups, with peak performance
occurring in early-to-middle iterations followed by substantial deterioration (Fig. 3, Tables S1 and S2).
One-way ANOVA indicated significant differences across rounds (p < 0.001), and GAM analyses
detected an inverted U-shaped trajectory for the average score in both crop scientists and LLMs (Table
S2), indicating improvement in the early-to-mid rounds followed by decline. In crop scientist evaluations,
the observed average score increased from 3.68 at Round 0 to a maximum of 4.91 at Round 3 (Δ =
+1.23), then declined to 2.64 by Round 10 (Δ = −2.26 from the observed peak) (Fig. 3a; Table S1). The
GAM also supported an inverted U-shaped trajectory with an estimated peak at Round 2.50 (estimated
peak value 4.523) and better fit than the linear baseline (ΔAIC = 9.25) (Table S2). In LLM evaluations,
the observed average score increased from 4.78 at Round 0 to a maximum of 6.21 at Round 4 (Δ =
+1.44), then decreased to 5.18 by Round 10 (Δ = −1.03 from the observed peak) (Fig. 3b; Table S1).
The GAM likewise detected an inverted U-shaped trajectory with an estimated peak at Round 3.53
(estimated peak value 6.028) and strong support relative to the linear model (ΔAIC = 13.42) (Table S2).

Metric-specific performance dynamics

Across metrics, the observed trajectories (Table S1) and GAM-based trajectory classification (Fig. 5;
Table S2) indicated three distinct patterns: (i) inverted U-shaped dynamics (background gray) for five
metrics, (ii) monotonic increases under linear trends (background orange) for Cost Consideration, and
(iii) monotonic decreases under linear trends (background blue) for Conciseness.

Five metrics (Specificity, Clarity, Practicality, Contextual Relevance, and Crop Science Credibility)

observed peaks (typically Rounds 2–4) in both crop scientists and LLMs (Table S1). GAM analyses
detected inverted U-shaped patterns for these five metrics in the crop scientists, and for four of the five
in the LLMs, with improved fit relative to linear baselines (ΔAIC > 2) (Table S2), supporting an early

4.

Discussion

4.1

Agentic XAI improved the recommendations in the early rounds

Our results demonstrate that the proposed agentic XAI framework substantially improved
recommendation quality in the early refinement rounds. Both evaluator groups independently identified
peak performance at Rounds 3–4, representing improvements of Δ = +1.23 (crop scientists) and Δ =
+1.44 (LLMs) over Round 0 (Fig. 3; Table S1). This trajectory aligns with recent findings in iterative
agentic workflows, where multi-turn reasoning loops rapidly converge to optimal solutions before
plateauing (Madaan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024).
By leveraging an LLM-driven agentic approach, our framework effectively bridges the persistent
“interpretability–usability gap” in XAI applications (Hegselmann et al., 2023; Bilal et al., 2025),
synthesizing raw SHAP-based outputs into intelligible narratives that facilitate practical decisionmaking for farmers (Wang et al., 2024). The convergent validation by both human domain experts and
LLM-as-judge evaluators (Table 4) strengthens confidence in these quality improvements and indicates
that the framework translates technical XAI outputs into actionable guidance.

4.2

Early stopping is necessary: the bias–variance trade-off

The improvement, however, was not monotonic, and our results support the hypothesized bias–variance
analogy in explanation comprehensiveness. In both evaluator groups, average quality followed an
inverted U-shaped trajectory identified by derivative-based GAM criteria, with an optimal range in the
early-to-mid rounds (Rounds 2–4) followed by deterioration (Fig. 3; Tables S1 and S2). In this framing,
early rounds lack analytical depth (analogous to bias), whereas continuing beyond the optimum
introduces verbosity and abstraction that obscure actionable insights (analogous to variance).
The bias side appears in Rounds 0–2. At Round 0, the MLLM interpreted the SHAP beeswarm plot (Fig.
2) alone, without supplementary quantitative analyses, and its recommendations lacked the specificity
and agronomic detail that actionable guidance requires (Fig. 4). The gains in six metrics (all but Cost
Consideration) from Round 0 to Rounds 2–4 reflect the analytical depth that refinement added (Fig. 5;
Table 3).
The variance side emerges from Round 5 onwards in several forms. Conciseness, which peaked early
and then declined in both groups, captures the growing verbosity that overwhelms lay readers (Fig. 5).
Beyond length, the MLLM drifted toward abstraction and generalization, producing recommendations
that were analytically sophisticated (Table 3) but detached from implementable practice. Cost
Consideration exemplifies this drift. Because the dataset contained no economic variables (Table 1), its
steady improvement rested on economic reasoning generated without grounding data, precisely the
elaborate but unfounded content that we hypothesized as “high variance” (Fig. 5). The late-round decline
of Contextual Relevance, Crop Science Credibility, Practicality, and Specificity in both groups shows
that the practical criteria are the most vulnerable to this degradation. They lost the concrete specificity
that makes recommendations actionable even as analytical sophistication increased, and their divergence
from Cost Consideration and Conciseness shows that the criteria cannot be optimized simultaneously by
iteration alone.
This pattern aligns with recent findings on diminishing returns in iterative AI systems, where models
lose substantial capabilities within limited refinement cycles because of reward overoptimization and
distribution shift (Zhu et al., 2024; Rafailov et al., 2024; Adnan et al., 2025). Recent studies of LLM

refinement point the same way. Without targeted feedback, most models fail to improve their answers
consistently over successive turns (Lee et al., 2026), and in document-level translation the gains from
refinement come mainly from fluency, with limited improvement in adequacy (Tan et al., 2026). In our
agricultural context, refinement optimized analytical comprehensiveness and economic completeness
while sacrificing the operational specificity and contextual grounding that farmer decisions require.
Early stopping after 3–4 rounds therefore outperformed extended iteration, extending emerging evidence
from machine learning research (Snell et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024) to knowledge translation, and it
should be treated as a design principle of agentic XAI rather than a tuning detail.

4.3

Deciding when to stop: LLM judges, screened panels, and human oversight

If early stopping is necessary, the practical question is who detects the peak. The agreement analysis
(Table 4) delimits what LLM judges can contribute. They cannot replace experts for absolute quality
assessment, because every criterion was overscored by 1.4–2.3 points and an LLM-reported score
therefore carries no calibrated meaning on the expert scale. What the LLM panel largely preserved was
the experts’ ordering of rounds, including the location of the quality peak, and detecting that peak is an
ordering task that is invariant to any constant shift in scores. Monitoring refinement therefore imposes a
much weaker requirement on an LLM judge than conventional LLM-as-judge applications assume, since
ordinal fidelity suffices and score calibration is unnecessary. In principle, then, the loop can be stopped
by an LLM judge, and the panel average did locate the expert peak within one round on every score
series.
In practice, however, relying on a single judge is risky, because individual judges differed widely (Fig.
6). The best judge located the expert peak within ±1 round on 87.5% of the score series and the worst
on 12.5%, and the worst judge was the model that had generated the recommendations, which echoes
the self-preference bias reported for LLM-based evaluation (Ye et al., 2024) and argues against letting
an agent grade its own refinement. Two safeguards follow. First, use several judges rather than one, and
screen them against expert-scored data: collect expert ratings once for a small pilot (in our case, 11
rounds scored by 12 crop scientists), benchmark candidate judges against the expert peak, retain only
those whose peak falls within ±1 round on the criteria of interest (Fig. 6), and exclude models that share
a family with the generating agent. The screened judges should also score against the same explicit
criteria as the experts, which in this study were the guiding questions of Table 2, because the criteria
given to an LLM judge are critical to its reliability (Yamauchi et al., 2026). Second, keep humans in the
loop at the decision point: during subsequent refinement runs, use the screened judges’ round ranking
as the stopping signal, halting once the running maximum stops improving, and route only the selected
output to experts for final validation. Compared with a fixed iteration budget, this hybrid procedure
respects the finding that quality trajectories differ across criteria (Fig. 5), and it reduces expert effort
from rating every round to validating a single output.
Two caveats bound this guidance. Screening presupposes at least one expert-scored pilot, so the
procedure economizes expert effort rather than eliminating it. And the procedure is operational guidance
distilled from a single case study, not a validated stopping algorithm; formal guarantees for judge-driven
stopping remain future work.

4.4

Keeping the agentic process transparent and accountable

Whoever decides when to stop, deploying agentic XAI raises risks beyond the choice of iteration count.
From a principal–agent perspective, the relationship between farmers and agentic systems introduces
information asymmetries and potential misalignment between system objectives and farmer interests
(Gabison & Xian, 2025), and unclear liability frameworks may impede adoption when agent-generated
advice leads to poor outcomes. Such concerns already surface in extension practice, where agents
hesitate to rely fully on generative AI out of concern for professional judgment and accountability
(Gouroubera et al., 2026), and where LLM tools, mostly developed by private firms, raise questions of
transparency and accountability (Edet & Chowdhury, 2026). Our results expose a specific vulnerability:
the agent has no internal mechanism for recognizing the groundlessness of its own reasoning. It
continued to generate increasingly complex analyses, including economic reasoning unsupported by any
economic data, while expert-judged quality deteriorated (Fig. 5; Table 3). Agentic generation and
execution of analytical code add further hazards, namely unintended data manipulation, accumulation
of analytical errors across iterations, and propagation of flawed analyses through successive rounds
without external validation (Raza et al., 2025).
The refinement process itself also introduces opacity that may reduce rather than enhance trust, even
though agentic XAI aims to improve interpretability. Non-deterministic execution flows and
unpredictable behavioral variability create “black box” challenges that hinder accountability and
comprehension (Moshkovich et al., 2025). Farmers must trust the original model, its SHAP
interpretation, the LLM’s self-generated analyses, the code producing those analyses, and the synthesis
of multiple information sources, and this chain lengthens with every iteration. Even optimally stopped
refinement therefore demands more trust than simpler advisory approaches.
Observability is the remedy that this study can offer. Systematic examination of intermediate outputs by
domain experts establishes observability into the agentic refinement process (Limonad, 2025;
Moshkovich et al., 2025). Archiving the agentically generated code, visualizations, and
recommendations of every round, as done in the Zenodo repository of this study (Yamaguchi et al.,
2026), lets experts verify each iteration’s analytical artifacts, identify analytical errors, and check
agronomic soundness. Transparency of the process is, in this sense, the precondition for the screened,
human-in-the-loop stopping described above to be trustworthy.

4.5

Methodological considerations and limitations

First, this study is based on data from a single crop system (rice) under specific geographical conditions
(Fukushima, Japan), which limits generalizability. Different agricultural systems may exhibit distinct
patterns of response to iterative refinement, particularly those with different complexity levels, decisionmaking timescales, or cultural contexts. The framework's performance with perennial crops, livestock
systems, or integrated farming approaches remains unexplored. However, the convergent patterns across
independent evaluator groups and the theoretical alignment with broader machine learning findings on
iterative refinement suggest that the early stopping principle may generalize beyond this specific context.
Second, the agentic XAI framework relied on a single generation model (Claude Sonnet 4), so the
observed refinement trajectories may be specific to its architecture or training data. The 14 evaluator
LLMs, although diverse, may also carry correlated biases. Models from the same provider or model
family often share training corpora and alignment objectives such as reinforcement learning from human
feedback, and may therefore share evaluation tendencies, so the consistency across evaluators may partly
reflect shared artifacts rather than independent validation. The per-LLM heterogeneity shown in Fig. 6

reinforces this caution, as agreement with the expert panel varied widely across judges and the judge
sharing its model family with the generating agent performed worst.
Third, the evaluation is based on recommendation quality assessed at the time of generation, without
field validation of implementation outcomes over time. While the quality metrics employed align with
established agricultural decision support system evaluation frameworks (Zhai et al., 2020; Htun et al.,
2022; Helps et al., 2024), the ultimate test of effectiveness lies in farmer adoption, implementation
fidelity, and agricultural outcomes. Field studies that implement recommendations generated at different
refinement levels would test our findings against agricultural outcomes and may reveal quality criteria
that our framework does not capture.

4.6

Future research directions

The generalizability of the bias–variance pattern and of the early stopping principle requires validation
beyond a single rice system. Other crops, livestock systems, and environmental management contexts
may exhibit different refinement dynamics depending on their complexity, decision timescales, and data
availability, and tests in medical, financial, or environmental advisory settings would reveal whether the
trade-off is a general property of knowledge translation systems (Holzinger et al., 2022; Wang et al.,
2024). Within the evaluation loop, three questions stand out. The judge-driven stopping procedure
proposed here needs formal validation and adaptive stopping criteria that respond to real-time quality
signals. The balance between automated LLM monitoring for efficiency, domain-expert validation for
credibility, and end-user evaluation for practical utility needs to be established in line with humancentered and responsible-AI principles (Holzinger et al., 2022; Shneiderman, 2020; Gardezi et al., 2022;
Dara et al., 2022). And the ultimate validation must come from end users, here farmers as laypersons,
who judge whether recommendations are understandable, actionable, and valuable for their decisions
(Zhai et al., 2020; Htun et al., 2022; Gardezi et al., 2023).
Future agentic XAI systems could also push the early stopping limit outward by incorporating additional
grounding data once the initial performance peak is reached. The degradation beyond Round 4 reflects
the finite information available to the agent. Integration protocols such as the Model Context Protocol
(MCP) (Anthropic, 2024) or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) architectures (Lewis et al., 2020;
Gao et al., 2023), which agricultural advisory systems have begun to use to ground their answers
(Ravindran et al., 2026; Sawant et al., 2026), could give agents access to real-time agricultural databases,
domain literature, or organizational knowledge when the initial refinement approaches are exhausted, so
that each data augmentation supports a further cycle of improvement before a new plateau is reached.

5.

Conclusion

This study asked whether an agentic XAI system that iteratively refines SHAP-based explanations produces
better explanations, and answered it with a case study in rice management judged by 12 crop scientists and
14 LLM judges. The framework was useful: refinement raised expert-judged recommendation quality by
about one third within three to four rounds.
The improvement was not monotonic. Beyond the peak, further iteration degraded quality below the
unrefined baseline through verbosity and ungrounded abstraction, and the seven quality criteria followed
divergent trajectories that no single iteration count can reconcile. Strategic early stopping is therefore a
design principle of agentic XAI, not a tuning detail.
Deciding when to stop can be delegated in part to LLM judges, which preserved the experts’ ranking of
rounds well enough to flag the peak. Their inflated scores and their heterogeneity, including the failure of
the generating model to judge its own output, mean that several judges screened against expert ratings and
combined with human validation of the selected output are safer than any single judge. All of this
presupposes a transparent process in which the code, figures, and recommendations of every round are
archived and open to expert scrutiny. Validating these principles across crops and domains, formalizing
judge-driven stopping, and testing recommendations with farmers themselves are the next steps.
