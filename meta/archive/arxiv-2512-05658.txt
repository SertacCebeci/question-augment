Abstract

arXiv:2512.05658v3 [cs.CL] 1 Sep 2026

Large Language Models with reasoning
capabilities have recently shown strong promise
for medical Question Answering. Current
approaches report competitive results on
English benchmarks through knowledge
distillation from large models, yet multilingual
coverage remains limited. We extend this line
of research by presenting a method to generate
multilingual reasoning traces that enrich the
distillation paradigm with factual information
from Wikipedia. Using a retrieval-augmented
generation pipeline over WikiProject Medicine,
we produce 500k reasoning traces in English,
Italian, and Spanish. The traces are generated
to solve medical questions drawn from MedQA
and MedMCQA, both of which we extend to
Italian and Spanish. We evaluate our approach
across in-domain and out-of-domain Medical
QA benchmarks, demonstrating consistent
performance gains in both few-shot in-context
learning and supervised fine-tuning settings,
achieving state-of-the-art results among
8B-parameter models across multiple model
families. With the aim of fostering the development of multilingual clinical decision-support
tools, we release the complete suite of resources:
reasoning traces, translated datasets, Medical
Wikipedia, and fine-tuned models.

Introduction

Medical reasoning with Large Language Models
(LLMs) is a rapidly evolving area aimed at
improving Natural Language Processing systems
in clinical settings. Generating explicit reasoning
traces, or chains of thought, has emerged as a
promising approach to guide models toward more
accurate answers (Wei et al., 2022). Moreover,
the development of models explicitly trained to
generate answers via complex, multi-step reasoning
traces has marked a notable achievement for
many domains (Plaat et al., 2025), while its direct
application to medicine presents unique challenges:

the context is usually incomplete, concepts can be
ambiguous, and there is a high degree of specialization. Such a scenario has fostered increased interest
in medical reasoning, although a few important
aspects remain underexplored. Recent approaches
(Chen et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Yu et al.,
2025) have primarily relied on distilling reasoning
traces from powerful models to train smaller
systems, suffering from heavy dependence on
synthetic data. Furthermore, multilingual reasoning
has been largely neglected. Only Chen et al. (2025)
proposes a bilingual approach, including English
and Chinese, that relies solely on distillation from
OpenAI’s models as a source of medical knowledge.
While achieving good performance, this approach
falls short in linking its reasoning to any sort of
external medical knowledge.
In this paper, we propose a methodology
to generate multilingual reasoning traces for
multiple-choice medical question answering, based
on the knowledge from the WikiProject Medicine1 .
We define reasoning traces as answer-conditioned
rationales that elaborate medical notions and
knowledge in order to reach a final answer to
a question. We evaluate such traces in various
settings and demonstrate that training on them
systematically yields state-of-the-art performance.
Our contributions can be summarised as follows:
• We present the first dataset of medical
reasoning traces for Italian, Spanish, and
English with the potential to be extended to
any language in Wikipedia.
• We provide comprehensive experiments that
demonstrate the effectiveness of applying our
reasoning traces to multilingual, multiplechoice medical QA via in-context learning and
supervised fine-tuning.

Related work

Reasoning with LLMs. In the context of Large
Language Models, reasoning refers to the ability to
answer questions by complex, multi-step processes
with intermediate steps (Zhang et al., 2025a),
producing a chain of thought before providing the
actual answer to the user. The first model explicitly
designed for this purpose was OpenAI’s GPT-o1
(OpenAI et al., 2024), whose development process
remains mostly undisclosed. Extensive work has

(Zhao et al., 2024), and skywork-o1 (He et al.,

benchmarks, these models have opened a line of research focused on the impact of reasoning in several
domains, including math (Ahn et al., 2024), physics
(Xu et al., 2025), and biology (Liu et al., 2025b).
Reasoning for the medical domain. Previous work
showed how medical-oriented tuning of foundational LLMs can lead to major improvements (Luo
et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024), even surpassing human
experts on some benchmarks (Singhal et al., 2025).
These results highlighted that domain-specific
alignment can be beneficial for medical tasks. More
recently, these findings have led to the integration
of domain-shift and reasoning capabilities. Huatuo
(Chen et al., 2025) was among the first released
models, trained via distillation on medical questions.
This approach was improved via test-time scaling
by Huang et al. (2025). Wu et al. (2025) introduced
the grounding of the distillation process in factual
medical knowledge (more details in Appendix A).

Methodology

We introduce a novel methodology for distilling
reasoning traces that are explicitly grounded in
Wikipedia medical knowledge. This involves
four steps: i) selecting medical questions, ii)
constructing a curated Knowledge Base (KB) of
reliable medical text, iii) enriching each medical
question with evidence retrieved from the KB using
retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020),
and iv) generating a step-by-step reasoning trace
that leads to the correct answer among the given
options with a high-capacity LLM.
3.1

Selection of Medical Questions

Datasets. The MultiMedQA benchmark introduced
in the Med-PaLM paper (Singhal et al., 2023),
including MedMCQA (Pal et al., 2022), MedQA
(Jin et al., 2021), PubMedQA (Jin et al., 2019), and
MMLU clinical (Hendrycks et al., 2021) is the main
attempt to standardize multiple-choice medical
QA datasets. Following prior work (Wu et al.,
2025; Chen et al., 2025), we focus our approach on
MedMCQA and MedQA, which are sets of exams
from India and the USA. Additionally, we include
MedExpQA (Alonso et al., 2024), as it is currently
the only manually validated multiple-choice
medical QA dataset available for Italian, Spanish,
and English. A summary of the selected datasets
is provided in Appendix B (Table 6).
Dataset translation. MedMCQA and MedQA are
English datasets. Given the multilingual objective
of our work, we translate each Question-Options
pair into Italian and Spanish using Qwen-2.5-72B
(Qwen et al., 2025), prompted with a 5-shot example
setup. To assess the quality of the translation, we employ back-translation: the obtained text is translated
back into English and compared against the original.
We compared the original and back-translated
question-answer pairs by means of COMET, chrF
(Popović, 2015), chrF++ (Popović, 2017), and
BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020) metrics. Prior
work has demonstrated that back-translation scores
can serve as a useful proxy for translation quality
and correlate with human judgments (Rapp, 2009;
Zhuo et al., 2023). Furthermore, the widespread
use of back-translation as a data augmentation
and validation technique (Sennrich et al., 2016;
Bourgeade et al., 2024; Sugiyama and Yoshinaga,
2019) supports its use for assessing translation
quality in Italian and Spanish. As reported in Appendix B.2, back-translation quality is consistently

high across languages, achieving BERTScore of
96.6, COMET 88.9, chrF 78.6, and chrF++ 77.9.
As a further validation of the back-translation
approach, we verify automatic metrics behavior on
medical questions which translation has been manually revised (MedExpQA). We employ the method
described above to translate English MedExpQA
into Italian and Spanish. We compare the automatic
translation to the manually revised translation using
the same metrics employed for back-translation.
The results (BERTscore 97, COMET 88, chrF 78,
chrF++ 75) are consistent with what observed on
MedQA and MedMCQA, supporting the hypothesis
that these metrics indicate good alignment.
Since automatic metrics may not fully capture
translation inaccuracies, we reserve the manually
validated multilingual MedExpQA dataset exclusively for testing, guaranteeing out-of-domain
evaluation on human-verified data.
3.2

Knowledge Base Generation

Our goal is to generate reasoning traces grounded
in factual medical knowledge across the three
languages. Hence, we require sources that comprehensively cover the range of medical specialties.
Moreover, to ensure a fair comparison of results
across languages, the underlying knowledge must
be aligned across all three of them. Accordingly, the
requirements for the KB are: i) reliability and diversity of the medical texts, and ii) parallel information
availability across the three languages. To satisfy
these conditions, and taking advantage of its open
collaborative editing model and of WikiProject
Medicine, we considered Wikipedia as our primary
source of knowledge, based on Smith (2020).
Medical-Wikipedia creation. Although previous
work on Medical QA has already tested Wikipedia
as a medical knowledge source (Alonso et al.,
2024; Xiong et al., 2024), our method to leverage
Wikipedia is novel. We construct our medical
knowledge base upon the WikiProject Medicine,
a Wikipedia initiative that aims to collect all pages
related to the medical domain. First, we collected
links to all the English-relevant pages4 . For each
page, we extracted the main text, infobox content,
and interlanguage links. Using the links, we
retrieved the corresponding pages in Italian and
Spanish. This procedure results in a multilingual
Medical-Wikipedia dataset in English, Italian, and

From Medical-Wikipedia to Knowledge Base.
We employ the constructed dataset to build
language-specific knowledge bases suitable for
querying to retrieve context to support answering
medical questions. To ensure a meaningful crosslingual comparison, we enforce consistency across
the three knowledge bases by retaining only pages
available in all three languages. While this filtering
step reduced the dataset size (particularly for
English) it resulted in nearly parallel multilingual
corpora. Although we cannot guarantee perfect
alignment in page content and information density
across languages, the resulting knowledge bases
ensure that the same medical topics are present in
all three languages. We segmented the collected
pages into chunks to construct the target knowledge
bases. Each section of a page is treated as a chunk.
Sections exceeding 5,000 words were discarded,
while sections containing fewer than 250 words
were merged with the preceding ones. We filtered
out irrelevant sections, such as “Bibliography” and
“External links” (a comprehensive list is provided in
Appendix C). An overview of the outcome of each

3.3

Retrieval Augmentation

Once the knowledge base is constructed, we
enriched each medical question from MedQA
and MedMCQA with the most relevant contextual information. Following the paradigm of
Retrieval-Augmented Generation, we pre-pended
the retrieved passages to the model input prior to
prompting (Figure 1). We pre-compute the embedding of each chunk in the three language-specific
knowledge bases, calculating their cosine similarity
with each Question-Options pair. We select the

top five most similar chunks as context. Embeddings are computed using the top-performing
model from the MTEB leaderboard (Enevoldsen
et al., 2025) retrieval task at the time of writing
(Qwen3-Embedding-8B by Zhang et al. (2025b)).
To address the issue of potential redundancy and
inclusion of irrelevant information in the context
highlighted by Wang et al. (2024), we prompt an
LLM (Qwen3-32B, Yang et al. (2025)) to rewrite
the retrieved chunks. This step ensures consistency
within the retrieved chunks, enhancing conciseness
and avoiding duplication. The prompt template and
more details on the results in Appendix B.1.
3.4

Generation of Reasoning Traces

We prompt an open-source reasoning model,
Qwen3-32B, to generate reasoning traces, providing
an input composed of four parts : i) the formatted
contextual information retrieved from the KB, to
provide factual knowledge on the medical topic; ii)
the medical question that defines the problem iii) the
answer options that constrain the space of reasoning paths; iv) the correct answer to guide the model
toward selecting the most appropriate path. We enforce the use of the correct answer, motivated by our
goal of generating the most accurate and informative
traces possible. To prevent the model from collapsing into simply stating the correct answer, we design
the prompt to encourage exploration of a space of
potential answers before reaching the conclusion.
The system prompt, with a structure based on the
findings of Wu et al. (2025), is described in Appendix B.1. We verify the traces’ conclusions by extracting the answer identifier via regular expression
matching and discarding the incorrect ones, as suggested by the “philosophy of verifiability” in Chen
et al. (2025). Notably, this resulted in discarding less
than 5% of the traces, meaning that our pipeline produces answers that usually lead to correct answers.

Traces Evaluation

We evaluate the usefulness of our generated
reasoning traces by measuring how effectively they
support Medical QA in both in-context learning
(ICL) and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) settings.
Our primary metric is downstream task accuracy,
which directly reflects whether the traces fulfill their
intended purpose. Nonetheless, in Section 5 we
offer a manual evaluation of our newly generated
reasoning traces by having 11 medical doctors
analyze the correctness of a randomly chosen
sample of question, option, reasoning triplets.
For both ICL (few-shot) and SFT experiments,
examples are drawn from the train splits of MedQA
and MedMCQA. Out-of-domain evaluation is
conducted on the combined train, validation,
and test splits of MedExpQA, guaranteeing
out-of-distribution testing on human-validated
multilingual data. An additional evaluation is also
performed on the test splits of MedQA and MedMCQA (Appendix B, Table 6), guaranteeing that the
models do not, at any time in the evaluation step,
see the correct answers from the training split used

to generate the reasoning traces. Finally, we also
report a comparison with previous state-of-the-art.
Baselines. To determine if our traces are helpful,
we need to define a baseline to compare against.
We adopt a few-shot evaluation setting, where each
prompt includes two example questions with their
options and correct answers, followed by the test
question. To ensure a competitive baseline, the
examples are selected through similarity search
in an embedding space: for each test question, we
retrieve the most similar training questions (along
with their answers) to use as few-shot examples.
Statistical analysis We employ statistical tests to
determine the significance of the differences in performance. For each comparison, we treat the combination of model, dataset, and language as an independent unit, representing a single instance of the
treatment (i.e., exposure to the reasoning traces). We
test the hypothesis that the treatment produces an effect greater than zero. Since multiple statistical tests
are performed on the same data, we apply a Bonferroni correction to adjust the significance threshold.

4.1

In-Context Learning

We determine the impact of utilizing our traces
via ICL. While baseline examples include only
questions, options, and answers, our ICL setup also
includes the reasoning traces in the 2 examples.
The retrieval strategy remains identical across both
settings, ensuring that any observed improvements
can be solely attributed to the inclusion of our traces.
We test several models from diverse families in their
instructed versions and report the results in Table 2.
Results. We observe that our traces enable all models to generate more accurate answers compared
to the baseline, as reported in Table 2. The average
effect of our traces among datasets and languages
is shown in Figure 2 (top-right). For medical
questions in MedMCQA and MedQA, we observe
an average increase in accuracy of +7 to +10 points
across all languages. The out-of-distribution dataset
(MedExpQA) is the one that benefits the least, with
the case of Gemma-3-27B and Qwen-3-32B not
getting any benefit. Nevertheless, the overall impact
among models on this dataset is positive, with an
average increase in accuracy of +1.8 points. To
verify its statistical significance, we apply a paired
t-test on the performance deltas between models
trained with and without our traces, obtaining a
p-value of 0.02, which falls below the standard
significance threshold of α = 0.05.
Disentangling reasoning from knowledge. When
we expose models to our traces via ICL, we select
the two examples that are the most similar to the
target. These example traces therefore include
medical knowledge that is required to answer the
test question. By removing the similarity-based
selection of the examples – and picking them
randomly instead – we can estimate how much of
the increase in performance is due to the reasoning
capabilities acquired via ICL by itself, disentangling
the effect of the similarity-based example traces.
For computational constraints, we re-run all ICL
with 2 random examples on 9/10 models (excluding
Llama-70B), MedQA (in-distribution), and the
test set of MedExpQA (out-of-distribution)5 .
Randomly picked examples have a positive impact
on 8/9 models (Llama-1B, the weakest model by
a margin), with an average impact of +2.5 points
(MedExpQA), and +4.7 (MedQA). This result
highlights that reasoning capabilities acquired

4.2

Supervised Fine-Tuning

To measure how our reasoning traces influence model learning, we fine-tuned models in
a supervised setting. Due to computational
constraints, we restricted our experiments to
Llama3 and Qwen3 in their 8B versions. We
trained each model on examples formatted as
<q>{question}</q><o>{options}</o>{trace}.
We follow Thapa et al. (2025) by using “reasoning intensive” examples for training as opposed
to “knowledge intensive”, and Liu et al. (2025a),
which suggests prioritizing MedQA sampling ratio
over MedMCQA. Our final fine-tuning dataset
comprises 5,837 traces from MedMCQA and 5,594
from MedQA per language, for a total of 34,293
examples across English, Italian, and Spanish.
We fine-tuned the models for 3 epochs on two
H200 GPUs, with a per-device batch size of 32,
AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 5e − 6,
a cosine scheduler, and a warm-up ratio of 0.1.
Training on these settings took 1.5 hours per model.
Results. We observed that training on our traces benefits both Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B,
yielding an average accuracy improvement of +3.9
points when compared with the baseline (Table 3).
The average impact is significantly higher than
zero with a p-value < 0.005. Interestingly, for
in-distribution datasets (MedMCQA, MedQA), the
gains are smaller than those seen in ICL settings.
We attribute this to the nature of the traces selected
as two-shot examples: they are highly relevant for
the test question, acting as targeted guidance for
instructed models that are capable of handling long
prompts. In contrast, fine-tuning aims to generalize
such knowledge, which turns out to be less effective
than delivering the exact relevant information at
inference time. We further analyze this behavior in
the next section, showing that combining fine-tuned
models with our traces in few-shot prompts leads to
the best overall performance. On the other hand, the
opposite is observed for out-of-distribution performance (MedExpQA), where SFT obtained a higher
average increase among languages with respect
to the baseline (+4.8 against +1.8 in ICL). This is
reasonable, as SFT enables the model to internalize
reasoning patterns and medical knowledge that
generalize beyond the specific examples provided at

inference time. In contrast, ICL relies on retrieving
highly relevant in-context examples, which may be
less aligned with out-of-distribution questions.

4.3

Comparison with previous work

As described in Section 2, the main previous efforts
to generate reasoning traces for medical question answering are Huatuo (Chen et al., 2025), MedReason
(Wu et al., 2025), and m1-m23k (Huang et al., 2025).
They released 19.704, 32.682, and 23.493 questiontrace pairs (licence apache 2.0), respectively, on
which they trained models using SFT and reinforcement learning techniques. We aim to compare our
traces against those from previous work. First, we
evaluate their effect in i) few-shot prompting and
ii) supervised fine-tuning. Finally, iii) we evaluate
each method under its strongest reported configuration. This final step goes beyond a trace-level
comparison, demonstrating that our best model
consistently outperforms any other alternatives.
We do not perform a comparison to the generation mechanisms presented in the literature, but to
the data resources existing work released 6 . A summary of the characteristics of the traces we employ
is reported in Table 4. Our traces’ length is comparable to MedReason (226 vs. 370 words on average)
and substantially shorter than m1 (1123 tokens),
while providing a much larger pool of ICL examples
(179k vs. 32.7k for MedReason and 23.5k for m1).
While m1 and Huatuo use GPT-4o for the generation,
m1 and us use open-source models (DeepSeek R1,

Ablation on SFT

To better understand the sources of performance
gains, we conduct an ablation study comparing three training settings: i) standard SFT on
question–answer pairs, and ii) SFT on question–reasoning–answer triplets. To quantify the
contribution of external medical knowledge to the
reasoning traces, we generated iii) an additional
set of reasoning traces without incorporating information from the Medical-Wikipedia by prompting
Qwen3-32B without retrieving context from the
knowledge base. This setup allows us to disentangle
the effects of externally injected medical knowledge
from those of reasoning capabilities distilled from
Qwen3-32B alone. Due to the training computational cost, we constrain the analysis to one model

(Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct). Finally, our ablation
study includes training the model individually
on each language versus training it jointly on all
languages simultaneously (see Appendix E for
more details). As reported in Table 5, training on
reasoning traces yields an average improvement
of +2.7 accuracy points over standard SFT, while
grounding traces in Medical-Wikipedia provides
an additional +3.3 points gain, showcasing how
structured reasoning and medical knowledge from
Wikipedia help to improve performance.

Conclusion

This paper introduces a method for generating
multilingual medical reasoning traces based
on knowledge extracted from the WikiProject
Medicine. Our contributions are threefold: first, we
present the first dataset of medical reasoning traces
for English, Italian, and Spanish, generated by
answering questions from MedQA and MedMCQA;
second, comprehensive experiments show that
exposure to these traces improves both in-context
learning and supervised fine-tuning, consistently
achieving state-of-the-art performance; third, we
release a multilingual reasoning model alongside
three new resources: a collection of 500k+ reasoning traces, translated versions of established medical
QA benchmarks, and a manual evaluation by medical doctors assessing the reasoning quality of a
randomly sampled subset of the generated traces.
Comparative evaluation against previous
work confirms that our approach yields stronger
accuracy in multilingual, multiple-choice medical
question answering, with gains observed even on
English-specific benchmarks. An ablation study
quantifies the contributions of reasoning, factual
knowledge injection, and multilingual training. A
qualitative, expert-driven analysis of our best model
further reveals that residual errors stem primarily
from limitations in the model’s medical knowledge
rather than from reasoning failures. Together, these
findings underscore the value of knowledge-based
multilingual reasoning traces as a scalable path
toward more reliable clinical decision-support tools.

Limitations
Our work has some limitations. First, we focus on
only three languages, namely, English, Italian, and
Spanish, whereas multilingual medical reasoning
could be extended to a much broader set of
languages. Second, we rely on Wikipedia as our
primary source of medical knowledge due to its
cross-lingual parallelism, though more authoritative medical sources could further strengthen
grounding. The use of Wikipedia may introduce
Wikipedia-derived biases into the multilingual
training, and the possible selection bias introduced
by filtering traces solely based on the correctness of
final answers. Furthermore, the English knowledge
base is significantly larger than the others due to the
nature of Wikipedia pages. Third, our fine-tuning
experiments are restricted to 8B-parameter models
because of computational constraints; scaling to
larger models remains unexplored. Finally, we
limit our study to multiple-choice medical question
answering, leaving open-ended clinical reasoning
tasks for future work.

Ethical considerations
Despite the promising results, our work presents
some potential risks. First, reasoning traces may
contain factual inaccuracies, and incomplete clinical considerations, even when grounded in external
medical knowledge. This is particularly critical
in healthcare, where incorrect recommendations
could negatively affect clinical decision-making.
Our manual analysis represents a first step to assess
this dimension. Second, reasoning traces should
not be interpreted as faithful explanations of the
internal decision process of the model. While they
may improve interpretability, they can also create
a false sense of reliability or transparency.

Related Work on Medical Reasoning

Chen et al. (2025) proposed Huatuo, among the first
works to investigate the generation of reasoning
traces for medical QA. Huatuo relies on automatic
generation of verifiable problems from multiplechoice medical QA datasets. The generated traces,
distilled from closed-source LLMs, are used to train
models via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) in both Chinese and English. The authors found that models trained on
those traces outperform their base counterparts on
medical QA, even if the sole source of medical
knowledge is a closed-source LLM.
Wu et al. (2025) advance beyond pure distillation
by linking each question with factual medical knowledge. Such knowledge is derived from PrimeKG
(Chandak et al., 2023), a knowledge graph of 17k
diseases designed for medicine analysis in English.
The model trained on those traces (MedReason)
achieves slightly better performance than Huatuo,
highlighting that medically grounded data can enhance reasoning capabilities for the English language.
Huang et al. (2025) further advanced the field
focusing on the effect of test-time-scaling (Muennighoff et al., 2025). The authors train a model on
a few thousand examples generated via distillation,
highlighting that the quality of the data is what matters.
Other recent works follow the approach of generating traces and training on them via SFT or RL. Sun
et al. (2025) build on distillation approaches with
architectural adjustments for trace generation, while
Yu et al. (2025) construct hundreds of thousands of
synthetic medical instructions derived from Common Crawl. While innovative, this method relies
heavily on LLM-generated content with limited human validation and uncertain underlying medical
quality. Liu et al. (2025a) explore eliciting reasoning capabilities from models without distillation
and avoiding intensive use of resources, similarly to
Thapa et al. (2025), who present a method to optimize the use of existing traces. Finally, Wang et al.
(2025) provide a comprehensive review of recent
advances.

Multilingual vs monolingual SFT

Experiments demonstrated that integrating multilingual data during fine-tuning leads to improved
accuracy on downstream tasks. To further examine
this phenomenon, we analyze cross-lingual transfer
effects throughout model training.
We train Qwen3-8B using reasoning traces from
individual languages as well as from all three languages put together, following the training protocol
outlined in Section 4.2. The results indicate that
multilingual training consistently matches or surpasses the performance of single-language training.
In contrast, models trained exclusively on one
language exhibit worse accuracy when evaluated
on other languages (see Figure 5).These findings
underscore the positive impact of cross-lingual
learning and emphasize the value of incorporating
diverse languages into the training data.
