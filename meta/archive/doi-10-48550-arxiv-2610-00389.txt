A BSTRACT
Open-ended query generation lacks standard answers, thus necessitating an effective reward mechanism. Pointwise scoring rubrics provide limited information
about the relative quality of sample answers under the same prompt; merging multiple rubric judgments into a single score may also mask the differences between
these answers. We propose MatrixReward, which constructs rewards from a
rollout-by-rubric win-rate matrix obtained by comparing every pair of sampled
responses under each rubric. The spread of each matrix column captures how
strongly that rubric distinguishes the current rollouts, while correlations between
columns reveal rubric repetition; together, these statistics yield data-dependent
rubric weights. We combine these weights with the prior weights of rubrics. After
column normalization and weighting, the observed per-rubric maxima and minima
define positive and negative ideal profiles. Each rollout’s distances to these two
ideals determine its relative-closeness quality reward. Evaluated using Qwen38B on four open-ended query-answering benchmarks, MatrixReward achieves an
average score of 63.02, outperforming the strongest baseline by approximately
2.0%. These results support the idea that matrices derived from relative comparisons can be used to construct rewards more reasonably for open-ended generative
reinforcement learning.1

I NTRODUCTION

Reinforcement learning has become an important approach to improving language models beyond
their initial capabilities, but its effectiveness depends on the quality of the reward signal (Ouyang
et al., 2022; Dubois et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2024). In verifiable tasks, such as mathematics and
programming, a correct answer or successful test provides a concrete target. In open-ended generation, feedback must instead account for instruction following, factual grounding, relevance, and
presentation (Zheng et al., 2023; Gunjal et al., 2026). A response may be fluent yet uninformative,
or detailed yet poorly grounded. Effective rewards must distinguish these qualities among responses
to the same prompt.
Rubric-based rewards offer a promising foundation by expressing these requirements as explicit
evaluation rubrics (Gunjal et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a; Liu et al., 2026c). Rather than leaving
quality entirely to a holistic judgment, rubrics identify the aspects that an evaluator should consider.
However, clear rubrics do not automatically produce informative training signals: an evaluator must
still determine how well each response satisfies them (Zheng et al., 2023; Tripathi et al., 2025), and
those assessments must ultimately guide a policy update. The distinction matters for group-relative
policy optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024): the training signal depends on differences among
sampled responses, not merely on whether each response appears satisfactory in isolation.
Many rubric-based reward systems assign pointwise scores to responses independently. Although
rubrics specify what to judge, a numerical score lacks a stable anchor when a query has no reference answer; the same value may reflect different quality standards across queries or evaluators

To address these problems, we propose MatrixReward. As illustrated in Figure 1, rubric-wise pairwise judgments retain a separate win-rate profile for each response. The matrix reveals which rubrics
distinguish the current rollouts and which provide overlapping information. We combine this evidence with query-specific weights, then compare the resulting profiles with positive and negative

Our contributions are as follows:
• Rubric-wise comparison matrix. We introduce MatrixReward, a reward framework for
open-ended group-based RL. By comparing every pair of rollouts under each query-specific
rubric, it constructs a rollout-by-rubric win-rate matrix that retains each response’s relative
quality.
• Distance-based reward construction. We derive group-specific rubric weights from column dispersion and cross-rubric correlation, then combine them with query-specific importance priors. After normalizing and weighting the matrix columns, we use their observed
maxima and minima as positive and negative ideal profiles. For each rollout, the Euclidean
distance to the negative ideal divided by the sum of its distances to both ideals gives a scalar
quality reward, favoring profiles near the positive ideal and far from the negative ideal.
• Four-benchmark evaluation. Training Qwen3-8B with MatrixReward yields a 63.02 average across four benchmarks, exceeding the strongest pairwise, pointwise, and weightaware baselines by 1.23, 6.93, and 5.60 points, respectively. Ablations examine weight
extraction and fusion, ideal-profile selection, distance metrics, and judging cost.

R ELATED W ORK

Rubric-based reward evaluation. Rubrics make the standards for judging open-ended responses
explicit, but do not by themselves establish a common numeric scale across queries or evaluators.
Rubrics as Rewards (RaR) uses instance-specific rubrics in on-policy RL, with both weighted rubriclevel assessments and rubric-conditioned holistic judgments (Gunjal et al., 2026). Chasing the
Tail improves discrimination among high-quality responses by constructing rubrics from diverse
examples (Zhang et al., 2026a); OpenRubrics derives rubrics from preference data (Liu et al.,
2026c). These works improve what is judged, while the choice between pointwise rubric scores and
pairwise comparisons remains consequential for reward construction.
2

Pairwise and rubric-wise comparisons. Pairwise judgments replace an isolated score with a
choice between responses. Tournament-GRPO converts rubric-guided comparisons among samequery rollouts into tournament rewards (Yang et al., 2026); ArenaRL uses process-aware, multilevel rubrics within tournament-based relative ranking (Zhang et al., 2026b). OpenRS uses adaptive
rubrics to guide pairwise comparisons and aggregates the resulting preferences for RL supervision
(Jia et al., 2026). MAPLE combines aspect-wise pairwise judgments while estimating evaluator
reliability and aspect importance for assessment rather than online reward construction (Jinji et al.,
2026).
Adaptive weighting of reward dimensions. Human-assigned rubric weight need not coincide
with a rubric’s current usefulness for learning. POW3R makes this distinction explicit and reallocates rubric-level reward weights using contrast among policy rollouts (Tyagi et al., 2026). SAW
uses batch-level reward variation to adapt dimension weights (He et al., 2026), while SA-MRPO
downweights saturated objectives at the advantage level (Wang et al., 2026b). AMRP shifts weight
toward dimensions with greater shortfall, volatility, or stalled progress (Yuan et al., 2026). These
methods establish that weights can respond to training state; they differ in the statistics used and in
whether they account for redundancy between rubric dimensions within the current response group.
Aggregation of multi-dimensional feedback. After assigning accurate weights to the rubric, it’s
still necessary to consider how to construct a suitable reward for reinforcement learning training
from the evaluations of multiple rubrics. GDPO normalizes reward dimensions separately before
aggregation (Liu et al., 2026b), and GD2 PO additionally handles conflicting cross-dimension advantages (Liu et al., 2026a). OpenRS aggregates rubric-level preferences outside the judge (Jia
et al., 2026), whereas AMRP identifies how fixed scalar projections can conflate distinct reward
profiles (Yuan et al., 2026).

E XPERIMENTS

4.1

E XPERIMENTAL SETUP

Training and data. We build the RL pipeline on verl2 and optimize Qwen3-8B (Yang et al.,
2025) with GRPO. The reward judge is Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (Qwen Team, 2026). We sample 2,560
queries from UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2023). Using the prompt in Appendix A.1, DeepSeek-V4Flash (Xu et al., 2026) generates three to five query-specific rubrics from each query and its four
responses provided in the dataset. The weights attached to these rubrics supply p in Eq. 5 and are
also used for weighted aggregation of the pointwise reward. Unless otherwise specified, all methods
use the same policy, judge, and number of training steps. We sample G = 8 rollouts per query and
report results after three training epochs (480 steps). Detailed hyperparameters and training settings
are provided in Appendix B.
Benchmarks. We evaluate on AlpacaEval 2 (Dubois et al., 2024), Arena-Hard v2 (Li et al., 2024),
WildBench (Lin et al., 2025), and WritingBench (Wu et al., 2026), using Qwen3.6-35B-A3B as
the judge. All scores are on a 0–100 scale. Avg refers to the average of four benchmarks.
4.2

C OMPARISON WITH BASELINES

We compare MatrixReward with three groups of baselines: pointwise, pairwise, and weightcalibration methods. The pointwise baselines—GRPO, Dr. GRPO, GOPO, GDPO, GD2 PO, and
DAPO—score each rollout independently using query-specific rubrics (Shao et al., 2024; Liu et al.,
2025; Choi et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b;a; Yu et al., 2026). The pairwise baselines RRC-SCR, ArenaRL, Tournament-GRPO, and Pairwise Rank construct rewards from comparisons between rollouts
(Wang et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026b; Yang et al., 2026). The weight-calibration baselines SAW,
POW3R, and AMRP adjust how rubric dimensions contribute to the reward (He et al., 2026; Tyagi
et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026). Table 1 reports the benchmark results.
MatrixReward averages 63.02 across the four benchmarks, exceeding RRC-SCR by 1.23 points,
DAPO by 6.93 points, and SAW by 5.60 points. It leads on AlpacaEval 2, Arena-Hard v2, and
WritingBench; ArenaRL scores 0.04 points higher on WildBench. The implicit variant achieves the
second-highest average of 62.23. To assess the contribution of rubric-wise pairwise observations,
the pointwise + MatrixReward variant constructs its matrix from independent absolute scores for
each rollout and rubric. Its 57.86 average is 5.16 points below the pairwise MatrixReward variant,
but it is still 1.77 points better than the best pointwise baseline. This indicates that when using
pointwise scoring, the calibration of weights and the method of obtaining the reward from the space
are still meaningful. The corresponding Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct results are reported in Appendix C.

C ONCLUSION

We introduced MatrixReward, a framework for constructing relative rewards from rubric-wise comparisons in open-ended group-based RL. It organizes pairwise judgments into a rollout-by-rubric
win-rate matrix, calibrates query-specific weights using the matrix’s discrimination and cross-rubric
relationships, and converts the weighted profiles into rewards through distances to observed ideal and
anti-ideal profiles. After training Qwen3-8B with MatrixReward, the model averages 63.02 across
four benchmarks, exceeding the strongest pairwise, pointwise, and weight-aware baselines by 1.23,
6.93, and 5.60 points, respectively. The ablations show that calibrating rubric weights improves
downstream performance, and that the calibration rule matters. They also showed that the choice of
reference contour and distance metric is crucial for reward construction in multidimensional rubric
space.

BASELINE R EPRODUCTION AND A DAPTATION

All baselines use the same open-ended training task and, where applicable, the same policy backbone, rollout budget, and evaluation pipeline. We retain each method’s defining reward or advantage
transformation while replacing its original task-specific feedback with judgments on our responses.
GRPO and Dr. GRPO. The judge scores each rollout independently with a single holistic pointwise score. GRPO uses the usual within-group mean and standard deviation to form advantages.
Dr. GRPO uses the same observations but removes division by the group standard deviation, uses a
fixed-horizon token-sum loss, and disables the actor KL term (Shao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). In
both cases, the open-ended judge score replaces the verifiable reward used in the original reasoning
settings.
DAPO. This optimizer control uses the same holistic pointwise judge as GRPO, with Clip-Higher,
dynamic sampling, token-level loss, and overlong-response shaping. The open-ended judge score
replaces DAPO’s original verifiable reward (Yu et al., 2026).
GDPO, GD2 PO, and GOPO. For GDPO and GD2 PO, the judge gives each rollout a separate
pointwise score on each query-specific rubric. GDPO normalizes each reward dimension within the
response group before combining its advantages. GD2 PO retains reward-wise advantages but filters
cross-dimension sign conflicts using its signal-to-noise rubrics and scales the retained query signal
(Liu et al., 2026b;a). GOPO instead aggregates the pointwise rubric scores into one scalar per rollout
and replaces score magnitude with the within-group ordinal order before policy optimization (Choi
et al., 2026).
RRC-SCR and Pairwise Rank. Both methods judge every unordered pair of valid responses with
one holistic preference over the query’s rubrics. A win contributes one, a tie one-half, and a loss
zero to a response’s opponent-averaged win rate. RRC-SCR uses that rate as the scalar reward (Wang
et al., 2026a). Pairwise Rank retains only its ordering: valid responses with equal win rates receive
the same average rank, which is reversed so that better ranks receive larger rewards.
ArenaRL and Tournament-GRPO. Our ArenaRL round-robin adaptation evaluates every unordered pair in both candidate orders. Each request returns scores for the two candidates; we sum
the identity-aligned scores across orders to decide a win, tie, or loss, then map the resulting competition ranks to rewards (Zhang et al., 2026b). Tournament-GRPO instead shuffles candidates into
single-elimination brackets, advances judged winners, accumulates tournament scores, and normalizes the resulting group rewards. Its number of repeated brackets is a run-specific setting (Yang
et al., 2026).
SAW, POW3R, and AMRP. These weight-adaptive controls begin with pointwise rubric feedback. SAW uses the coefficient of variation of each reward dimension over an optimizer batch
to reweight rewards or reward-wise advantages (He et al., 2026). POW3R obtains one binary verdict for every rollout–rubric pair and adjusts rubric weights using group variation and a prompt-level
moving state (Tyagi et al., 2026). AMRP uses optimizer-step batch statistics and a moving-state projection to update its multi-reward combination (Yuan et al., 2026). Query-specific rubric columns
have no shared meaning across queries. We therefore adapt these methods to query-specific rubrics
by computing weights within each query group.
