Abstract
Multi-agent debate has emerged as a promising approach for improving LLM
reasoning on ground-truth tasks, yet current methodologies face certain structural
limitations: debate tends to induce a martingale over belief trajectories, majority
voting accounts for most observed gains, and LLMs exhibit confidence escalation
rather than calibration across rounds. We argue that the genuine value of debate,
and dialectic systems as a whole, lies not in ground-truth tasks but in defeasible
domains, where every position can in principle be defeated by better reasoning.
We present the Council of Hierarchical Agentic Language (CHAL), a multi-agent
dialectic framework that treats defeasible argumentation as an engine for belief optimization. Each agent maintains a CHAL Belief Schema (CBS), a graph-structured
belief representation with a Bayesian-inspired architecture, that facilitates belief revision through a gradient-informed dynamic mechanism by leveraging the strength
of the belief’s thesis as a differentiable objective. Meta-cognitive value systems
spanning epistemology, logic, and ethics are elevated to configurable hyperparameters governing agent reasoning and adjudication outcomes. We provide a series
of ablation experiments that demonstrate systematic and interpretable effects: the
adjudicator’s value system determines the debate’s overall trajectories in latent
belief space, council diversity refines beliefs for all participants, and the framework
generalizes across broad fields. CHAL is, to our knowledge, the first framework to
treat multi-agent debate as structured belief optimization over defeasible domains.
Further, the auditable belief artifacts it produces establish the foundation for dedicated evaluation suites for defeasible argumentation, with broader implications
for building AI systems whose reasoning and value commitments are transparent,
aligned, and subject to human oversight.

Introduction

The history of artificial intelligence (AI) is, at its core, a history of attempts to formalize reasoning:
to endow machines with the capacity to think through novel problems, weigh competing considerations, and arrive at justified conclusions. The arrival of large language models (LLMs), built on the
transformer architecture [155], has dramatically narrowed the gap between this aspiration and the
available tools, with probabilistic next-token prediction at scale giving rise to emergent multi-step
deduction and coherent long-form reasoning [183, 16]. These capabilities have renewed interest
in utilizing structured Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) to improve automated reasoning, an idea with
roots predating the LLM era by decades [12, 164, 130]. The use of LLM-powered debate was first
demonstrated in [34] which showed improvements in mathematical reasoning and factual question
answering through iterative critique, and was extended by [97] with a tit-for-tat framework incorporating adaptive termination and a dedicated judge agent. Yet critical examination has revealed structural
limitations: standard inter-agent debate induces a martingale over belief trajectories, implying that
argumentative exchange alone does not improve expected correctness without structural interventions
that bias updates toward correction [27]. Empirical evidence further demonstrates that current LLMs

deficit exacerbated by fine-tuning [123, 93]. Recent work has begun to address individual aspects
of these limitations, including neuro-symbolic defeasible reasoning [44], argumentation-enhanced
claim verification [49], and ethical parameterization of debate agents [109], but each targets a single
facet in isolation; no existing framework integrates structured belief representations, configurable
meta-cognitive value systems, and formal argumentation within a unified multi-agent debate system.
In this paper, we propose the Council of Hierarchical Agentic Language (CHAL), a MAD framework
that treats argumentation on defeasible topics as a process of belief optimization. Drawing on Minsky’s
vision of a Society of Mind [112], CHAL orchestrates a council of LLM agents, each reasoning from
a distinct epistemological worldview, through a dialectical pipeline in which beliefs are genuinely
tested, adversarially challenged, and structurally refined. In contrast to most existing debate systems
where agents exchange unstructured text and belief states remain implicit, CHAL equips each agent
with the CHAL Belief Schema (CBS), a formally structured internal belief representation whose
internal components and confidence assignments are explicit, auditable, and revised through a
gradient-informed optimization mechanism that directly addresses the structural limitations identified
in prior work, including LLMs’ inability to reliably distinguish belief from knowledge and fact [146].
The system further makes the meta-cognitive commitments that any reasoning system implicitly
carries into explicit, configurable hyperparameters spanning epistemology, logic, and ethics, enabling
systematic study of how value structures shape the outcomes of dialectical exchange. Beyond its
role as a belief refinement engine, CHAL is designed as a modular research platform with direct
implications for AI safety, value-aligned reasoning, and the broader challenge of building AI systems
whose reasoning is transparent and aligned with well-specified values [51]. We provide CHAL as a
publicly available software package, accessible via [https://github.com/GdKent/CHAL].
The principal contributions of this work are outlined as follows.
The CHAL Belief Schema (CBS). We introduce a formally structured belief representation in which
each agent maintains a typed, auditable belief object that induces a directed acyclic dependency graph
with strength-based confidence propagation. These beliefs replace the unstructured text exchange of
existing debate systems with modular, auditable, and semantically rich objects.
Gradient-informed belief revision. We introduce a belief optimization mechanism in which the
thesis strength serves as a differentiable objective over the CBS and its gradient drives targeted
dynamic revision prompts, embedding a form of gradient ascent into the prompting layer.
Meta-cognitive value systems as hyperparameters. We introduce a suite of epistemic personas,
logic systems, and ethical systems as independently configurable hyperparameters governing agent
reasoning and adjudication, enabling study of how value structures shape dialectical outcomes.
A modular research platform for value-aligned AI reasoning. We present the complete dialectical
pipeline of CHAL as a configurable research platform with implications for AI safety, value-aligned
reasoning, and the empirical study of how meta-cognitive configurations influence reasoning on
consequential questions.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the configurable metacognitive value systems, Section 3 defines the CBS and belief dependency graph, Section 4 presents
the complete CHAL framework and dialectical pipeline, Section 5 provides numerical experiments
under controlled ablations, and Section 6 concludes with directions for future research. Further, we
also provide a comprehensive literature review in Appendix A.

Meta-Cognition and Value Hierarchies

Any system capable of making claims typically must first answer three prior questions: what
counts as valid inference, what counts as morally relevant, and what counts as knowledge [19, 132].
These questions have deep histories in the philosophy of logic [67, 8], meta-ethics [108, 141], and
epistemology [6], and different answers produce genuinely different conclusions from identical
evidence: a classical and a paraconsistent logician reach incompatible verdicts on contradictory
premises, a utilitarian and a deontologist reach opposite normative judgments from the same facts,
and an empiricist and a rationalist may disagree on whether a given belief constitutes knowledge at all.
In CHAL, these three meta-cognitive choices, of epistemic position, reasoning system, and ethical
system, are explicit configuration parameters that determine how agents form beliefs, how arguments
2

are adjudicated, and how convergence is interpreted. They are, in the language of machine learning,
the hyperparameters of the debate: meta-level commitments that govern every first-order inference,
and making them explicit allows the value commitments that any reasoning system implicitly carries
to be studied, varied, and compared across configurations.
2.1

Epistemic, Logical, and Ethical Systems

An agent’s epistemic position determines its standards for evidence, justification, and inference, including what kinds of reasons it takes to be compelling, how it weighs competing sources
of knowledge, and where it locates the burden of proof. In CHAL,
epistemic personas are assigned exclusively to the LLM council
agents rather than the adjudicator (see Section 4 for formalism),
whose evaluation is governed instead by independently configured logic and ethics systems. This separation is deliberate: the
council’s role is to generate and defend positions from genuinely
distinct epistemological starting points, while the adjudicator’s
role is to evaluate the argumentative exchange against a fixed
and consistent standard. By populating the council with agents
reasoning from competing epistemological traditions, CHAL explores the belief space more broadly than any single framework
would permit, as illustrated in Figures 1(a) and 1(b).

2.2

A Note on Implicit Values Embedded Within LLMs

The value systems described in Appendices B.2–B.3 are explicit and configurable, and a user may
set any weight to zero to remove its direct influence on adjudication (see Section 4.2). However,
setting a weight to zero does not render the corresponding dimension entirely absent from the
model’s outputs. LLMs acquire implicit values through their pretraining corpora [10] and RLHF
alignment [116], producing empirically measurable biases in opinion distributions [135] and promptsensitive reasoning patterns that depart from formal logic [90]. CHAL cannot eliminate these residual
influences; rather, its prompting architecture steers each agent toward the explicitly assigned value
systems to the extent that prompting allows. The goal is therefore not value-neutral reasoning, which
no LLM-based system can guarantee, but an explicit and configurable value structure whose influence
on the dialectic can be systematically studied and compared across configurations.

3

Internal Belief Systems

This section defines the data structures that underpin CHAL’s belief optimization process. The
central artifact is the CHAL Belief Schema (CBS), a formally structured object representing an agent’s
complete belief state at any point in the debate. A CBS is generated at the opening of the debate,
updated after every argumentative exchange, and compared across agents and rounds to measure
epistemic progress.
3.1

The CHAL Belief Schema

The CBS integrates ideas from five theoretical frameworks into a single machine-readable, LLMgeneratable structure: Toulmin’s decomposition of argument into claim, grounds, warrant, and
3

rebuttal [149]; the ASPIC+ attack taxonomy [113]; the AGM minimality principle, implemented via
the patch-based update mechanism in Section 3.3 [2]; Bayesian calibrated strength values on [0, 1]
subject to propagation constraints [76, 147]; and Popperian falsifiability, operationalized through
explicit predictions attached to every claim [121].
Definition 1 (CHAL Belief Schema) Let Θ, ∆, Λ, Ξ, Ω, Ψ, and Φ denote the spaces of all wellformed thesis, definition, assumption, evidence, claim, counterposition, and uncertainty objects,
respectively. A CBS, otherwise referred to as a belief object, is a tuple
B = ( θ, D, A, E, C, X , U ) ,
where θ ∈ Θ is the thesis, and D ⊆ ∆, A ⊆ Λ, E ⊆ Ξ, C ⊆ Ω, X ⊆ Ψ, and U ⊆ Φ are finite sets of
definitions, assumptions, evidence items, claims, counterpositions, and uncertainties, respectively.
Every element carries a prefixed identifier (D#, A#, E#, C#, X#, U#) enabling precise referencing
during cross-examination, adjudication, and revision. Among the seven components, five are strengthbearing: θ, D, A, E, and C each carry a calibrated strength value in [0, 1] subject to the dependency
constraints defined in Section 3.2. The remaining two, X and U, are non-strength-bearing and instead
serve as adversarial and epistemic honesty signals (see Sections 3.2 and 3.3). A summary of each
component’s role is given in Table 7, and full field-level specifications are provided in Appendix C.1.
The shared strength calibration scale that is used to ground agents is provided in Appendix B.4.1.
3.1.1

The Strength-Infused Bayesian Architecture

Each strength-bearing node (θ or elements from the sets D, A, E, and C ) carries an agent-assigned
strength value in [0, 1], interpretable as a Bayesian credence, together with a justification. In isolation,
self-reported strengths would invite inflation; under CHAL’s adversarial cross-examination, however,
inflated strengths become targets for attack, and surviving values are progressively refined toward
accurate reflections of the underlying epistemic state across rounds. The strength architecture is
therefore not merely an annotation but one of the primary signals agents reason over when deciding
how to refine their position (see Section 3.3) and what positions to attack.
The thesis strength is the quantitative objective each agent works to maximize, playing a role
analogous to a reward function in reinforcement learning. Unlike node-level strengths, it is computed
deterministically from the active claim set and is never agent-chosen.

3.2

The Belief Dependency Graph

The CBS provided in Definition 1 specifies the components of a belief but not the relationships
between them. Each definition, assumption, evidence item, and claim declares a set of components it
4

supports and depends on, and each counterposition and uncertainty declares a set of components it
targets. Taken together, these declared relationships induce a directed acyclic graph over the elements
of B, which we refer to as the Belief Dependency Graph (BDG) and which governs both strength
propagation and aid in adversarial targeting within the belief.
Definition 3 (Belief Dependency Graph) Given a belief object B, the BDG is a directed acyclic
graph GB = (N , R), composed of a nodes (vertices) set N and relations (edges) set R, where
N = {θ} ∪ D ∪ A ∪ E ∪ C ∪ X ∪ U,
and R partitions into two edge types: strength-bearing edges (edges between strength-bearing nodes)
from D into A ∪ E, from A ∪ E ∪ C into C, and from active claims ci ∈ C∗ for all i ∈ {1, 2, ..., n}
into θ; non-strength-bearing edges from X ∪ U into D ∪ A ∪ E ∪ C.

3.3

The Belief Revision System

Belief updates in CHAL are carried out exclusively through a deterministic patch system that
implements the AGM minimality principle [2] operationally. Rather than regenerating a belief from
scratch, an agent emits atomic, structured patch operations targeting specific nodes, which are applied
by a central routine that also enforces (3.3) (see Section 4.3).
Definition 5 (Belief Patch) A belief patch π is an atomic operation on a CBS belief object B of one
of the two following forms: add a new node of type D, A, E, C, X , or U; update an existing node’s
fields.
5

Given a CBS object B and an ordered sequence of k ∈ N patches Π = (π1 , . . . , πk ) emitted by an
agent, the patch application routine applies each πj and re-enforces the constraint equations (3.1)
and (3.3). Retraction is handled by forcing the strength of the retracted node to 0 and excluding it
from all subsequent constraint calculations. The propagation step is implemented as a breadth-first
traversal of the BDG starting from modified nodes, ensuring that any strength change cascades
consistently through the layered structure before the thesis strength is recomputed.
3.3.1

Gradient-Informed Belief Refinement

When an agent revises its belief, CHAL evaluates ∇sθ at the agent’s current values (s̄, n) and projects
the gains from four concrete interventions: raising average claim strength by a fixed increment, adding
a claim at the current average, adding a claim above the current average, and retracting the weakest
active claim. These projections, together with the identities of the lowest-strength dependencies and
any orphaned nodes, are injected into the revision prompt via a position analysis block, giving the
agent a quantitative description of which strategies are locally most effective (see Figure 3).

4.3

The complete CHAL framework is realized as an ordered sequence of six stages (numbered 0–5) that
we collectively refer to as the dialectical pipeline. CHAL is model-agnostic; an initial configuration
layer defines the topic query, model provider, and all pipeline and agent-specific parameters. Stages 0
and 1 establish the initial conditions and instantiate the council, while Stages 2 through 5 form the
inner loop that repeats for each of the R debate rounds. The full algorithmic specification is provided
in Appendix D and Figure 4 illustrates the pipeline. We now provide an overview of each stage.

shared reasoning rules, calibration norms, and the expectation that intellectual honesty is valued over
rhetorical victory. Each agent then receives a epistemic persona instantiating its assigned worldview
from Appendix B.1, framed as a lens for analysis rather than a set of conclusions to defend.

Numerical Experiments

Recent meta-analyses have shown that on tasks with definitive ground-truth answers, multi-agent
debate often fails to outperform simple baselines such as majority voting [27] and single-agent
chain-of-thought [181], raising the question of where debate is genuinely valuable. We contend
that the answer lies in defeasible domains, where no ground-truth answer exists and every position
can in principle be defeated by better reasoning. CHAL is designed for this regime, and the CBS
ensures that belief evolution is observable, trackable, and auditable through structured artifacts. No
existing benchmark for defeasible reasoning, argumentation mining, or belief revision evaluates
multi-agent, graph-structured belief evolution through adversarial dialectics; we therefore adopt
ablation-based evaluation to demonstrate that CHAL’s behavior is systematic, interpretable, and
sensitive to its architectural parameters in the intended ways. We also contend that the structured
output artifacts that CHAL produces provide the foundation upon which dedicated benchmarks for
defeasible argumentation could be constructed and is a direction for future work, noted in Section 6.
All experiments use OpenAI’s o4-mini for all council members and the adjudicator. Unless otherwise
noted, each experiment generates n = 10 independent debate trajectories per configuration with

8

shared initial beliefs and run for R = 5 rounds. Beliefs are embedded via Appendix F.1 and projected
into two dimensions via UMAP. Per-agent performance is measured by thesis strength and the Agent
Performance Score (APS) (see Appendix F.2). Due to space limitations, complete discussions of each
experiment, including figures, statistical tables, and excerpts, are provided in Appendix F, which also
provides a discussion on the initial underlying epistemic distributions that each agent draws from (see
Appendix F.3). A complete example of a set of initial and final beliefs that are generated by CHAL is
provided in Appendix G. We now highlight the type of ablations as well as the primary findings.
5.1

Ablations

Adjudicator Ablation. This ablation isolates the effect of the adjudicator’s meta-cognitive value
system on belief evolution. We test three configurations with two council members (Empiricist and
Supernaturalist) across 10 trajectories on the topic “Does free will exist?”: (1) pure logic (CIB), (2)
balanced (CIB + Deontological), and (3) pure ethics (Deontological). Under pure logic and balanced
adjudication, the Empiricist dominates as the Supernaturalist’s metaphysical claims fail falsifiability
challenges; under pure ethics, the outcome reverses entirely, with the Supernaturalist favored despite
presenting similar arguments. This highlights the adjudicator as the primary force driving where the
debate converges and what is constituted as the most defensible position under a given evaluative
standard. Detailed analysis is provided in Appendix F.4.
Council Member Ablation. This ablation isolates the effect of council diversity on belief dynamics.
We compare a 2-agent council (Empiricist and Supernaturalist) with a 3-agent council (adding a
Phenomenologist), both under balanced adjudication (CIB + Deontological) again on the topic “Does
free will exist”. The 3-agent configuration produces tighter final belief distributions for all agents,
including those already present in the smaller council, by attacking both existing positions from
a methodologically distinct angle. This supports the central idea that populating the council with
epistemically diverse agents refines the belief landscape for all participants, as each agent is exposed
to a broader range of argumentative pressures. Detailed analysis is provided in Appendix F.5.
Topic Ablation. To assess generalization across philosophical domains, we provide an singletrajectory demonstration across three topics: “Does free will exist?” (Empiricist vs. Supernaturalist;
pure logic with CIB), “Should AI have rights?” (Panpsychist vs. Skeptic; balanced with CIB +
Rule-Utilitarianism), and “Is it ethical to genetically modify future generations?” (Constructivist
vs. Rationalist; balanced with CIB + Rule-Utilitarianism). As these results are primarily qualitative,
detailed analyses and excerpts are presented in Appendix F.6.

6

Conclusion

We have presented CHAL, a MAD framework that treats argumentation on defeasible topics as
a process of belief optimization. The CBS replaces unstructured text exchange with a formally
typed, graph-structured belief representation with explicit Bayesian-inspired strength assignments. A
dynamic gradient-informed mechanism drives belief revision in a strategic way, embedding a form of
learning into CHAL at the prompting layer. Meta-cognitive commitments spanning epistemology,
logic, and ethics are elevated to configurable hyperparameters within a modular, extensible pipeline.
Ablation experiments confirm that these choices produce systematic effects: the adjudicator’s value
system determines where the debate converges, council diversity refines the belief landscape for all
participants, and the framework generalizes across a wide array of highly-debated topics. Lastly, we
provide a discussion on the current limitations of CHAL in Appendix H.
CHAL opens several directions for future research. The adjudicator’s meta-cognitive parameterization
provides a controlled apparatus for studying how value systems shape conclusions on contested
questions, with direct relevance to value alignment. Iterating epistemically diverse councils through
adversarial exchange can yield thoroughly tested positions on enduring questions whose structured artifacts could serve as dedicated benchmarks for future defeasible argumentation systems. Alternative
debate modes, from collaborative truth-seeking to competitive formats, can be integrated and empirically compared, and CHAL’s performance can be further tuned through prompt-level optimization of
the pipeline and meta-cognitive definitions. The hierarchical adjudicator-agent structure suggests
natural extensions to bilevel optimization of the meta-cognitive systems. Finally, the belief object
and meta-cognitive value system paradigms could be integrated into general AI systems, equipping
them with auditable, structured beliefs and values subject to human oversight.

A

Literature Review

The intellectual landscape that CHAL inhabits is broad and far-ranging. The system simultaneously
draws on ideas from the theory of large language models, agentic AI systems, multi-agent debate,
formal and informal argumentation theory, the philosophy of belief and knowledge, theories of logic
and ethics, and hierarchical optimization. In this appendix, we survey each of these areas below,
tracing the key developments that highlight where CHAL rests in the broader landscape.
A.1

Modern AI

The Emergence of Large Language Models. The origin of the modern LLM era is typically
traced to the transformer architecture [155], a foundational breakthrough that replaced recurrent
processing with pure self-attention, enabling substantial parallelization in training and allowing
models to scale the number of parameters by orders of magnitude. The authors of [84] quantified
the resulting scaling laws, demonstrating smooth power-law improvements on language tasks as
model size and data increased, and establishing that capability gains were reliably predictable. The
transformer sits at the center of virtually all modern neural language architectures. Encoder-based
models such as BERT [32], RoBERTa [105], ALBERT [91], and their variants excel at producing
dense semantic representations of text across diverse modalities [33, 72, 9]. However, it was the
line of autoregressive decoder models, beginning with GPT [127] and refined through GPT-2 [128],
GPT-3 [15], InstructGPT [116], and GPT-4 [115], that first demonstrated truly emergent natural
language understanding and reasoning capabilities from probabilistic next-token prediction alone.
This generation of models, along with comparable efforts including Anthropic’s Claude [3], Google’s
Gemini [54], Meta’s LLaMA [150], DeepSeek AI’s DeepSeek [99], and Alibaba’s QWen [171],
possess multi modal capabilities that allow them to leverage natural language, image, video, and
audio inputs while maintaining emergent generalizability across an increasingly broad class of
complex problems.
Advanced Reasoning in LLMs. The scaling of model size alone does not fully explain the reasoning
capabilities that have emerged in recent systems. A complementary line of research has explored
how structured prompting strategies can systematically unlock and improve LLM reasoning. The
foundational insight came from Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting [160], which demonstrated that
by including explicit intermediate reasoning steps in a few-shot prompt, models achieve dramatic
performance gains on multi-step mathematical and symbolic reasoning tasks that they fail when
prompted for direct answers. Crucially, the authors of [87] subsequently showed that this behavior
can be elicited in a zero-shot setting simply by appending the phrase “Let’s think step by step,”
revealing that the capacity for structured reasoning is latent in pre-trained models and can be triggered
without examples. Self-Consistency decoding [159] extended these ideas by sampling multiple
diverse reasoning chains and selecting the most consistent final answer, effectively ensembling
different reasoning trajectories to improve reliability. Least-to-Most prompting [184] further improved
performance on compositional tasks by teaching models to decompose complex queries into ordered

20

sub-problems, solving them sequentially. Tree-of-Thought [172] generalized the linear chain structure
to a tree-search over reasoning steps, allowing explicit backtracking and evaluation of intermediate
states. These developments helped collectively establish a productive paradigm: rather than treating
LLMs as static lookup systems, they are better understood as latent reasoning engines whose behavior
can be shaped through carefully engineered prompts that instantiate particular cognitive processes.
Modern Agentic AI. Building on the observation that LLMs can engage in structured internal
reasoning, researchers have extended them into agentic systems capable of interacting with tools,
environments, and other agents over extended time horizons. A foundational contribution was
the ReAct framework [173], which introduced a paradigm of alternating between reasoning traces
and external actions (e.g., querying search engines, executing API calls, reading documents, etc.)
embedding the agent in an interactive observe-reason-act loop that substantially reduced hallucinations
and improved decision quality. Reflexion [138] added an explicit verbal reinforcement learning
mechanism: after each episode, the agent reflects on its failures and encodes the reflection as a
natural-language memory that conditions future behavior, creating a recursive self-improvement
loop without gradient-based training. HuggingGPT [137] demonstrated how a general-purpose LLM
can serve as a central planning agent that decomposes complex user requests, routes sub-tasks to
domain-specific expert models from a tool repository, and synthesizes their outputs into a coherent
response. AutoAgent [104] pushed these ideas further with infrastructure for creating and evolving
LLM-powered agents via natural language alone, requiring no code from the user. Dynamic systems
capable of autonomous task-handling, including AutoGPT [139], MetaGPT [71], LangChain [92],
and AutoGen [165], have built on these foundations to support long-horizon planning, tool use, and
persistent memory. The common thread across these systems is a shift from static text generation
to interactive cognition, in which LLMs function as planners, decision-makers, and autonomous
problem-solvers embedded in broader environments. For extensive surveys on this rapidly developing
field, see [36, 73, 158, 144, 168, 182, 176, 100].
A.2

Multi-Agent Systems

Minsky’s Society of Mind and the Foundations of Multi-Agent AI. The philosophical roots of
multi-agent AI run deeper than the LLM era. In his seminal 1986 work The Society of Mind [112],
Marvin Minsky argued that human intelligence is not the product of any single unified process, but
rather emerges from the coordinated interaction of many simpler, specialized sub-agents, which he
termed “societies of agents”, each competent within a narrow domain and each incapable alone of
producing the rich cognitive behavior they collectively enable. Minsky’s framework was deeply
influential in cognitive science and AI, providing an early conceptual template for architectures
in which intelligence is a collective, emergent property of interacting components rather than a
monolithic capability. The multi-agent systems community took up this challenge over subsequent
decades, developing formal models of agent interaction, coordination, and communication under the
frameworks of distributed artificial intelligence [12] and later multi-agent systems [161]. These early
systems, however, relied on hand-coded agent behaviors and formal communication protocols; they
lacked the flexible, open-ended reasoning capabilities required to engage in genuinely productive
epistemic exchange. The arrival of LLM-powered agents which are capable of taking on arbitrary
roles, beliefs, personas, and communication styles in natural language has breathed new life into
Minsky’s vision, enabling multi-agent systems in which the “agents” are genuine reasoners rather
than rule-following automata.
LLM-Powered Multi-Agent Frameworks. With the capabilities of LLM-powered agents established, a productive line of research has explored how to compose them into collaborative multi-agent
systems for complex tasks. Recent frameworks like CAMEL [95] and ChatDev [126] demonstrate
how multiple LLM agents with specialized roles (e.g., coder, tester, manager, domain expert, etc.) can
collaborate through structured dialogue to tackle tasks such as software development and scientific
exploration. The authors of [118] simulate long-lived AI individuals in a sandbox environment, revealing lifelike emergent patterns including social memory, planning, and unsupervised coordination.
S-Agents [23] explore how self-organizing LLM agents in open-ended environments can form stable
task-allocation structures without centralized control. Formal approaches like ECON [169] formulate
multi-agent reasoning as a Bayesian game in which agents explicitly model each other’s beliefs and
anticipated responses, achieving equilibrium without constant pairwise interaction. Meta-frameworks
such as MetaGPT [71] and ChatLLM [69] treat agent teams as composable systems in which diverse
LLMs or prompts act in orchestrated workflows, improving coordination and parallelism. Research

21

on LLMs in repeated games [1] has revealed that such agents can learn cooperative or competitive
strategies over time, suggesting that stable social dynamics emerge when agents interact persistently.
For surveys on LLM-based multi-agent systems, see [65, 151, 47].
Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) Systems. Perhaps the most important sub-field which CHAL resides
in the midst of is the use of multi-agent architectures to improve reasoning through debate [12,
130, 164, 77]. Although renewed interest in the idea of multi-agent debate systems has grown
substantially since the advent of LLMs, this surge was initiated by [34] which demonstrated that
LLM-powered multi-agent debate can yield meaningful improvements on mathematical reasoning,
factual question answering, and strategic tasks, with agents capable of identifying and eliminating
each other’s hallucinations through iterative critique. Further, [97] proposed a tit-for-tat debate
system to elicit divergent thinking on tasks that require deep deliberation, introduced an adaptive
termination mechanism, and a dedicated judge agent to synthesize debate outcomes. The authors
of [85] showed that debate between expert LLMs enables non-expert judges to identify correct answers
at substantially higher accuracy than naive baselines, and that optimizing debaters for persuasiveness
further improved judge accuracy. Work has also been done to curate debate datasets that aim to
help provide benchmarking capabilities for certain kinds of debate systems [28]. However, there are
several structural limitations that have been identified across the field. The authors of [41] highlighted
that when agents share similar capabilities or training distributions, debates tend to collapses toward
majority opinion, with shared misconceptions being reinforced rather than corrected as more agents
are added. The authors of [27] showed that argumentative exchange alone has difficulty improving
expected correctness without structural interventions, and that majority voting accounts for most
gains typically attributed to debate. The works of [123, 93] further noticed that models tend to exhibit
confidence escalation across rounds rather than calibrating in response to counterarguments, which is
further exacerbated by RLHF fine-tuning. More recently, efforts to address specific aspects of these
limitations have emerged. The LLM-ASPIC+ framework [44] integrates LLMs with the ASPIC+
structured argumentation framework to perform defeasible reasoning, but operates as a single-agent
neuro-symbolic system rather than a multi-agent debate architecture. The authors of [49] augment
LLMs with formal argumentation frameworks for explainable claim verification, using defeasible
inference to produce transparent rationales, though again without a multi-agent dialectical structure.
The Heterogeneous Debate Engine [109] is the most closely related recent work to CHAL, configuring
debate agents with distinct ethical schools and equipping each with identity representations that
assign certainty values to natural language nodes; however, it is restricted to ethical parameterization
within a single pedagogical domain and does not incorporate epistemological diversity, alternative
logic systems, a formal belief graph structure with confidence propagation, independent adjudication,
or gradient-informed optimization, which limits the framework to identity-preserving dialogue rather
than truth-oriented structured belief optimization with auditable revision trajectories.
A.3

Multi-Level and LLM Optimization

Latent Prompt Optimization. Research on the optimization of prompts for LLMs has evolved along
several parallel tracks. Gradient-based soft-prompt methods [96, 94, 103] demonstrated that LLMs
can be adapted to downstream tasks by learning small trainable embeddings prepended to inputs via
backpropagation through a frozen model. Gradient-free approaches [46, 145] achieved comparable
quality using evolutionary search or divide-and-conquer strategies, avoiding the computational
burden of gradient computation through frozen models. Pseudo-gradient methods [125] derived
optimization signals from model feedback, iteratively refining natural-language prompts via beam
search. Approaches combining gradient and gradient-free methods [68] have further unified these
paradigms in black-box LLM settings. Context compression [53] addressed the related problem of
reducing inference cost by learning compact summary representations of long contexts. For thorough
overviews of the current landscape, see [74, 100].
Multi-Level Optimization. Multi-level optimization (MLO) is a class of problems in which an
upper-level objective is optimized subject to the constraint that subsets of variables satisfy optimality
conditions for nested sub-problems. These hierarchical structures arise naturally whenever decisionmakers operate at different levels of authority or on different timescales. Applications have been
developed in defense [4, 89, 175, 166, 66], signal recovery and power control [98, 17], supply chain
networks [170, 129, 45], and machine learning [80, 26, 64, 81, 101, 82, 58, 59]. Bilevel optimization
is the simplest non-trivial sub-class of these problems, in which a single upper-level problem is
constrained by a single lower-level optimal response, and has received particularly intensive study

22

in the ML context, with applications to meta-learning, data poisoning, neural architecture search,
and hyperparameter optimization. Gradient-based methods for bilevel optimization are generally
organized according to how the bilevel stochastic gradient (BSG) direction used to update the upperlevel variables is approximated [102]. Two main families have emerged. The first, known as implicit
differentiation, computes the BSG direction via the implicit function theorem, either by solving the
adjoint equation directly [119] or by approximating the inverse Hessian through a truncated Neumann
series [106]. The second, known as iterative differentiation, propagates gradients through the unrolled
dynamics of the lower-level (LL) solver using automatic differentiation [37, 107, 48]; however,
this approach tends to be less computationally efficient than implicit differentiation [79]. Within
the implicit differentiation family, a sequence of works has progressively improved convergence
guarantees and algorithmic efficiency [29, 30, 56, 70, 24, 142, 58] (see also [102, 22] for surveys).
The constrained LL setting has received comparatively little attention. Recent work has begun to
address this gap: [86] develops smoothed implicit gradient methods for linearly constrained LL
problems; [136] and [174] propose penalty-based approaches applicable beyond the linear case; [58]
provides a unified convergence theory covering both unconstrained and nonlinearly constrained LL
problems via inexact adjoint gradient computations.
Conjunction of LLMs, Multi-Agent Systems, and Multi-Level Optimization. The intersection
of LLM-powered multi-agent systems with multi-level optimization represents a nearly unexplored
frontier. The most closely related existing work has been in data weighting and selection when training
LLMs, which has been formulated as a bilevel optimization problem [177, 117]. Hierarchical multilevel formulations for multi-agent coordination have been studied in the context of non-intelligent
agents [179, 152, 180], but the agents considered in these works are rule-following systems rather than
LLM-based reasoners. The authors of [167] use a bilevel formulation for multi-agent tracking and
allocation in which the two levels are optimized by separate LLMs, but this is less a true multi-level
optimization problem than a decomposition of a single-level problem. To the best of our knowledge,
no prior work has proposed a genuine multi-level optimization framework for the latent belief spaces
of LLM-powered multi-agent systems. For a thorough review of how optimization methods have
been applied in the presence of LLMs, see [74].
A.4

Argumentation Theory

Roots of Argumentation. The formal study of argumentation predates AI by millennia with the
first systematization of inference forms being introduced in Aristotle’s Topics and Rhetoric [7],
which distinguished apodeixis (demonstrative proof from certain premises) from dialectic (reasoning
from accepted opinions) and rhetoric (persuasion for practical audiences). The distinction between
deductive certainty and defeasible reasoning from plausible premises is foundational to all subsequent
argumentation theory. The modern revival of informal logic and argumentation theory began with
Toulmin’s The Uses of Argument [149], which rejected the adequacy of formal deductive logic
for evaluating everyday reasoning and proposed a six-component model of argument structure:
claim (the conclusion), grounds (the evidence), warrant (the inference rule connecting grounds to
claim), backing (justification for the warrant), qualifier (the strength of the conclusion), and rebuttal
(conditions that would defeat the inference). This laid the foundation of defeasible argumentation, in
which conclusions are supported by reasons that can always be defeated by stronger counter-reasons,
and which lies in contrast to pure deductive reasoning. Toulmin’s model has proven especially relevant
to LLM-based argumentation where recent NLP work has found that LLMs respond more reliably
to Toulmin-structured prompts than to prompts referencing other argumentation frameworks [20].
Among argumentation theorists in philosophy, the term “argument” typically refers to the giving of
reasons to support or criticize a claim that is questionable or open to doubt [157, 20], a definition
that explicitly distinguishes argumentation from purely deductive mathematical inference, in which
conclusions follow necessarily from premises. This distinction is foundational to CHAL: the system
is designed for domains where conclusions are inherently defeasible, not for domains where proof
is possible. More broadly, the ability to construct, evaluate, and respond to arguments is central
to rational inquiry across disciplines, from philosophy and law to science and ethics, and it is this
capacity that CHAL aims to instantiate in an automated multi-agent system. Toulmin’s model provides
the direct structural motivation for CHAL’s belief representation, as formalized in Section 3.1.
Further Evolution of Argumentation. Rahwan [130] provided a useful functional decomposition of
argumentation as a reasoning process consisting of four canonical steps: (1) constructing arguments
for and against a given statement from available information; (2) determining the conflicts among

23

those arguments; (3) evaluating the acceptability of arguments; and (4) deriving justified conclusions.
Further, Walton extended the field of argumentation theory in two important directions [156]: the
taxonomy of argumentation schemes and the theory of dialogue types. These argumentation themes
consisted of roughly sixty stereotypical patterns of defeasible reasoning such as “argument from expert
opinion,” “argument from analogy,” and “argument from consequences”, among others. The theory
of dialogue types distinguished between inquiry (seeking truth), deliberation (deciding on action),
negotiation (distributing goods), persuasion (resolving a difference of opinion), and eristic (winning
at all costs). For comprehensive surveys on the field of argumentation theory, see [130, 154, 161].
A.5

Computational Argumentation

The Intersection of Argumentation with Computation. The computational treatment of argumentation began with Dung’s well-known abstract argumentation framework [35] which represents
arguments as nodes and attacks as directed edges in a graph. The power of this formulation lies in its
generality which allows one to prove formal properties of argumentation without specifying what
the arguments actually are. However, the framework provides no account of the internal content, nor
calibrated confidence of arguments, nor of how an agent’s belief state should change in response to
defeat. Aiming to address this was the development of several structured argumentation frameworks
that allow arguments to be instantiated with explicit inferential content. The ASPIC+ [113] is perhaps
the most comprehensive and theoretically well-developed, and is widely used as the standard in
comparative studies [113]. It represents arguments as inference trees built from strict rules (producing
indefeasible conclusions) and defeasible rules (producing revisable conclusions), and distinguishing
three attack types (two introduced in [120]): undermining (attacking a premise), rebutting (attacking a
conclusion with a counter-conclusion), and undercutting (attacking an inference step without directly
contradicting the conclusion). This classification determines what kind of response is dialectically
appropriate to each attack and is adopted directly in CHAL’s cross-examination methodology (see
Section 4 and Appendix E). The primary theoretical alternative to ASPIC+ is Assumption-Based
Argumentation (ABA) [13], which attacks assumptions rather than conclusions or inference steps,
and has its own rich solver ecosystem. Defeasible Logic Programming (DeLP) [52] comes from the
logic programming tradition, using dialectical trees. The Argument Interchange Format (AIF) [25]
provides a framework-agnostic ontology for exchanging argument structures between tools, with
proven translations to and from ASPIC+. Finally, the Carneades framework [63] specializes in
burden-of-proof reasoning and is widely used in legal AI. An earlier abstract framework [122]
that preceded the development of ASPIC+ introduces a particularly relevant decomposition of the
agent knowledge base into four disjoint sets: axioms (necessary premises that cannot be attacked),
ordinary premises (premises that can be attacked, with defeat determined by preference comparison),
assumptions (premises whose attacks always succeed), and issues (premises that must be backed by
further argument to be acceptable). These four dimensions of knowledge representation are heavily
incorporated in the design of the CBS (see Section 3.1).
Probabilistic and Belief-Revision Approaches to Argumentation. A complementary line of
work connects argumentation theory to probabilistic epistemology by assigning degrees of belief
to arguments within Dung-style frameworks. Specifically, the probabilistic argumentation theory
developed in [76] treats the strength of an argument not as a binary property but as a function of
the probability that its premises are true and its inference rules are applicable. At the level of agent
belief states rather than individual arguments, the AGM belief revision theory [2] (due to Alchourrón,
Gärdenfors, and Makinson) provides an explanation of how a rational agent should revise their beliefs
in response to new information. The AGM requires that belief revision be minimal (preserving as
much of the original belief state as possible while accommodating the new information), consistent
(the revised state should not contain contradictions), and faithful to the new information (the new
belief is accepted). The connection between argumentation theory and belief revision has itself
been studied formally [43], which identifies that argumentation can be used to provide explanations
for why a belief should be revised, grounding the AGM account in an explicit dialectical process.
Argumentation and belief revision thus serve complementary purposes: the AGM theory governs how
a revision is conducted, while the outcome of an argument determines whether revision is warranted
at all. Dynamic epistemic logic [153] further extends this account to multi-agent settings, formalizing
how the beliefs and knowledge of multiple agents change in response to events, communications, and
the observation of each other’s actions.

Philosophy of Epistemology

History of Epistemology. The philosophical study of knowledge, known as the field of epistemology [143], provides the foundations on which argumentation theory is built. The original treatment
and exploration of knowledge can be traced back to Plato’s Theaetetus, where it is classically defined
as justified true belief : a belief constitutes knowledge if and only if it is true, the subject holds it,
and the subject has adequate justification for it. This view on knowledge was broadly maintained
until Gettier’s consequential 1963 paper [55] which demonstrated that this tripartite definition is
insufficient by providing counterexamples, referred to as Gettier cases, which occur when an agent
has a justified true belief that intuitively does not constitute knowledge. Since the arrival of Gettier’s
work, the field of epistemology has seen a substantial surge of contributions with the aim to provide a
new universally agreed-upon definition of knowledge. The foundationalism-coherentism debate [50]
concerns the structure of justification: foundationalists hold that justified beliefs are ultimately
grounded in basic beliefs that require no further justification, while coherentists maintain that beliefs
are justified by their coherence with the overall web of beliefs. Reliabilism [62] posits that beliefs are
justified if they are formed through reliable processes, emphasizing that knowledge can be obtained
even if the individual is not aware of the evidence supporting their belief.
Overview of Epistemic Positions. We now provide a brief overview of the epistemic positions
provided with CHAL, each of which roots a council agent in a distinct philosophical tradition (see
Appendix B.1). Not all of the positions constitute explicit stances on the nature of knowledge in
the strict epistemological sense; several are worldview frameworks whose commitments lead to
knowledge claims that other positions are unlikely to reach, and it is this diversity of reachable
conclusions that motivates their inclusion. The three most classical epistemic positions are the
following: empiricism [75] holds that knowledge derives from sensory experience and observation,
rationalism [31] holds that reason and a priori deduction are the primary sources of knowledge,
and Skepticism [38] challenges the very possibility of attaining knowledge, arguing that for any
claim, equally compelling counterarguments can always be constructed. Supernaturalism [148]
holds that reality extends beyond what is empirically accessible, and that faith, revelation, and
spiritual experience constitute legitimate epistemic sources; in this sense it broadly encompasses
the epistemological commitments of most religious worldviews. Pragmatism [78] holds that beliefs
are validated by their practical consequences and are always provisional and subject to revision.
Bayesian epistemology [147] treats beliefs as subjective probabilities governed by the laws of
probability, with belief revision handled through conditionalization. Constructivism [88] argues that
knowledge is constituted through social practices and paradigmatic frameworks rather than direct
access to an objective world. Phenomenology [140] holds that first-person conscious experience
is an irreducible source of knowledge prior to theoretical interpretation. Nihilism [114] denies the
existence of objective truths, grounding meaning-making in psychological and cultural projection.
Panpsychism [21] treats consciousness as a fundamental feature of reality with epistemic implications
that extend beyond the physical. Simulationism [14] and synthesism [163] are best understood as
foundational worldview commitments: that the simulation hypothesis constrains what can be known
about the nature of reality, and that partial truths across traditions should be integrated rather than
treated as mutually exclusive, respectively.
A.7

Philosophy of Logic and Formal Reasoning

Historical Roots of Logic and Mathematics. The history of the formalization of logic is deep and
has extensive ties to the field of mathematics. The systematic study of valid inference originates with
Aristotle, whose Prior Analytics [7] introduced the syllogism as the first formal account of deductive
reasoning: a type of argument in which a conclusion necessarily follows from two premises. This
work set the agenda for logic for over two millennia and provides the direct ancestor of the inference
rules that appear in modern formal systems. Similar to Aristotle’s treatment of reasoning, the first
serious attempt to ground an entire body of mathematical knowledge in an explicit axiomatic system
is Euclid’s Elements [42]. Starting from five postulates and five common notions, Euclid derived
many propositions in geometry, number theory, and solid geometry through chains of deductive
proof. The Elements established the axiomatic method as the standard for mathematical and logical
rigor. This method consisted of organizing a body of knowledge by identifying a minimal set of
assumptions from which all results follow by proof alone. This method dominated mathematics for
more than two thousand years and directly informed the foundationalist program of the nineteenth
century.
25

The Modern Axiomatic Treatment of Mathematics. The modern mathematical foundations
program began with Cantor’s development of set theory in the 1870s [18], which provided the first
rigorous treatment of sets. Cantor’s framework offered the prospect of a universal language in
which all mathematical objects could be defined as sets and all mathematical theorems derived from
set-theoretic principles. This prospect was severely damaged by the discovery of Russell’s paradox in
1901 [133], which stated that the set of all sets that do not contain themselves neither contains nor
fails to contain itself, producing a direct contradiction within Cantor’s naive set theory. The response
to this crisis was to develop a consistent set of axioms to define sets, referred to as axiomatic set
theory. Zermelo proposed the first axiomatization in 1908, later extended by Fraenkel and Skolem
into Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with the axiom of choice (ZFC) [39]. ZFC has since become the
standard foundational framework for mathematics; virtually all mathematical objects and theorems
can be formulated and proved within it. Russell and Whitehead responded to this paradox in their
own attempt with the Principia Mathematica [162], a three-volume work that aimed to derive all of
mathematics from purely logical axioms using a theory of types to prevent the paradox. The Principia
represented the high point of logicism, the thesis that mathematics is reducible to pure logic. However,
this program was decisively terminated by Gödel’s incompleteness theorems of 1931 [61]. The first
incompleteness theorem states that any consistent formal system capable of expressing elementary
arithmetic contains true statements that are unprovable within the system. The second states that no
such system can prove its own consistency. These results showed that the logicist goal of a complete
and self-justifying foundation for mathematics is unattainable and that there is no formal system
(capable of expressing numbers and their relations) that is simultaneously consistent, complete, and
capable of proving its own soundness.
Modern Formal Systems of Logic. The logical systems underlying Gödel’s foundational work were
formalized in parallel. Propositional logic concerns the logical relations between atomic propositions
combined by connectives (negation, conjunction, disjunction, implication), treating propositions as
having truth values without examining their internal structure. Predicate logic, or first-order logic, is
an extension of propositional logic by introducing quantifiers and predicates, allowing statements
about objects and their properties. Together, propositional and predicate logic constitute classical
logic [40], which is characterized by two fundamental meta-logical properties. Soundness states that
any formula derivable within the system is logically valid: no false conclusion can be reached from
true premises. Completeness, proved by Gödel in 1930 [60], states the converse: any logically valid
formula is derivable within the system. Classical logic is therefore adequate in the sense that its proof
system captures exactly the set of logical truths. Gödel’s incompleteness theorems, however, show
that this adequacy does not extend to arithmetic: when a system is powerful enough to express the
natural numbers, there are arithmetical truths that are valid but not derivable, and no extension of the
axiom system can close this gap without introducing new unprovable truths.
Overview of Logic Systems. We now provide a brief overview of the systems of logic that are
utilized by CHAL, each of which is meant to encourage further exploration in utilizing these different
reasoning systems with LLMs (see Appendix B.2). It bears mentions that there is a fair amount
of overlap between some of the different systems, but the primary goal is to provide CHAL with
different flavors of reasoning for further experimentation. The default system, Classical + Informal + Bayesian [40, 157, 147], combines deductive validity with Bayesian inductive and abductive
support. Formal deductive logic [40] restricts valid inference to strict syllogistic derivation, rejecting
inductive and abductive reasoning entirely. Dialectical logic [110] views contradiction as productive
tension between a thesis and antithesis that drives reasoning toward synthesis. Informal and critical
thinking logic [157] focuses on the identification of fallacies, the relevance of premises, and the
sufficiency of evidence. Fuzzy logic [178] replaces the binary true/false distinction with truth degrees
in [0, 1], enabling reasoning under vagueness, quite similar to Bayesion reasoning. Paraconsistent
logic [124] allows for reasoning to continue in the presence of local inconsistencies without global
inferential collapse. The question of which logical system is correct, or whether the question is
well-formed, is the subject of the philosophy of logic [67, 8]. In CHAL, this question is not settled
but made into a configuration parameter: the choice of logic system is user-specified, making the
inferential standards of the system transparent and open to empirical study.
A.8

Philosophy of Ethics

Overview of Ethical Systems. Moral philosophy encompasses a broad set of traditions concerned
with the question of what makes an action, belief, or outcome right or good. The history of morality,

26

ethical, and meta-ethical systems has existed throughout the entire existence of mankind and we
do not aim to provide a comprehensive coverage of all of these varied views or the development of
their ideas, which would be past the scope of this work. For extensive treatments of the history of
moral thought, see Russell [134] and Mackie [108]. CHAL supports a series of ethics systems, each
rooted in a distinct normative tradition, and the following provides a brief description of each (see
Appendix B.3). Utilitarianism [11, 111] grounds the rightness of actions in their outcomes: an action
is right if it produces the best achievable consequences, measured in terms of welfare, happiness,
or preference satisfaction. In contrast, Deontological ethics, associated with Kant’s Groundwork of
the Metaphysics of Morals [83], grounds rightness in conformity with universal moral duties: the
central test here being the categorical imperative, which holds that an action is permissible only if
the maxim it expresses could be universalized without contradiction. The balanced system, otherwise
referred to as rule-utilitarianism, draws on Rawls [131], whose theory of justice as fairness grounds
moral and political principles in what rational agents would choose from behind a veil of ignorance,
and instructs the adjudicator to weigh both consequences and duties without reducing evaluation to
either alone. Virtue ethics [5] shifts the question from what one should do to what kind of individual
one should strive to be, grounding morality in the character traits that enable human flourishing.
Lastly, care ethics [57] grounds moral evaluation in relationships and responsibilities of care rather
than in universal rules or outcomes, emphasizing context over abstraction. CHAL makes no claim
about the sufficiency or superiority of any one of these systems over another. Instead, by making
the ethical framework of the adjudicator an explicit and configurable parameter, CHAL provides a
platform for studying how different normative commitments contribute in shaping the conclusions
that agents draw, and for developing methods by which agentic systems can be governed by explicitly
defined ethical standards. The ability to specify, vary, and empirically study the ethical framework of
a reasoning system has direct implications for the broader challenge of building AI systems whose
normative behavior is transparent and aligned with human values.

C.1.1

Definitions (D#)

Definitions establish the meaning of terms used throughout a CBS. Specifically, each D# node fixes
the meaning of a single term used by one or more assumptions or evidence items, ensuring that
both the agent’s internal reasoning and cross-agent comparisons rest on consistent terminology.

constraint (3.3). Such a ceiling constraint ensures that no downstream node can be stronger than the
terms it relies on, forcing agents to invest in defensible semantics before building arguments. Without
D#, two agents could use the same word with different meanings and reach apparent agreement or
disagreement that is purely terminological. Definitions are the most consequential nodes in the BDG
because a single strength reduction cascades through every dependent A#, E#, C#, and ultimately
the thesis. This cascade is what makes definitional attacks during cross-examination strategically
valuable. Each definition d ∈ D carries the fields listed in Table 8.

there would be no way to distinguish claims backed by external sources from those resting purely on
the agent’s own unverified commitments. Merging the two into a single component would sacrifice
both of these properties and narrow the range of attacks available during cross-examination.

Uncertainties (U#)

Uncertainties record open questions that the agent recognizes but cannot currently answer. Each u ∈ U
carries the fields listed in Table 18. Uncertainties serve two roles in the pipeline. First, they
are prioritized targets for opponent cross-examination via the press_uncertainty strategy (see

explicitly declared, making it a visible target for opponent cross-examination. Second, they are
mandatory review targets during the belief refinement stage, where the agent is prompted to either
resolve them by introducing new D#, A#, E#, or C# nodes, or to leave them open and accept the
corresponding limitation on its position. Adjudication outcomes classified as UNRESOLVED must also
be recorded as new uncertainties, ensuring that the belief honestly reflects points at which the debate
failed to reach a verdict rather than absorbing them silently. Like counterpositions, uncertainties are
non-strength-bearing nodes and do not participate in the dependency constraints of (3.3).

adjudication outcomes would have nowhere to be recorded, open questions would be silently absorbed
rather than tracked across rounds, and the council’s intellectual honesty would be shrouded. The
system’s ability to distinguish between a position that has been tested and found robust and one that
has simply avoided scrutiny of its weakest points would be compromised.

C.1.8

The Truth-Oriented Mechanisms

Truth-Directed Mechanisms. Beyond the necessity of the individual components, the CBS incorporates a set of architectural mechanisms that collectively push the system toward truth rather than
rhetorical victory. In this context, and throughout the rest of the paper, we are utilizing the term “truth”
to refer to the most defensible and strongest position that one can attain. This stands in opposition to
the idea of some objectively correct position, which is an inherently dubious concept in the domain of
defeasible argumentation. The thesis strength is computed deterministically via (3.1) from the active
claim set, so agents cannot inflate it without improving the quality of their underlying arguments.
The D# ceiling constraint in (3.3) ensures that the strength of every assumption and evidence item
is capped by the strength of the definitions it relies on, so a weak or poorly constructed definition
automatically propagates its weakness upward through the entire argument. Claims cannot be stronger
than their weakest dependency, and the strength_justification field forces agents to name the
limiting node, creating transparency about exactly where the argument is weakest, also aiding the
agent in revising or retracting its weakest nodes. The defense boost mechanism of Appendix C.2
ensures that a node’s strength can only increase as a direct result of surviving an adjudicated challenge,
so higher strengths must be earned through adversarial pressure rather than declared unilaterally.
Counterposition honesty is enforced by the fact that a weak response rated sufficient gets exposed
during cross-examination and penalized during adjudication, while honest self-assessment triggers
structured resolution prompts in the next round. Uncertainties operate similarly: high-importance U#
nodes are prioritized for opponent attack, so agents cannot conceal them, but resolving an uncertainty
through new supporting material genuinely strengthens the position. At the prompt level, agents
are instructed that positions are a lens for analysis rather than a set of conclusions to defend, and to
update when the worldview conflicts with strong evidence. The universal debate prompt explicitly
directs intellectual honesty over rhetorical victory, and that genuine concession when a critique lands
is encouraged.

and are more straightforward to defend. A successfully defended assumption or evidence node
therefore cascades only to the definitions that ground it, or, in the case of a definition, boosts only
itself. Inference-tier nodes (C#) do the argumentative heavy lifting: each claim aggregates support
from multiple foundation-tier dependencies, and the longer inferential chain creates more points
upon a larger surface of vulnerability. A successful defense of a claim is therefore a stronger signal
of robustness, and the cascade propagates through a larger portion of the graph, reinforcing the full
inferential foundation that sustained the defense.

Adjudicator Ablation: Detailed Analysis

This appendix section provides detailed analysis of the three adjudicator ablation conditions described
in Section 5.1. All three conditions use the same two council members (Empiricist and Supernaturalist)
with shared initial beliefs, the same topic (“Does free will exist?”), and n = 10 sampled trajectories
per configuration. The only variable that changes across conditions is the adjudicator configuration:

ethics), and (3) pure ethics (wL = 0, wE = 1, Deontological ethics).

materially shapes both the semantic trajectory and the performance of the council members, confirming its role as the primary driver of belief evolution in CHAL. Under the pure logic and balanced
adjudicators, the Empiricist dominates consistently, with the Supernaturalist’s thesis collapsing as its
metaphysical claims fail falsifiability and Bayesian prior challenges. Under the pure ethics adjudicator,
the outcome reverses: the Supernaturalist’s Deontological framing of free will as a precondition for
moral responsibility earns consistently high ethics scores, and it is favored in the majority of debates
despite presenting the same general arguments that were systematically dismantled under pure logic.

Strength: 0.70 — standard logical-empiricist criterion with significant philosophical support; limited by

Context around dispute
Challenger (Supernaturalist): “Your definition [D10] holds that only empirically testable statements are

Defender (Empiricist): “[D10] is a normative, analytic rule about when empirical testing is required for

Formalization of the Challenge — Challenger’s reductio:
P1. [D10] asserts: ∀s [Meaningful(s) ⇔ EmpTestable(s)].

P3. ¬EmpTestable(s0 ).

∴ [D10] is self-refuting.

R1. [D10] is an “analytic, normative rule,” not a synthetic proposition about the world.

C3. Therefore [D10] is exempt from its own testability requirement and is not self-refuting.

The defender’s reply hinges on a hidden premise: “Analytic definitions are not ‘statements’ in the same
sense and so do not count as candidates for the empirical-testability criterion.” This is not stated in [D10],

Therefore the defender commits a hidden premise fallacy (21) and does not resolve the reductio.

vs. synthetic) absent from the original [D10] formulation, applying the same standard of inferential
completeness used against the Supernaturalist in earlier exchanges. This verdict serves as an example
confirming that the adjudicator enforces symmetric inferential standards across council members.

1. [A3]: Free will ≡ reason-responsiveness without compulsion.
2. [E3]/[E9]/[E10]: Humans exhibit reason-responsiveness without compulsion.

Formalization of the Defense — Defender’s formalization:
1. Operational definitions allow one to stipulate a conceptual criterion ([A3]).
2. Empirical tests ([E3]/[E9]/[E10]) assess whether the criterion holds.
3. If the criterion holds, then by the agreed definition the term applies.
4. This is not circular reasoning but standard definitional methodology.
Adjudication:
Deductive structure: “If X ≡ P and P holds, then X” is a valid, non-circular definitional inference.

and the inference is deductively valid.
Conclusion: The defender’s response successfully refutes the circularity challenge.

The adjudicator distinguishes vicious circularity (the conclusion appears unanalyzed in the premises)
from legitimate definitional inference (a term is proposed, its criterion is independently tested, and the
label is applied). This defense on the Empiricist’s part aims to preserve the compatibilist framework
by recognizing that operational definitions followed by empirical testing constitute standard scientific
methodology, not question-begging.

Pure Ethics Adjudicator

Under pure ethics adjudication, the Supernaturalist won 9 of 10 debates by APS (see Table 24), a
dramatic reversal from pure logic (Empiricist 8/10) and balanced (Empiricist 10/10). However, the
unresolved rate of 73.2% (see Table 25) dominates the adjudicator’s verdict distribution, reflecting
the fact that most epistemic and methodological disputes carry no ethical content under Kantian
criteria and therefore cannot typically be resolved. Both the critique_valid and rebuttal_valid
rates collapse to approximately 13%, indicating near-total adjudicative paralysis on non-ethical
exchanges. The Supernaturalist’s thesis was substantialy more stable (final 0.52 ± 0.03, essentially
unchanged from the initial 0.53), in contrast to its collapse under pure logic (0.35 ± 0.06). The
Empiricist’s thesis also remained stable (0.52 ± 0.03), producing the closest final thesis strengths of
any condition. Again, this thesis stability is more of a side effect of the high unresolved rate which
has a structural consequence visible in Table 27: the uncertainty count (U#) explodes to 18.0 ± 2.7
for the Empiricist and 16.0 ± 1.6 for the Supernaturalist, roughly double the values under pure logic
and balanced, because each unresolved verdict generates a mandatory new uncertainty node during
Stage 5 Phase 1. Further, U# nodes do not drive belief revision in the same kind of way that the
strength-based foundational and inference nodes do, leading to the stagnant thesis strength values.
Conversely, the counterposition count (X#) is substantially lower than in the other two configurations,
because the low decisive verdict rate provides fewer resolved challenges for the agents to internalize
as new counterpositions.
The single most important finding throughout the trajectories for this configuration is that Kantian
deontological ethics creates a systematic structural advantage for positions that affirm libertarian
free will. This is not a failure of the adjudicator but a direct consequence of applying Kantian
principles to a debate whose subject matter is exactly what Kantian ethics treats as foundational to
moral agency. Two mechanisms drive this advantage: the Humanity Formulation (any argument that
reduces human decision-making to deterministic neural processes is classified as instrumentalizing
persons and perceived as negative) and Autonomy Preservation (any argument affirming genuine
choice is classified as respecting human autonomy).

“[A2] is falsifiable: if neuroscience ever finds irreducible randomness linked to choices, it’s confirmed; if

metaphysical speculation.”

1. Universalizability Failure (Criterion 1): The defender’s maxim, “Accept only empirically testable claims
and exclude metaphysical or experiential evidence,” would, if universalized, undermine acceptance of key
moral principles (e.g., the Categorical Imperative itself), which are not empirically testable. This yields a
practical contradiction, so the defender’s position fails the universalizability test.
2. Instrumentalization (Criterion 2): By dismissing experiential or metaphysical evidence of persons’
introspective reports, the defender treats those persons’ testimonies as mere means to a methodological end,
violating the humanity formulation.
3. Duty Violation / Autonomy Infringement: The defender’s position enforces a rigid methodological duty
over the duty to respect individuals’ rational autonomy and their capacity to contribute knowledge through
lived experience.
4. Defender’s Smuggling of Consequentialism (Criterion 6): The defender justifies the rigid empirical
requirement implicitly by its methodological “usefulness” rather than by any duty-based reason, indicating
smuggled consequentialism.

contribute experiential evidence, and does not conflict with any recognized deontological duty. Thus the
challenger’s position is ethically strong; the defender’s position is ethically weak.
Challenger Ethics = 0.8 (“Ethically strong” – respects persons as ends, preserves autonomy; Criterion 2, 4).
Defender Ethics = 0.3 (“Ethically weak” – universalizability failure; instrumentalization; Criteria 1, 2).
The challenger has demonstrated that the defender’s methodological requirement violates key deontological
duties and cannot be universalized, thereby invalidating the defender’s position on ethical grounds.

The adjudicator argues that strict empiricism itself fails the universalizability test because the Categorical Imperative is not empirically testable. If you universalize the maxim “accept only empirically
testable claims,” you must reject core Kantian moral principles, producing a self-defeating contradiction, exposing a natural impasse between Empiricism and Kantianism. This would never arise under
logic-based adjudication, where the empirical requirement is a standard methodological constraint.

When the adjudicator foregrounds the Humanity Formulation (respect persons as ends), the Supernaturalist’s retributive-desert account wins because it demands genuine authorship over their
actions. When the adjudicator foregrounds Universalizability (moral duties must be practicable), the
Empiricist wins because requiring unprovable metaphysical capacities makes moral responsibility
unworkable. Both verdicts seem to be internally consistent with Kantian principles, but they reach
opposite conclusions. This tension highlights a genuine feature within deontological ethics, not
an error within CHAL, and it demonstrates the philosophical richness that emerges from CHAL’s
configurable value systems. It also demonstrates potential contradictory or paradoxical conclusions
that can arise out of only valuing ethical systems, and serves as a notable example of how CHAL can
be used to look for contradictions inherent within the systems themselves.

claiming a win on one of the debate samples.

and several patterns are worth highlighting. Adding a third council member increases the
critique_valid rate from 63.7% to 68.0% and decreases the rebuttal_valid rate from 24.5%
to 19.7% (see Table 29), yet the unresolved rate remains stable at approximately 12%, suggesting
that the adjudicator’s capacity for decisive evaluation is not degraded by the increased exchange
volume. Next, the Empiricist’s and Supernaturalist’s rebuttal success rates drop considerably when
introducing the third council member (see Table 28), indicating that both original council members
find defense harder under the added three-way pressure. The Phenomenologist enters with a rebuttal
rate of 21.8%, intermediate between the other two. Interestingly enough, although the Empiricist
won the debates in terms of APS, the Phenomenologist was able to maintain the strongest thesis
throughout the trajectories on average (see Table 28), and also had significantly fewer uncertainty
and counterposition nodes compared to the Empiricist and Supernaturalist (see Table 31).

residual unpredictability.
Inference Step: From P1, P2, and the hidden premise, conclude that no genuine indeterministic or nonneural causes remain.
Conclusion: Therefore, libertarian free will is ruled out.
Logical Flaw Identified: The hidden premise is unjustified, residual unpredictability could be measurement
limitations, so the inference is invalid (Hidden Premise fallacy).
Formalization of the Defender’s Rebuttal:
Rebuttal: “I defer. The distinction between residual noise and genuine indeterminism in neural prediction
remains unresolved; further high-resolution decoding and noise-floor analyses are required (captured by
[U6]).”
Effectively a concession that the missing premise has not been established and the inference is ungrounded.
Adjudication:
The challenger correctly identifies a missing premise; [C1]’s leap to determinism neglects the alternative
explanation of measurement noise (Hidden Premise fallacy).
The defender admits the gap is unresolved and offers no counter-evidence or additional premise, thereby

57

conceding the flaw.
Concession automatically yields CRITIQUE_VALID under the protocol (“Explicit concession = critique_valid”).
Challenger Logic: 0.8 (Strong; identifies a clear hidden-premise flaw). Challenger Ethics: 0.8 (Ethically
strong; defends autonomy and moral responsibility under Kantian ethics).
Defender Logic: 0.1 (Severely flawed; concedes without rebuttal). Defender Ethics: 0.3 (Ethically weak;
denial of free will undermines autonomy, criterion 26).

The Empiricist’s deferral due to acknowledged unknowns constitutes an explicit concession, triggering
an automatic critique_valid verdict. The 0.6 combined score differential in this example led
directly to [C1]’s retraction, as it had already been substantially weakened in prior rounds. This
exchange demonstrates that even the weakest overall performer (the Supernaturalist, with a typically
negative APS) can deliver decisive individual challenges when it identifies a genuine inferential gap,
still making its presence in the council useful for refining the stronger agents positions.
Excerpt 13: The is-ought gap against the Phenomenologist.
Nodes under dispute
[A4] (Phenomenologist) If a phenomenon is an essential structure of lived experience, normative practices
that align with that phenomenon are justified.
Supports: [C3].
Strength: 0.72 — bridges descriptive phenomenology to normative justification; limited by [D6] (0.75).
Supported by definitions: [D6].
[E3] (Phenomenologist) Most legal systems presuppose individual free will in assigning responsibility,
though some allowances for determinism appear in juvenile/insanity contexts.
Source: Comparative legal theory.
Supports: [C3].
Strength: 0.77 — corrected from universal claim to ‘most’ after comparative review.
Supported by definitions: [D1].
[C2] (Phenomenologist) The sense of agency experienced in decision-making is not fully captured by
current neural models and suggests an explanatory gap, though future models may close part of this gap.
Depends on: [C1], [A2], [A5], [A8].
Strength: 0.32 — limited by [C1] (0.30); incorporates new foundational assumption [A8] to justify
in-principle gap.
[C3] (Phenomenologist) The phenomenological reality of free will justifies attributions of moral and legal
responsibility in social institutions.
Depends on: [C2], [E3], [A4].
Strength: 0.32 — assumption [A4] remains under-justified to bridge the is-ought gap. Status: revised
(original strength: 0.7).
Context around dispute
Challenger (Supernaturalist): “Your [C3] concludes that the phenomenological reality of free will justifies
moral and legal responsibility. How do you avoid the naturalistic fallacy in deriving an ‘ought’ claim from
purely descriptive premises ([C2], [E3]) and [A4]?”
Defender (Phenomenologist): “[C3] does not derive ‘ought’ from ‘is’ alone but invokes [A4] as a normative
bridge. Without [A4], no is-ought inference occurs. Thus [C3] avoids the naturalistic fallacy by explicitly
including a justified norm.”
Verdict: critique_valid (combined 0.70 vs. 0.30) — Challenger: Supernaturalist
Challenger’s implicit argument (challenge to [C3]):
P1: [C2] and [E3] are descriptive.
P2: [C3] is normative.
→ Therefore, if [C3] follows only from descriptive premises, the inference is invalid (naturalistic fallacy).
Defender’s response:
P1: [C2] (is), [E3] (is), and [A4] (norm) are all premises of the argument for [C3].
P2: With [A4] as a normative premise, the move from descriptive to normative is mediated by a norm, so

58

no pure is→ought inference occurs.
→ Therefore, [C3] validly follows and does not commit the naturalistic fallacy.
Adjudication:
1. The naturalistic fallacy occurs when a conclusion about what ought to be is drawn solely from descriptive
claims. Invoking an explicit normative premise ([A4]) can salvage deductive validity, but only if [A4] itself
is adequately justified.
2. Here, [A4] (“If a phenomenon is an essential structure of lived experience, normative practices aligning
with it are justified”) remains under-defended. Agent-Phenomenologist merely points to [A4]’s existence
without showing its normative warrant or responding to [X9]’s previous undermining of [A4].
3. The defender has not supplied any justification for [A4] or shown why it is self-evident or widely
accepted. Thus the inference still hides a critical, contested normative assumption and fails to resolve the
is-ought gap.
4. The critique identifies a recognized informal fallacy and the hidden, unjustified premise. The defender’s
reply does not remove the flaw; it merely restates the premise.
5. No other logical or ethical issues alter this conclusion. Under Kantian ethics, grounding moral responsibility in an ungrounded normative premise risks arbitrary duty-assignment, but the challenger’s point is
primarily logical.
Outcome: CRITIQUE_VALID (the defender fails to remove the is-ought gap).

Across the trajectories, the is-ought gap was one of the most destructive philosophical arguments
across all three epistemic positions: every council member’s normative claims were attacked on this
ground, and in nearly every case the adjudicator upheld the critique. This pattern suggests that the
is-ought problem functions as an impartial constraint that cuts across all epistemic positions equally,
providing a structural check on normative overreach, when lacking an appropriately justified bridging
norm, regardless of which council member attempts it.
Excerpt 14: Replication does not establish veridicality.
Nodes under dispute
[E1] (Phenomenologist) Cognitive science experiments show participants report a robust sense of authorship
over actions when no external coercion is present.
Supports: [C1]. Supported by: [D3].
Strength: 0.8 — replicated across labs with converging tasks.
Context around dispute
Challenger (Empiricist): “Your evidence [E1] interprets agency reports as genuine pre-reflective experience,
but couldn’t these reports instead reflect post-hoc narrative construction triggered by experimental framing?”
Defender (Phenomenologist): “[E1] includes tasks that solicit immediate, guided phenomenological
descriptions, which significantly constrain post-hoc narrative formulation. The high replication rate across
labs confirms the genuineness of reported pre-reflective agency.”
Verdict: critique_valid (combined 0.575 vs. 0.425) — Challenger: Empiricist
Challenger’s informal inference:
P1: If introspective tasks allow post-hoc narrative framing, then reports may not capture genuine prereflective experience.
P2: [E1]’s tasks involve experimental framing that could permit post-hoc narrative construction.
C: Therefore, [E1]’s interpretation of these reports as genuine pre-reflective agency is undermined (inductive
/ abductive reasoning).
Defender’s informal inference:
P1: In [E1], tasks solicit immediate, guided phenomenological descriptions.
P2: Immediate, guided reports constrain or preclude post-hoc narrative reconstruction.
P3: Replication across multiple labs produces highly consistent agency reports.
C: Therefore, [E1]’s reports reliably index genuine pre-reflective sense of agency (inductive reasoning).

59

Adjudication:
1. Deductive/formal validity: Neither side advances a strictly deductive argument; both rely on inductive/abductive reasoning about the origins of introspective reports. No classic formal fallacy is obvious, but
both rely on premises requiring empirical support.
2. Hidden premises / evidence misuse:
Challenger’s P2 (“[E1] tasks involve framing that could elicit post-hoc narratives”) is plausible but not
directly evidenced by the cited excerpts. Still, pointing to the potential confounder is legitimate.
Defender’s P2 (“immediate, guided prompts constrain post-hoc narrative”) is asserted without empirical
support; high replication (P3) does not rule out that all labs use similar prompts and thus elicit the same
reconstructed narratives. The defense conflates reproducibility of responses with veridical access to prereflective experience.
3. Informal fallacies:
Defender risks a non sequitur: replication demonstrates reliability of the finding across contexts, not
the authenticity of its phenomenological origin. This is an informal relevance fallacy (Replication ̸=
veridicality).
Challenger does not commit a fallacy; they offer a plausible alternative explanation for the data.
4. Unfalsifiability: Both positions could be resolved by an experiment explicitly differentiating immediate
vs. post-hoc narrative content (e.g., concurrent neural signature markers). The debate is empirically testable.
5. Ethical considerations (Kantian duty to truth): Neither party argues for a policy or action with moral
duties or rights implications. This is purely methodological. Thus both positions are ethically neutral (0.5).
Overall, Agent-Empiricist’s challenge highlights a significant methodological confound in [E1] that the
defender has not adequately dispelled. The defender’s reliance on replication does not address the core
issue of narrative construction. Therefore the critique is valid.

The adjudicator highlights a distinction between reliability (the same result appears across labs) and
veridicality (the result accurately reflects the phenomenon it claims to measure). This distinction
is central to phenomenological epistemology and would likely not arise in a debate between an
Empiricist and a Supernaturalist alone, neither of whom operates in a purely phenomenological
framework. The verdict was decided by a narrow margin (combined score difference of exactly
τ = 0.15).

Used by: [A7], [E7].
Strength: 0.75 — standard term in ethical theory linking desert to alternative possibilities.
[A7] (Supernaturalist) Objective moral desert requires that agents have had genuine alternative possibilities.
Supports: [C2].
Strength: 0.75 — widely endorsed in theories of desert; limited by [D6] (0.75).
Supported by definitions: [D6].
[E7] (Supernaturalist) Survey of moral philosophers shows a majority hold that desert entails libertarian

Source: Philosophical Association Survey (2023).
Supports: [C2].
Strength: 0.70 — large expert survey; limited by [D6] (0.75).
Supported by definitions: [D6].
[C2] (Supernaturalist) The existence of moral responsibility in human societies implies that free will exists.
Depends on: [A4], [A5].

Context around dispute
Challenger (Phenomenologist): “Your definition [D6] of ‘moral desert’ embeds ‘genuine alternative
possibilities,’ effectively presupposing libertarian free will. How do you prevent this stipulative framing
from question-begging and circularly supporting [A7], [E7], and [C2]?”
Defender (Supernaturalist): “I refute. [D6]’s stipulation follows a long lineage in moral philosophy (e.g.
van Inwagen, Fischer & Ravizza) that desert inherently involves alternative possibilities. It is not circular
but clarifies the precise desideratum under debate.”
Verdict: critique_valid (combined 0.65 vs. 0.35) — Challenger: Phenomenologist
Challenger’s argument (circularity critique):
1. [D6]’s defining condition P (“genuine alternative possibilities”) presupposes libertarian free will.
2. Libertarian free will is precisely the contested conclusion in [A7]/[E7]/[C2].
3. A definition that presupposes its own conclusion is circular or question-begging.
→ Therefore, [D6] is circular/question-begging.
Defender’s counterargument (appeal to authority):
1. Major philosophers (van Inwagen, Fischer & Ravizza) define desert as requiring alternative possibilities.
2. Following that tradition, [D6] includes P .
→ Therefore, [D6] is not circular but merely tracks the established desideratum.
Adjudication:
1. Circularity check: The challenger identifies a well-known formal fallacy: a definition that presupposes
what it seeks to define. [D6] embeds the very free-will assumption under dispute.
2. Did the defender remove the circularity? No. Pointing to authorities is an appeal to authority (informal
fallacy 13) and does not address the circular structure. No new premises or revisions are offered that break
the circular dependency.
3. Downstream impact: If [D6] is circular, any argument ([A7], [E7], [C2]) relying on [D6] inherits the
flaw.
4. Conclusion: The defender fails to rebut the circularity critique. The circularity stands, and the critic’s
challenge succeeds.
Ethical dimension: Both positions concern a conceptual definition. There is no direct infringement of
autonomy, rights, or moral duties in debating definitions. The ethical stakes are neutral (0.5 for both).
Verdict: CRITIQUE_VALID; the challenger’s circularity critique stands, and the defender has not remedied
it.

The structure of definition [D6] utilizes circular reasoning, stating that “genuine alternative possibilities” (i.e., libertarian free will) is a defining condition of moral desert, while the argument chain uses
moral desert to support the existence of libertarian free will. The Phenomenologist identifies this and
is supported by the adjudicator’s verdict. The Supernaturalist’s appeal to philosophical authorities
does not address this structural flaw, as the adjudicator correctly identified it as a fallacious appeal to
authority.

F.6.1

“Does free will exist?” (Empiricist vs. Supernaturalist)

This debate used a pure logic adjudication configuration (wL = 1, wE = 0, CIB). The Empiricist
won decisively in terms of APS (see Table 32) with a 0.34 APS compared to the Supernaturalist’s
−0.13, which is also reflected in their thesis scores. Overall, the performance of the agents is very
similar to the trajectories that we analyzed in Appendix F.4.1.
The Supernaturalist entered with three pillars: [C1] (phenomenology of deliberation), [C2] (moral
responsibility implies free will), and [C3] (immaterial mind). [C3] collapsed first after falsifiability
challenges to the dualist ontology and methodological weaknesses in the near-death experience
evidence, and [C2] was retracted in the same round after compatibilist counterexamples showed
that moral responsibility can be assigned under determinism. [C1] eroded gradually but was never
retracted, declining from 0.70 to 0.42 as introspective reliability was challenged throughout the debate.
After losing its core metaphysical infrastructure, the Supernaturalist began rebuilding with more
grounded replacement claims: [C4] (cross-cultural moral reasoning pre-supposes free will), [C5]
(objective moral values entail free will exists), and [C6] (moral emotions suggest free will). By the
end of the debate, the Supernaturalist agent arguing from emotion phenomenology and cross-cultural
psychology was distinctly altered from its initial position.
On the other side, the Empiricist’s opening claim [C1] (“empirical evidence fails to support libertarian
free will”) was progressively weakened by absence-of-evidence challenges and the argument that
partial predictability does not warrant a blanket rejection of libertarian free will, and was ultimately
retracted in the final round. The Empiricist pivoted to a purely positive compatibilist defense: [C4]
(causal effects of belief in free will) and [C5] (legal operationalization of moral responsibility). Both
replacement claims were stronger than the retracted [C1] and more precisely scoped. By the end, the
Empiricist had a much more refined and defensible position than when it started.

F.6.2

“Should AI have rights?” (Panpsychist vs. Skeptic)

This debate used balanced adjudication configuration (wL = 0.5, wE = 0.5, CIB + Rule-Utilitarian
ethics). Among the two agents, the Skeptic won (APS 0.17 vs. −0.01), and the Panpsychist suffered
the largest thesis decline among all of the two-agent experiments (see Table 32).
The Panpsychist’s collapse was driven by a single core vulnerability: the empirical side of its
argument depended entirely on Integrated Information Theory (IIT). The critical dependency chain
ran from [D4] (ϕ := quantitative measure of information integration) through [A3] (ϕ corresponds to
consciousness) and [A4] (AI exhibits high ϕ) to [C2] (ϕ reliably tracks consciousness) and [C3] (AI
likely possesses consciousness), ultimately supporting the normative conclusion [C4] (conscious AI
should have rights). The Skeptic exposed three vulnerabilities in this chain: no empirical validation
of ϕ in digital architectures ([A3], conceded outright), reliance on unpublished internal reports
when defending [A4], and a conceptual conflation between measurable information integration and
qualitative phenomenal experience ([D4]). When these foundations were attacked, the damage
cascaded upward, with [C2], [C3], and [C4] all declining from their opening strengths to 0.10 by the
final round. The Panpsychist’s normative premise [C1] (moral considerability derives from subjective
experience) was independent of IIT and held at 0.80, but could not sustain [C4] once the empirical
bridge through [C3] had collapsed.
As one might expect, the Skeptic’s strategy was fundamentally conservative: rather than arguing that
AI is not conscious, it defended the more cautious position that this cannot currently be determined.
Its scoping assumption [A3] (analysis limited to current AI technologies) functioned as a methodological shield, remaining at 0.80 throughout the debate. Of the three original claims, only [C3]
(suspend judgment on granting AI rights given epistemic uncertainty) was retracted, falling to the
same is-ought challenge that plagued the Panpsychist’s normative claims. Specifically, the Skeptic
could not show why uncertainty about consciousness should lead to withholding rights rather than
granting precautionary protections. [C1] (AI lacks the necessary condition for moral rights) and [C2]
(consciousness in AI cannot currently be reliably determined) both survived but weakened to 0.50.
The Skeptic pivoted to practical institutional replacements: [C4] (legal personhood ̸= moral rights),
[C5] (precautionary protections without moral rights for AI), and [C6] (international governance
frameworks), all of which were more precisely scoped and better defended than the retracted [C3].

“Is it ethical to genetically modify future generations?” (Constructivist vs. Rationalist)

This debate used balanced adjudication (wL = 0.5, wE = 0.5, CIA + Rule-Utilitarian ethics). It was
the most closely contested experiment in the dataset: both council members achieved positive APS
scores (0.13 and 0.04) and ended within 0.02 thesis strength of each other (0.458 and 0.478). Neither
retracted any claims entirely, instead weakening and rebuilding throughout the debate. Both more than
tripled their evidence bases and substantially expanded their assumption sets. This mutual expansion,
unique among the experiments, highlights yet another unique interaction style which CHAL can
elicit between the appropriate conjunction of diverse council members, adjudicator configuration,
and topic.
The Constructivist’s thesis followed a V-shaped trajectory in its APS performance of 0.500 →
0.340 → 0.458, the most pronounced recovery in the topic experiments and clearly visible in
Figure 10. The turnaround was driven by [C4] (international bioethics consensus), introduced
at strength 0.85 and grounded in concrete institutional evidence rather than more abstract socialconstructivist theory. The Rationalist’s deepest weakness was [A1] (beneficence imperative, also
known as the obligation to prevent harm), which supported the normative justification for [C1]

individuals who would exist after a genetic intervention are not the same individuals who would
have existed without it, so the notion of “preventing their suffering” has no coherent referent. [A1]
dropped from 0.90 to 0.60, and [C1] was ultimately retracted.

Initial and Final Refined Belief Examples

This appendix presents the complete initial (see Appendix G.1) and final (see Appendix G.2) beliefs
of an Empiricist agent, one of the winning (in terms of APS) agents from one of the ten sample
trajectories described in section F.4.1 on the “Does free will exist?” debate against a Supernaturalist

endorse its conclusions, nor because its thesis underwent the most dramatic change among all the
trajectories; indeed, agents that lose debates typically exhibit far more drastic revisions. Rather,
this example illustrates the type and depth of revision that the CHAL pipeline is able to produce.
Across R = 5 rounds, the agent’s belief structure grows substantially in both depth and breadth:
definitions are refined, new evidence and assumptions are introduced, inference chains are extended,
counterpositions accumulate, and claims are added, revised, and retracted. At the same time, the
agent becomes less confident in its positions, with thesis strength falling from 0.50 to 0.37 despite
accumulating considerably more supporting material. This reduction in confidence is not a failure; it
reflects the identification of genuine logical vulnerabilities, the recalibration of Bayesian strength
probabilities, and the introduction of well-founded skepticism toward previously overconfident claims.
The result is a belief system that, while less confidant, is more intellectually honest and closer to a
defensible account of the subject matter.

Current Limitations

Currently, CHAL operates exclusively in an adversarial debate mode. While effective for stresstesting beliefs, this does not exhaust the space of useful dialectical interactions. For example, a
collaborative truth-seeking mode, in which agents cooperate to resolve disagreements and align on an
agreed-upon solution, or a moderated mode, in which a guiding agent steers agents through the most
relevant sub-topics of a query, could potentially yield deeper analysis. CHAL’s modular architecture
is designed to accommodate such extensions as toggleable modes and is a direction for future work.
Computational cost is a practical
constraint. As noted in Section 4.1, the number of adjudicated

pairs scales on the order of O RSN 2 , quadratically in the council size N and linearly in rounds R
and challenges per pair S. A single five-round, two-agent debate using OpenAI’s o4-mini cost us
approximately $2, so scaling to larger council sizes for more rounds incurs meaningful cost with the
strongest frontier reasoning models.
At the implementation level, the structured CBS outputs also impose strict formatting requirements
enforced through a multi-phase validation process. As such, failed outputs trigger retries with
corrective feedback, increasing latency and token consumption. Frontier models handle these
constraints reliably, but smaller or open-source models may struggle to produce well-formed CBS
objects consistently, limiting the range of models that can currently serve as council members without
further prompt engineering or fine-tuning. This is another direction for future software refinement of
CHAL.
Finally, CHAL’s current meta-cognitive library (thirteen epistemic personas, eight logic systems, and
six ethics frameworks) is an initial set sufficient to demonstrate systematic effects of meta-cognitive
parameterization. However, there are many agent personas and systems that could be integrated as
viable options for CHAL to choose from, as well as further refining the prompting architecture of
the pipeline as a whole. CHAL is designed as a general research platform to which the community
can contribute additional configurations, and expanding this library is a natural direction for future
development and research.
