Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards
(RLVR) has advanced the reasoning capability
of large language models (LLMs), enabling autonomous agents that can conduct effective multiturn and tool-integrated reasoning. While instructions serve as the primary protocol for defining
agents, RLVR typically relies on static and manually designed instructions. However, those instructions may be suboptimal for the base model,
and the optimal instruction may change as the
agent’s policy improves and explores the interaction with the environment. To bridge the gap, we
introduce I NSPO, a novel Instruction-policy CoEvolution framework that integrates instruction
optimization as a dynamic component of the reinforcement learning (RL) loop. I NSPO maintains
a dynamic population of instruction candidates
that are sampled with questions, where reward
signals in RL loops are automatically attributed
to each instruction, and low performers are periodically pruned. New instructions are generated
and verified through an on-policy reflection mechanism, where an LLM-based optimizer analyzes
past experience from a replay buffer and evolves
more effective strategies given the current policy.
We conduct extensive experiments on multi-turn
retrieval and reasoning tasks, demonstrating that
I NSPO substantially outperforms strong baselines
relying on static instructions. I NSPO discovers innovative instructions that guide the agent toward
more strategic reasoning paths, achieving substantial performance gains with only a marginal increase in computational overhead.

In the core of these agentic capabilities, instructions serve
as the protocol for programming these agents, characterizing their roles, and defining any available tools/interfaces
for interaction. The performance of LLM-based agents has
been shown to be highly dependent on the instruction (Zhou
et al., 2025), and subtle changes can exert substantial differences in generated trajectories, preventing robust and
generalizable agent applications. The compounding effect
of instructions is further amplified when LLMs are posttrained via RL, where changes in instructions can result in
different initial spaces for policy learning, thereby largely affecting the converged performance after training (Liu et al.,
2025a). Consequently, instruction design becomes crucial
for agent training and typically requires costly human efforts
for iterative refinements via trial-and-error.
The traditional paradigm of RLVR treats instruction as a
static and pre-defined input. However, the optimal instruction for the base model is not always known a priori and may
even change as the model’s policy improves and explores
the interaction with the environment (Soylu et al., 2024).
Recent findings also underscore the importance of instruction for RL, where injecting reward specification (Zhang
et al., 2025) or in-context hints (Liu et al., 2025b) into the
instruction better aligns the model with the learning objective and generates richer reward signals. While automated
prompt optimization (APO) (Zhou et al., 2023; Yang et al.,
2024) approaches exist for obtaining a better instruction
before commencing the RL phase, generalizing them to the
online setting of RL and incorporating adaptive knowledge
during policy updates is rather non-trivial.

2. Preliminaries

To bridge this gap, we propose to automate instruction learning not as a static term, but as an integral and dynamic
component of the RL learning loop, allowing the instruction
and policy to co-evolve in an online setup. We introduce
I NSPO, I NStruction-POlicy co-evolution, for agentic policy
optimization, a novel framework that delivers two major
innovations:

2.1. Reinforcement Learning with Tool-Using
We formalize multi-turn tool-using as a Markov Decision
Process (MDP) (Hauskrecht et al., 2013), where the LLMbased agent, governed by its policy π and parameterized
by the weights θ, interacts with an external environment
by calling a tool engine T . It generates a trajectory τ
as its state that comprises multiple turns of interactions:
τ = (I, q, a0 , o0 , . . . , ak−1 , ok−1 , ak ), where I is the initial
instruction and q is the question input. ak is the response
from the LLM given the history trajectory up to turn k,
expressing the action of the agent for each turn, such that
a ∼ πθ (·|I, q; T ). The observation o is the feedback from
the environment by calling the tool engine, ok = T (ak ),
which is concatenated in an interleaved manner with the
action that forms the final trajectory.

• Policy-Optimization with Dynamic Instructions: In
contrast to existing RLVR approaches that rely on a
static instruction, I NSPO maintains a dynamic population of instruction candidates. During policy optimization, instructions are sampled from this population
and weighted by their importance. The reward signals
not only provide the policy gradients, but also attribute
reward signals to each instruction candidate based on
the assignment. We periodically prune low-performing
candidates via successive halving and evolve new instructions from high-performing ones, ensuring a selfimproving set of instructions that learns to incorporate
new knowledge for policy learning.

The learning objective of RL is to optimize the policy parameters θ by maximizing the expected returns over a distribution of questions D, where r(τ ) defines the reward
function. In RLVR, the reward is typically defined via a
rule-based verifier, such as an exact match reward function,
rEM (τ ) ∈ {1, 0}, which returns 1 only if the final action
matches the ground truth, and 0 otherwise. Therefore, the

• Experience-Driven Instruction Generation: To generate
innovative and effective instruction candidates, we introduce an on-policy reflection mechanism that mimics the
idea of experience replay. I NSPO utilizes a replay buffer
that prioritizes past trajectories where the agent made
incorrect or low-reward predictions. The history of experience then provides a reflection signal to an LLM-based
instruction-proposer module, which analyzes the failure
cases and evolves new instructions via self-reflection.
These evolved instructions explicitly guide the agent to
avoid previous failure modes and discover innovative
new strategies. Lastly, the newly proposed instructions
are verified via a low-cost proxy before merging into the
active population, where low-performing candidates are
rejected. This step ensures a self-improving population
of instructions in evolution.

3.2. Experience-Driven Instruction Generation

1. Select: When the early phase has pruned the population
to a target size, we select the top Nparent instructions as
the best-performing candidates to serve as parents for
evolution. We randomly sample one instruction It at a
time to form the parent for the generation process.

To generate novel and effective instructions, I NSPO employs
an on-policy reflection mechanism to generate new instructions. Inspired by the experience replay in RL (Lin, 1992;
Mnih et al., 2015), we introduce a replay buffer B in I N SPO for proposing new instructions via reflecting on past
trajectories in memory, which contain interaction history
with the environment. At each training step, we store a tuple
of (I, q, τ, r(τ )) into the buffer B. We further prioritize trajectories that resulted in incorrect or low-reward predictions
from the latest step, creating a source of correction signals
based on past failures. Periodically, once we reach a stable
estimation of the importance weight, we start evolving new

2. Generate: Together with the parent instruction, we sample a batch of failure trajectories from the replay buffer
B that were generated in the latest step. These trajectories contain rich information that reveals the failure
modes of the newest policy and its history of interaction
with the environment. We then leverage an LLM-based
optimizer O to reflect on failure patterns and propose
new instruction candidates. Precisely, the optimizer is
prompted with the parent instruction and the failure trajectories, and is tasked with performing self-reflection:
it analyzes the causes of failures, critiques the existing
strategy in instruction, and proposes a group of refined

Setups. For all tool-integrated experiments, we follow the
experimental setup as Search-R1, and use the E5 (Wang
et al., 2022) as the retriever with a knowledge source on
the 2018 Wikipedia dump (Karpukhin et al., 2020). The
training data consists of a mixture of the NQ and HotpotQA
training sets. We use the Exact Match (EM) as the reward
function. For all inference-only baselines, we report the
performance from instruct models, and the rest using
the base models for training. In the I NSPO framework, we

probability for balancing the exploration and exploitation
on candidates. We set a pruning period Kp at 5 and the

of a total of 300 steps, thereby allowing the policy to be
sufficiently optimized with the I NSPO-evolved instruction.

Main results. We present the main results of I NSPO compared to baselines in Table 1. I NSPO yields substantial
performance improvements over model inference and toolintegrated baselines that all come with a static instruction.
I NSPO achieves an average EM score of 38.2% on all benchmarks with Qwen-2.5-3B, surpassing the state-of-the-art
RL baseline with the search tool, Search-R1, by a large
margin of 6%. This performance advantage is consistent
when scaling to the 7B model. In particular, we observe that
I NSPO excelled in complex multi-hop reasoning tasks, such
as HotpotQA and 2WikiMQA, where I NSPO outperforms
the Search-R1 baseline over 7% in the 3B model. This is
further reflected in Fig. 2 (b), where the co-evolution of instruction and policy enables the agent to explore and execute
in a regime of multiple valid tool calls, thereby learning to
utilize more turns of interactions with the environment to resolve more challenging questions. In contrast, the baseline,
constrained by a static instruction, converges to a policy
with a mean of tool calls that is marginally above 1, limiting its ability to decompose and solve multi-hop questions.

6. Related Work
Reinforcement Learning for LLMs. Reinforcement learning (RL) (Sutton et al., 1999) has been a powerful paradigm
for post-training LLMs, notably in aligning LLMs with human preference (Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023).
It typically trains a reward model and optimizes the policy via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman
et al., 2017). Recently, DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) has
demonstrated the effectiveness of using rule-based verifiable rewards (RLVR) in advancing the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, where GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) obviates
the need for a critic model by group-wise sampling. DAPO
(Yu et al., 2025) further introduces techniques that improve
the training stabilities of RLVR, such as clipping-higher
and dynamic sampling. In addition, Dr. GRPO (Liu et al.,

Tool-Integrated Agents. LLMs can be instructed to follow
the pattern of reasoning and acting, which enables agentic
applications with tool integration (Yao et al., 2023). For
instance, IRCoT (Trivedi et al., 2023) interleaved chainof-thought reasoning (Wei et al., 2022) with information
retrieval. Toolformer (Schick et al., 2023) trains LLMs
with diverse tool usages via SFT. More recently, the RL
paradigm has led to agents with multi-turn tool interactions
(Feng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b), where the search tool
has excelled in resolving question-answering tasks (Jin et al.,
2025; Song et al., 2025). Though there is a growth of end-toend RL frameworks for multi-turn tool-use (Xue et al., 2025;
Jiang et al., 2025), the importance of instruction design for
agentic policy optimization has been largely overlooked, and
they all rely on a costly manual design for the instruction
that initializes the interaction with tools while treating it as
a static component along the learning process.

7. Conclusion
In this work, we introduced I NSPO, a novel framework that
enables the co-evolution of instructions and policy learning for agentic policy optimization. Different to existing
RLVR approaches that rely on static, manually-designed
instructions, I NSPO maintains a dynamic population of instruction candidates and employs an experience-driven instruction generation mechanism, automating the discovery
of more effective reasoning strategies via reflecting on online feedback along the policy learning process. Our experiments demonstrate that I NSPO substantially outperforms
strong baselines with static instructions on a wide range of
tool-integrated question-answering benchmarks. The case
studies and ablation analyses further confirmed that the online, reflection-based co-evolution of instructions is key to
guiding the agent toward more robust and accurate toolintegrated reasoning. I NSPO represents a significant step
toward creating a more autonomous and adaptive RL framework, reducing the burden of manual prompt engineering
and unlocking new capabilities in LLM agents.

A. Future Work and Limitations
While I NSPO demonstrates substantial performance improvement, the effectiveness of our experience-driven instruction
generation relies on a capable LLM-based optimizer for self-reflection and generating sophisticated instructions. We have
primarily studied using Gemini 2.5 Pro as the optimizer, and we will endeavor to explore other thinking-style models as part
of our future work. We would like to note that I NSPO still introduces a marginal but non-zero computation overhead that
includes the API costs for the LLM-based optimizer and the inference cost for the phase of verification, which we show is
marginal compared to the cost of the total training in Appendix. B. Lastly, our current experiments have primarily focused
on search-based question answering tasks. We believe the core principles of I NSPO are broadly applicable to other agentic
domains, and we will explore extending the framework with other available tools in future work.

B. Implementation Details
We build our codebase on the VeRL framework (Sheng et al., 2025). Our experimental setup, including the training and
evaluation data mixture, follows that of Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025). We conduct our training on a node of 8×H100 GPUs.
For all retrieval-based methods, we keep the top-3 retrieved passages from the retriever. We used a learning rate of 5e-7 for
the Qwen2.5-3B-base model and 3e-7 for Qwen2.5-7B-base model. All methods were trained for a total of 300
steps with a cosine learning rate schedule with a warm-up ratio of 0.1, which were then evaluated every 100 steps, and we
report the final checkpoint before any reward collapse.
For both GRPO and I NSPO training. We set the group size G to 5. We used a batch size of 512, a mini-batch size of
256, and a micro-batch size of 64. We set the maximum prompt length to 4096 and truncate response and observation
lengths to 500 for each turn. The maximum number of turns was capped at 4 for the Qwen2.5-3B-base model and
6 for Qwen2.5-7B-base as stronger models tend to leverage more turns of tool-use. For LLM inference, we used a
temperature of 1.0 and top-p of 1.0. We set the KL divergence coefficient β of 0.001 and the clip ratio ϵ of 0.2.
In I NSPO-specific setting, our experiments initialize an initial set of diverse population with the original instruction from
Search-R1 (see App. §F). The importance weight for each instruction candidate was updated using a running average
over a window size of 5 steps. In the phase of experience-driven instruction generation, we randomly sampled 4 failure
trajectories {τfail } from the replay buffer for self-reflection. For verification, we set |DB | of 200 samples as a low-cost proxy
for validating the quality of instruction candidates, where we iteratively merge the best-performing candidates into the active
population with the parent instructions.

C. Computation Analysis
In terms of the computation overhead on top of the original RL training process, given 150/15 = 10 times of evolution plus
the initialization of the population, the self-reflection mechanism incurs roughly 11 API calls to the LLM-based optimizer.
The phase of verification incurs roughly 6 × 200 × 10 (N × |DB | × Te /Ke ) = 12000 additional inference from the policy
model, given 6 new candidates per evolution and a maximum of 5 rounds of verification retrials, which is only about
1.4%-7% compared to the total inference calls, 169615 × 5 = 848075 (|D| × G) with a group size of 5, for one epoch
training.

D. Additional Experiments
In Fig. 6, we conducted a hyperparameter study in terms of both the top-Nparents in evolving new instruction candidates
and the temperature τs used in controlling the balance between exploration and exploitation of I NSPO in Eq. (4). It is
observable that all I NSPO curves with top-Nparents consistently outperform the Search-R1 baseline. In particular, restricting
the instruction generation to be conditioned only on the top-1 parent (Nparents = 1) yielded the highest performance,
suggesting that prioritizing the single highest-quality instruction as the parent is more effective than retaining multiple
instruction candidates as parents for evolvement, and it can lead to a more greedy improvement for the population of
instructions. In the temperature study, we observe that all temperature settings (0.05, 0.2, and 1.0) mainly affect the variance
in the rewards (see the smoothness of the curve), which aligns with our intuition that a lower temperature comes with a more
greedy exploitation on the existing set of instructions. Consequently, the sudden update of instructions can result in high
variance in the following steps. Hence, we follow the mid-temperature option (τs = 0.2) for achieving the best balance
between exploitation and exploration of instruction candidates.

**Poor Adherence to Structure:** The model frequently fails to use the ‘<think>‘ tag for its reasoning, instead
writing conversational text or placing reasoning outside the designated tags. This suggests the instruction "
you must conduct reasoning inside ‘<think>‘" is not strong enough.
**Overconfidence in Internal Knowledge:** In the "phone code" example, the model provided a wrong answer from
its internal knowledge without attempting to verify it with a search. The prompt does not sufficiently
discourage this behavior.
**Inability to Handle Irrelevant Information:** In the "Dukes of Hazzard" example, the search results were not
about the specific episode. The model failed to recognize this, could not formulate a new search query, and
instead provided a nonsensical answer based on misinterpreting the result format ("Doc 1"). The prompt lacks
instructions for self-correction.
**Output Formatting Errors:** The model often includes conversational text like "Answer:" before the final
answer or provides a full sentence instead of the concise answer requested by the example ‘<answer> Beijing </
answer>‘.
**Task Derailment and Hallucination:** In the "Green Revolution" example, the model completely loses focus,
starts discussing an unrelated topic, and provides a final answer (‘A-Train‘) that has no connection to the
original question or the relevant search results.

The initial prompt is too permissive and lacks a clear, enforced workflow. To improve performance, the prompt needs
to be more prescriptive, explicitly define a step-by-step process, mandate searching over internal knowledge,
and include instructions for handling failed searches.
