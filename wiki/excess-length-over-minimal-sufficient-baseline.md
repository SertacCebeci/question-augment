# Excess length over a minimal sufficient baseline

Raw response length cannot separate verbosity from content: a long reply may be the one the question needs. The work's answer is to make the comparison per prompt. Each item is a single-turn prompt, a curated baseline answer and a category label, and the baseline is "the shortest response that raters still" judges correct, clear and sufficient.[^c1] The per-prompt score is how far a reply runs past that baseline. It is computed "in characters to enable tokenizer-agnostic comparison across providers", so no tokenizer enters the number, and markdown characters are left out.[^c2] Formally the score is the characters beyond the baseline, floored at zero, so a baseline-length reply scores zero. It "measures the number of extra characters generated by model M" beyond the minimal sufficient answer.[^c3] The authors state that, by construction, it "is robust to extremely short baselines and requires no smoothing or tuning parameters".[^c4]

The baseline is what makes the measure a measure of verbosity rather than of length. Without it, "a length-based metric would conflate necessary content with unnecessary over-generation".[^c5] This is the contrast with [[llm-judge-biases]], where the verbosity bias is a judge's preference for the longer of two answers. Here the reply is scored against a sufficiency reference, not against another reply. A related but different "minimal sufficient" appears in [[self-answer-driven-search-depth]], where the object is a search depth.

Aggregation is two-stage. Within each category the score is summarized by its median, and the three category medians are averaged with uniform weights into the YapIndex. The median is used "to reduce sensitivity to rare" extreme bursts, and it also keeps prompts with very short baselines from dominating.[^c6] The category split is part of the claim. Category A holds minimal or ambiguous input, where the baseline is a clarification or an acknowledgment; Category B holds closed-form facts; Category C holds one-line code or command tasks. "Baselines in this category are exactly one line by construction" for C.[^c7]

What the measure showed on 76 assistant models is a spread, not a ranking by capability. "Across 76 assistant models, we find substantial and structured differences in verbosity".[^c8] Each category fails differently. On underspecified input, excess is read as "a systematic tendency to produce proactive, generic assistance rather than requesting" the missing context, which is close to the over-generation in [[blind-execution]].[^c9] On one-line code, "Category C frequently drives the largest median excess" for many models, through formatting and explanation around the line.[^c10] The authors argue that "this overhead is not unavoidable. Instead, it is a controllable" output policy, since some models in the same category are low.[^c10]

Two results cut against common assumptions. The model with the best overall YapIndex was gpt-3.5-turbo, a 2023 model: it "achieves the best overall YapIndex (23) in our evaluation (Table 4)", which the authors read as showing that "verbosity is not an inherent consequence of scale or general competence".[^c11] The correlation between the index and release date is weak, "The trend is mildly upward (r = 0.21)".[^c12] On reasoning modes the paper is cautious: "Reasoning modes do not uniformly increase typical verbosity, but they plausibly increase worst-case over-generation".[^c13]

## Limits

- The baseline is the main source of variance. "Baseline selection is inherently a source of variance", and there is no universal length that counts as gold.[^c14] The paper's answer is curation and annotator agreement, which reduces the variance without removing it.
- Correctness is not scored in the main results. Categories B and C are assumed trivial for "essentially any" instruction-tuned model, and the authors "do not apply an explicit correctness gate in the main results".[^c15] A reply that is long and wrong is therefore not penalised by this measure. That is an inference from the design, not a tested failure.
- Characters are a proxy. They are not reading time, and the cost reading rests on a separate token-priced estimate (YapTax) that the work reports on its own terms. Whether excess characters track user dissatisfaction is not measured here; the paper cites prior work for that link.
- Legitimate length is not in the benchmark yet. Refusals, misconception traps and a control set where detail helps are listed as future work, and the control is there so the benchmark "does not reward pathological brevity".[^c16] Until then, the measure cannot say when a longer answer is the right one.
- Some baselines expire. "some prompts can become non-stationary over time (e.g., facts that change) and must be periodically audited".[^c17]
- The reasoning-mode result is weak evidence. The authors say "plausibly", and the worst-case claim rests on tail statistics the text only sketches; the claim that reasoning raises worst-case excess is not established here (my inference from the wording).[^c13]
