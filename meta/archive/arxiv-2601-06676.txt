Autonomous Deep Research

Abstract
Large Language Model (LLM)-based deep research agents
perform multi-step reasoning, web exploration, and long-form
report generation. In these long-horizon workflows, early deviations from user intent can misdirect research and propagate
through planning, search, and synthesis, making timely interaction essential. However, existing benchmarks primarily treat
deep research as a static input-output task, overlooking agents’
ability to elicit and use user feedback. We introduce IDRBench, a benchmark for evaluating interactive deep research
with controlled opportunities for clarification. Within a common workflow and stage-wise interaction budget, IDRBench
compares autonomous and interactive trajectories, measuring interaction benefit through changes in task-specific report
alignment and interaction cost through turns and tokens. Comprehensive experiments on 100 tasks with seven proprietary
and open-weight LLMs show that interaction improves all
five alignment measures for every model, yielding an average
gain of 6.39 points, while revealing distinct trade-offs among
autonomous performance, alignment gain, and communication cost. At the task level, interaction improves performance
in 74.4% of cases but degrades it in 19.9%, demonstrating
that access to clarification alone does not guarantee better
outcomes: success depends on what agents ask and how effectively they incorporate the resulting feedback.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have revolutionized information seeking, evolving from single-turn question answering to deep research agents that independently reason, navigate the web, and generate long-form reports (Zheng et al.
2024; Li et al. 2025; Zheng et al. 2025; Yun and Jang 2025).
Unlike traditional Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
systems (Lewis et al. 2020; Gao et al. 2023; Wang et al.
2024) that handle isolated queries, these agents iteratively
plan, search, and synthesize information to address openended user needs (Wei et al. 2025; Du et al. 2026).
Despite these advances, deep research remains largely autonomous, with agents controlling the trajectory after the initial query (Zheng et al. 2025). As new evidence and alternatives emerge, choices about scope, emphasis, audience, and
time horizon can amplify early intent mismatches throughout
planning, search, and synthesis. Recent clarification mechanisms for planning and tool use (Zhang et al. 2024b, 2025,

Yet, benchmark design has not kept pace with this shift.
They largely formulate deep research as a static input-output
task and evaluate final reports through retrieval, factuality,
and citation-based metrics, leaving three important gaps:
• Autonomous Task Formulation: Agents receive fixed
prompts and complete the entire research process without
opportunities to seek clarification or refine user intent (Du
et al. 2026; Coelho et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2025).
• Lack of Controlled Interaction Environment: Reliable
comparison requires consistent feedback, while repeated
human-in-the-loop evaluation is costly and difficult to
scale (Yoon et al. 2024; Ye et al. 2025; Yao et al. 2025).
• Outcome-Only Evaluation: By focusing on final reports,
existing benchmarks overlook whether interaction improves intent alignment and the cost it introduces during
research (Shao et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2025; Du et al. 2026).
To bridge these gaps, we introduce IDRBench, an
Interactive Deep Research Benchmark for systematically
evaluating the interactive capabilities of deep research
agents. Its benchmark protocol compares complete autonomous and interactive trajectories under the same research

workflow, tools, execution limits, and task target. In the interactive setting, models receive a shared stage-wise clarification budget and standardized, question-conditioned feedback, while independently deciding whether, when, and what
to ask and how to use the response. IDRBench constructs
tasks with varying levels of query specificity while retaining
the corresponding reports as consistent evaluation targets. Its
interaction-aware metrics jointly capture outcome and burden: Report Alignment measures document-, structure-, and
aspect-level agreement with the task target, while Interaction
Cost quantifies the associated turns and tokens.
We evaluate seven representative proprietary and openweight LLMs across 100 research tasks. Interaction improves
all five alignment measures for every model, yielding an
average gain of 6.39 points. However, the models exhibit
distinct interactive profiles: GPT-5.1 achieves the highest autonomous score (75.59), Claude-Sonnet-4.5 the highest interactive score (80.47), and Llama-4-Maverick the largest
improvement (+10.96). At the task level, interaction improves performance in 74.4% of cases but degrades it in
19.9%, demonstrating that clarification opportunities alone
do not guarantee better outcomes. Additional diagnostics
across feedback models and context settings further characterize the stability of the evaluation protocol. These findings establish interactive capability as a distinct dimension
complementary to autonomous model strength and position
IDRBench as a reusable testbed for controlled comparison.
Our contributions are threefold:
• Interactive Deep Research Benchmark: We introduce
IDRBench, which extends deep research evaluation from
static input-output assessment to complete autonomous
and interactive research trajectories.
• Controlled Interaction Protocol: We develop a controlled protocol that provides consistent, task-grounded
responses under a shared stage-wise clarification budget,
enabling scalable and reproducible comparison without
repeated human-in-the-loop evaluation.
• Interaction-Aware Metrics: We jointly measure taskspecific report-alignment and interaction cost, revealing
how models differ in when they seek clarification, what
information they elicit, and how effectively they use the
resulting feedback.

Related Work

Deep Research Frameworks. Deep research systems combine long-horizon reasoning, web exploration, and citationgrounded reporting through iterative planning and tool
use (Shao et al. 2024; Coelho et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2025;
Zhou et al. 2024b; Zhao et al. 2026). Existing approaches include multi-agent frameworks with specialized roles (Zheng
et al. 2024; Alzubi et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025) and end-toend agents trained with reinforcement learning (Jin et al.
2025; Zheng et al. 2025). Despite their growing capabilities,
these systems are predominantly designed and evaluated for
autonomous execution, providing limited insight into how
agents seek and use user guidance. Rather than proposing
another agent architecture, IDRBench instantiates a common

deep research workflow with controlled clarification opportunities across planning, research, and generation, enabling
systematic comparison of interactive behavior.
Deep Research Benchmarks. Existing benchmarks evaluate individual capabilities such as web retrieval (Wei et al.
2025; Zhou et al. 2025), long-form writing (Bai et al. 2025;
Wu et al. 2025), citation-grounded reports (Shao et al. 2024),
and long-horizon research (Du et al. 2026). However, most
formulate evaluation as a fixed prompt-to-report task and
assess only the final output. Consequently, they cannot determine whether research-time clarification improves alignment
with user intent or quantify the communication overhead it
introduces. IDRBench addresses this limitation by comparing complete autonomous and interactive trajectories for the
same model and task under matched tools, execution limits,
and evaluation criteria.
Interactive Agents. Clarification has been extensively studied in conversational search for resolving underspecified information needs (Rahmani et al. 2023; Tavakoli et al. 2022;
Feng et al. 2023; Aliannejadi et al. 2021). Recent LLM
agents extend this capability to planning and tool use (Zhang
et al. 2024b, 2025, 2024a), while τ -bench (Yao et al. 2025)
and Ask-or-Assume (Edwards and Schuster 2026) employ
simulated users for scalable evaluation. Within deep research, Interaction-Driven Browsing (Yun and Jang 2025)
and STEER (Ai et al. 2026) incorporate iterative feedback,
with STEER emphasizing adaptive pausing and live persona
modeling. These studies demonstrate the value of interaction but do not provide a common protocol for comparing
existing deep research agents across complete autonomous
and interactive trajectories. Drawing on controlled-feedback
environments used in conversational recommendation, product search, tool use, and social-agent evaluation (Yoon et al.
2024; Ye et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2024a),
IDRBench provides consistent, question-conditioned feedback under a shared stage-wise budget.

3

IDRBench

We present IDRBench, a benchmark for systematically comparing deep research agents under both autonomous and interactive task settings (Figure 2).

3.1

Benchmark Protocol

Task Setting. Open-ended research requests can support
multiple reasonable reports, and users may not exhaustively
specify task-specific preferences, such as coverage and emphasis, in the initial query. During interaction, users can express these preferences in response to an agent’s questions,
guiding subsequent planning, search, and generation. The
same preferences also inform how users assess whether the
completed report meets their expectations. IDRBench follows this setting by pairing a broader query with a report that
reflects one set of task-specific choices. The paired report
provides a consistent task target for feedback and evaluation
across models. It represents one reasonable outcome for the
query among other possible reports.

The feedback available in each trajectory depends on the

research process. IDRBench therefore compares the taskspecific report-alignment change realized by each model

Basic Architecture. To support this comparison, we instantiate a common workflow using the langchain-ai open
deep research architecture (LangChain-AI 2025), a modular pipeline comprising Planning, Research Loop, and Generation. These stages expose comparable interaction points
while keeping execution conditions consistent across models.
The pipeline coordinates four agents. The Planner converts
the query into a structured research brief. During research,
the Supervisor decomposes the brief, assigns sub-tasks to
Researchers, and monitors progress. Researchers explore assigned topics and summarize findings, while the Reporter
synthesizes them into a coherent long-form report.
Controlled Interaction Protocol. IDRBench permits
bounded interaction during Planning, Research Loop, and
Generation. Every model in the interactive setting receives
stage-wise maxima of one, three, and one clarification turn.
Within this budget, Clarification determines whether, when,
and what to ask, while Controlled Feedback returns a reply
under a standardized policy. The stages cover initial framing,
evidence-driven adjustment, and final emphasis, respectively.

Clarification. Clarification involves two roles. The Evaluator determines whether interaction is warranted by balancing
the benefit of resolving ambiguity against the interruption
burden, based on the current research context and remaining budget. When interaction is triggered, the Questioner
uses the Evaluator’s rationale and relevant research state to
formulate one or two focused questions about scope, direction, or emphasis, adapting to the user’s language style. The
two-role design supports controlled stage-wise comparisons
(Section 4.5) by triggering question generation at designated
stages without an Evaluator decision.
Controlled Feedback. Controlled Feedback provides
question-conditioned guidance under a common policy
across models, while keeping private task context inaccessible to the research agent. The guidance available in each
trajectory therefore depends on what the agent asks. We implement this role with an LLM policy that answers clarification questions in a natural first-person style using the paired
report as private task context. The policy is instructed to
remain question-relevant and high-level, without directly exposing the report or introducing unsupported requirements.
Prompt details are provided in Appendix A.

3.2

Data Construction

IDRBench uses 100 DeepResearch Bench query-report pairs
spanning 22 fields and evenly split between Chinese and English (Du et al. 2026). This scale is consistent with costly,
long-horizon agent evaluations that involve multi-step planning, web exploration, and long-form generation. However,
source queries are often highly detailed (up to ∼800 tokens),

leaving limited room for meaningful clarification.
We therefore use an LLM to compress each query by 10%–
90%, varying its specificity while retaining its topic and core
objective (see Appendix B). Each compressed query remains
paired with its original report as the task target. We manually
verify all queries for consistency, naturalness, and safety.
Ethical considerations are provided in Appendix C.

3.3

Model Selection

To cover diverse modeling paradigms, we study four proprietary LLMs: GPT-5.1 (OpenAI 2025), Gemini-2.5-Pro (Comanici et al. 2025), Claude-Sonnet-4.5 (Anthropic 2025),
and Grok-4.1-Fast (xAI 2025). These represent leading commercial systems optimized for long-context reasoning and
tool use. We also include three open-weight models, Qwen3235B (Yang et al. 2025), Llama-4-Maverick (Meta AI 2025),
and DeepSeek-V3.2 (DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025), spanning varied scaling and alignment characteristics.
Although Gemini-3-series Pro models (Google DeepMind
2026) are newer, preliminary experiments showed unstable
compatibility with the LangChain-based long-horizon workflow. We therefore use Gemini-2.5-Pro for more reliable
structured prompting and tool invocation.

3.4

Interaction-Aware Metrics

Because clarification can redirect the entire research trajectory, IDRBench evaluates each model’s joint policy for
whether, when, and what to ask and how to use feedback,
rather than attributing effects to individual questions. It captures both outcome and burden through Report Alignment,
which measures agreement with the task target, and Interaction Cost, which measures turns and tokens.
Report Alignment. Report Alignment is evaluated through
three complementary views: document-level semantic similarity, multi-granularity structural coverage, and aspect-level
coverage of task requirements. These measures characterize task-specific coverage and emphasis and complement
evaluations of factual correctness, citation reliability, source
grounding, and other general report-quality criteria.

Interaction Cost. Beyond Report Alignment, effective interaction balances alignment gains against communication
costs, measured in Interaction Turns and Interaction Tokens.
Interaction Turns. Interaction turns measure how often
agents request feedback and allocate their shared stage-wise
budget across Planning, Research Loop, and Generation. Because every model operates under the same stage-wise limits, turn counts provide a comparable view of interaction
frequency and budget allocation. More turns may improve
clarification but also increase interruptions and latency.
Interaction Tokens. Interaction tokens quantify communication per interaction. We separately report question tokens
sent by the agent and response tokens returned by the feedback provider. Token usage reflects the trade-off between
informativeness and communication cost; therefore, fewer
tokens are not necessarily better.

Experimental Setup

For each model-task pair, we compare autonomous execution without clarification against interactive execution under
the shared stage-wise budget and feedback policy. Following Open Deep Research (LangChain-AI 2025), each evaluated LLM fills all six agent roles (i.e., Planner, Supervisor, Researcher, Reporter, Evaluator, and Questioner), while
lightweight models such as GPT-4.1-nano handle frequent
utilities to reduce cost.
GPT-5.1 implements the common feedback policy; Tavily
provides retrieval,1 and other settings follow the default configuration. Main results use all 100 tasks; feedback-model
robustness, context sensitivity, and interaction timing use
the same 30-task subset, and the human-feedback diagnostic
uses 10 tasks for a focused comparison. The hyperparameter
configurations are provided in Appendix D, and the experimental environment is detailed in Appendix E.

Report Alignment

Table 1 presents the report alignment results for seven models in autonomous and interactive modes, along with their
interactive benefits. It also includes the unweighted average
alignment score and estimated per-report API cost.
Main Results. Interaction improves all report alignment
measures for every model, but reveals distinct leaders: GPT5.1 achieves the highest autonomous score (75.59), ClaudeSonnet-4.5 the highest interactive score (80.47), and Llama4-Maverick the largest gain (+10.96) despite the lowest interactive score (65.78). These ranking shifts show that autonomous strength, interactive performance, and alignment
gain capture distinct capabilities.
Gain vs. Autonomous. Models with lower autonomous
scores generally exhibit larger interactive gains. Llama-4Maverick and Grok-4.1-Fast improve by +10.96 and +7.97,
compared with +3.37 for GPT-5.1 and +4.96 for ClaudeSonnet-4.5. This pattern is consistent with greater taskspecific alignment headroom at lower autonomous scores.
Granularity Shift. Interactive gains also vary across evaluation granularities. Llama-4-Maverick improves most on
Paragraph F1, Chunk F1, and LLM-ACS, reflecting broader
structural coverage and task-aspect alignment. In contrast,
Claude-Sonnet-4.5 achieves its largest gain on Sentence F1,
reflecting finer-grained content matching. These patterns
show that similar aggregate improvements can arise from
different forms of report alignment across models.

5

Conclusions

We introduce IDRBench, which extends deep research evaluation from fixed prompt-to-report tasks to controlled comparisons of complete autonomous and interactive trajectories. It
combines specificity-controlled queries with paired report
targets, a common multi-stage workflow with shared clarification budgets and standardized feedback, and interactionaware metrics for multilevel report alignment and turn/token
costs. These designs enable scalable and reproducible evaluation of whether, when, and what agents ask, and how effectively they use the resulting feedback. Extensive experiments on 100 tasks and seven LLMs show that interaction
improves all report alignment measures, but its benefits vary
substantially across models, tasks, research stages, and communication patterns. These findings establish interactive capability as a distinct dimension that cannot be inferred from
autonomous performance alone.
IDRBench uses standardized feedback to support consistent and reproducible cross-model comparison. This setting
reduces variation arising from individual interpretation, familiarity, and fatigue, but does not capture the full diversity
of real users. Broader studies with independent participants
can further examine real-world interaction.

A

Core Interaction Prompt Designs

This section presents the prompts implementing the three
components of the Controlled Interaction Protocol introduced in Section 3.1 of the main paper: the Evaluator, Questioner, and Controlled Feedback modules.

A.1

Evaluator

The Evaluator acts as the interaction gatekeeper (Figure 6).
Given the current research state and remaining interaction budget, it determines whether the expected benefit of
clarification outweighs the interruption burden. Its stagespecific guidelines distinguish ambiguities in initial framing,
evidence-driven research, and final report synthesis.

A.2

Questioner

When interaction is triggered, the Questioner formulates one
or two targeted clarification questions (Figure 7). It focuses
on high-level ambiguities in scope, intent, emphasis, and report structure while avoiding trivial technical details. Questions are kept concise and adapted to the language of the
original query to reduce user burden.

A.3

Controlled Feedback

Controlled Feedback answers clarification questions under
a common question-conditioned policy (Figure 8). It uses
the paired report as private task context but is instructed
to provide only concise, high-level guidance in natural user
language, without mentioning or directly reproducing the
report. Appendix G quantifies the remaining object-level
responses.

B

Examples of Query Compression

Table 6 provides examples of the query-compression procedure described in Section 3.2 of the main paper. Each Source
Query specifies detailed constraints, background information, and output requirements, whereas the corresponding
Compressed Query reduces this detail while preserving the
topic and core objective. The original paired report remains
unchanged and serves as a consistent task target.
For example, Example 68 replaces a detailed description
of the standard Cluster Autoscaler’s reliance on pending pods
with a broader request for alternative approaches. All compressed queries are manually reviewed for source-task consistency, naturalness, and safety.

C

Ethical Considerations

IDRBench builds on a publicly available dataset and follows its license and usage conditions. We manually review
compressed queries for task consistency, naturalness, and potentially sensitive content. The benchmark focuses on report
alignment and does not directly assess factual accuracy, citation reliability, or source quality. Its scores should therefore
be interpreted within this scope.

D

Hyperparameter Configuration

This section provides the execution and metric configurations used in the main experiments. Table 7 summarizes the
settings omitted from the main paper for brevity.

D.1

Framework Execution Parameters

We bound the research process to ensure comparable execution across models and prevent unbounded tool use.
• Iteration Limits: We set Max Supervisor Iterations to 6
and Max Researcher Tool Calls to 5, following the default
Open Deep Research configuration. These limits control
research depth, latency, and API cost.
• Concurrency and Context: To model the parallel nature
of human research teams, we allow up to 3 concurrent
research units and limit raw web content to 50,000 characters, balancing parallel exploration and information retention against context overhead.

D.2

Evaluation Metrics Configuration

The metric configurations correspond to the interactionaware evaluation described in Section 3.4 of the main paper.
• Report Similarity: We use Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding0.6B to encode complete reports for cosine similarity. Its
32K-token context window accommodates all evaluated
reports.
• Multi-Granularity F1-Score: The same embedding
model computes semantic similarity between sentence, paragraph-, and chunk-level units. Reports are segmented into 300-token chunks with a 50-token overlap,
and matches are identified using a threshold of τ = 0.8.
• LLM Aspect Coverage Score (LLM-ACS): We extract
8–20 task aspects from each detailed source query. GPT4.1 at temperature 0 assigns 0–5 coverage scores to the
paired and generated reports, as defined in the main paper.

G

Semantic Leakage Classification

The main paper reports low lexical overlap between controlled responses and paired reports. To examine semantic
leakage beyond direct copying, we further classify all feedback responses using GPT-5.5 and the binary prompt in Figure 9. We define the two labels as follows:
• Preference Clarification: High-level guidance about
user preferences, constraints, scope, audience, format,
priorities, or evaluation criteria.
• Object-Level Leakage: Content that the final report
should directly assert, including specific conclusions,
rankings, recommendations, source content, or numerical findings.
Table 10 shows that 1,778 responses (97.91%) are classi-

fied as preference clarification and 38 (2.09%) as object-level
leakage. This model-based diagnostic indicates that most responses provide high-level intent guidance, although a small
number contain task-specific information. These classifications should be interpreted as diagnostic evidence rather than
human-validated leakage labels.

H

Feedback-Context Sensitivity

This section expands the context-sensitivity diagnostic in
Section 4.4 of the main paper. We compare feedback policies
given either the full private task context or a compressed version retaining only 10–20% of that context. The compressed
setting preserves high-level task signals while removing most
fine-grained details. As shown in Table 9, the two settings
produce comparable average alignment scores on the shared
30-task subset, suggesting limited aggregate sensitivity to the
amount of private context available to the feedback policy.
