Designing for Doubt: The Case for Informed Abstention in Autonomous Agents
Victor Ojewale, Suresh Venkatasubramanian
Center for Tech Responsibility, Brown University
{victor ojewale,suresh}@brown.edu

arXiv:2606.02965v2 [cs.AI] 3 Aug 2026

Abstract
As large language models gain tool access and are deployed
as autonomous agents capable of editing records, executing
transactions, and modifying infrastructure, we still evaluate
them based on the sole metric of task completion. We argue
that this evaluation focus constitutes a systematic design failure. Benchmark scoring, product metrics, and default deployment configurations all reward agents for proceeding even
when they lack the inputs, evidence, or authorization required
to do so safely. We call this phenomenon compliance bias.
This paper makes three contributions. First, we show how
compliance bias is embedded in the evaluation regimes
that currently shape agent development: prominent benchmarks either penalize agents for pausing or fail to measure
whether pausing was appropriate. Second, we introduce the
Informed Abstention Framework, which reconceptualizes
abstention not as a failure mode but as a structured capability: a precondition-aware pause that blocks the next tool
call, names what is missing, and routes to a concrete recovery action. Third, we specify what informed abstention
requires in deployment, arguing that runtime enforcement,
calibrated guard mechanisms, and auditable trace generation
should become standard properties of agentic system design
rather than optional additions. We perform a preliminary evaluation of our approach across 144 scenarios and seven model
families. Our results show that runtime enforcement achieves
87.5–91% hazardous-action blocking and 75–92% usability
on authorized scenarios, that compliance bias takes two structurally opposite forms across model families, and that the
safety–usability tradeoff is tunable rather than fixed.

Introduction

The deployment of tool-using large language models as autonomous agents marks a qualitative shift in what AI systems can do and what it means for them to fail (Yao et al.
2023; Park et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024). An agent that navigates a web browser, calls an enterprise API, or executes
infrastructure commands is not producing text for human
review. It is taking actions whose consequences extend into
the world and may be difficult or impossible to reverse (Anthropic 2026; Google 2026; OpenClaw contributors 2026).

This operational character of agent failure has not yet
been adequately reflected in how agents are built or evaluated. The dominant evaluation paradigm measures task success as the fraction of assigned tasks an agent completes, the
number of steps it takes to reach the goal, or the accuracy of
its final answer (Liu et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2024; Mialon
et al. 2023; Jimenez et al. 2024). Under this paradigm, an
agent that guesses a missing parameter and happens to complete the task scores well, while an agent that pauses to ask
whether the parameter is correct may score poorly, or may
not be counted at all. The implicit message is that agents that
proceed are good and agents that pause are defective.
Our Work. We use the term compliance bias to describe
the incentive for agents to proceed even when proceeding is
not justified. We first outline how compliance bias is embedded in current agent evaluation regimes. Next, we introduce
the idea of informed abstention, a precondition-aware agent
pause that blocks the next action, identifies what is missing,
and finds a concrete recovery action before continuing. We
illustrate how agent infrastructures need to be instrumented
to enable informed abstention, and support this with preliminary empirical evidence that our proposed framework can
actually achieve the goals of safe performance with high
utility.

The Agentic Failure Mode

In a conversational setting, an LLM failure is typically informational, the model gives incorrect advice, hallucinates
a fact, or misunderstands a question. These failures are visible and recoverable; the user reads the output, notices the
error, and either corrects the model or discards the response
without any external state changing. In an agentic setting,
failure is operational. The model produces actions whose effects persist beyond the conversation, so a failure that would
be a correctable misstatement in a chatbot becomes an unrecoverable state change when the agent has tool access. This
distinction is not merely one of severity but it is unique, and
it requires different design responses.

2.1

What Makes Agentic Failure Distinctive

Three properties of agentic action combine to make operational failures uniquely difficult to manage.
Irreversibility. Many actions an agent might take are difficult or impossible to reverse. A deleted record, a submitted
form, a sent message, or a committed financial transaction
may have downstream consequences that persist regardless
of what the agent does next (Ruan et al. 2024; Yuan et al.
2024). In multi-step workflows, an early irreversible action
can constrain or determine the space of available corrections, so the cost of a wrong action is not simply the action
itself but the entire downstream trajectory it sets in motion.
Compounding uncertainty. Agent workflows are usually
multi-step by design. At each step, the agent receives a new
observation and decides on a next action, and uncertainty
about the correct action at step k propagates forward. If the
agent makes an unverified assumption at step two, subsequent steps build on that assumption until the agent may be
operating on a foundation of accumulated inference with no
grounding in verified state (Sumers et al. 2024). A chatbot
can hedge, but an agent that has already taken three actions
cannot.
Invisibility of the decision to proceed. In human
decision-making, the choice to proceed despite uncertainty
is itself a decision that can be examined and challenged.
When an agent proceeds, it typically does so silently, leaving
no record of what it did not know, no log of the assumption
it made to fill a gap, and no moment at which a human could
have intervened (Ojewale, Suresh, and Venkatasubramanian
2026; Dong, Lu, and Zhu 2024) and as such invisible decisions are difficult to audit or correct.

2.2

What the Failure Literature Documents

The failure modes these properties produce are welldocumented across independent empirical programs. Ruan
et al. (2024) introduce ToolEmu, an LM-emulated sandbox
spanning 36 toolkits and 144 test cases, and find that even
the safest evaluated agent fails 23.9% of the time, with failures spanning financial loss, data deletion, and privacy exposure. Agents fabricate tool inputs when required information is absent, make unauthorized binding commitments,
and proceed past ambiguous instructions rather than seeking
clarification, even in settings where user intent is benign but
details are omitted. Their framework explicitly excludes adversarial intent, making their findings directly relevant to the
routine deployment failures that compliance bias produces.
Microsoft’s AI Red Team identified twenty-six distinct
failure modes across deployed agentic systems (Bryan et al.
2025). Several are directly traceable to the proceed-whenuncertain default, including the “misinterpretation of instructions” failure in which an agent deletes an entire
database table when asked to “get rid of” a specific record,
and the “excessive agency” failure in which an HR agent terminates an employee when asked only for advice on managing underperformance.
Xie et al. (2025) document that tool calls which are
individually safe can produce harmful outcomes when

chained, because no individual step checks whether
the preceding step succeeded before committing to the
next.Andriushchenko et al. (2025) benchmark susceptibility
to explicitly malicious agentic requests and find that many
leading models comply without any jailbreaking required.
Zhang et al. (2025) provides a structured benchmark across
8 safety risk categories and finds that none of 16 leading
LLM agents achieves a significant safety score, including in
non-adversarial conditions.
Taken together, this literature establishes that unsafe proceeding is both common and consequential. What it does
not provide is a unified account of why: none of these works
names the evaluation incentive structure that makes proceeding the correct local strategy for any agent optimized under current benchmarks, nor do they specify what a correct
pause should look like or what it should do.

3

The Origins and Entrenchment of
Compliance Bias

Compliance bias does not emerge by accident in current
agent design. Rather, it originates in training, is reinforced
by benchmark scoring, and is entrenched by the product
metrics that govern deployment decisions. In what follows,
we provide evidence for each of these claims.

3.1

Training Regimes Encourage Compliance

Before an agent encounters a benchmark, its disposition
toward compliance or caution has already been shaped
by training. Sharma et al. (2025) provide direct empirical evidence that RLHF-trained models exhibit sycophancy:
they tailor outputs to match the user’s apparent preferences even when this requires producing incorrect or unsupported claims. The mechanism is straightforward since
human raters tend to prefer confident, task-completing responses over hedged or uncertain ones, and the reward
model learns this preference.
This training-level disposition becomes a system-level
problem when the model is deployed as an agent with tool
access. A sycophantic chatbot produces agreeable text, but
a sycophantic agent produces agreeable actions, actions that
may be irreversible and whose agreeableness is no guarantee of correctness. The connection between sycophancy in
training and compliance bias in deployment is direct but underexamined which is the same reward signal that teaches a
model to agree with a user’s stated preference also teaches
it to proceed with a task even when the conditions for safe
proceeding are not met.
Alternative training approaches offer partial mitigation.
Constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022) introduces self-critique
as a training signal, and direct preference optimization
(Rafailov et al. 2024) simplifies the RLHF pipeline in ways
that may reduce some forms of reward hacking. Neither approach, however, explicitly addresses the multi-step, actionoriented structure of agent workflows and both optimize for
individual output quality rather than for the disposition to
pause when the next action is not yet justified. The traininglevel roots of compliance bias remain largely unaddressed.

3.2

Task-Completion Benchmarks Do Not Value
Abstention

Under task-completion scoring, an agent facing an ambiguous task with a missing required parameter must choose
between pausing to ask (costing turns and risking noncompletion) and guessing (potentially scoring credit). The
expected value of guessing is weakly better than asking under most current scoring regimes.
AgentBench (Liu et al. 2025) evaluates agents across
eight environments and uses outcome-based scoring
throughout. In the database environment specifically, success is measured by comparing the hash of the final table state against a gold standard; an agent that guesses a
missing record ID and happens to produce the correct final
state receives the same score as one that verified the correct
ID before acting, and the scoring mechanism cannot distinguish between them. WebArena (Zhou et al. 2024) evaluates agents by comparing the final state of the web environment against a programmatically checked expected outcome; an agent that spends additional turns on intermediate
verification rather than proceeding directly risks exceeding
the step limit and receiving no credit, regardless of whether
its caution was warranted. GAIA (Mialon et al. 2023) evaluates agents solely on whether their final answer matches the
ground truth via quasi exact match; intermediate reasoning
and verification steps are neither scored nor penalized, so the
only signal the benchmark produces is whether the agent got
the right answer in the end.
Kapoor et al. (2025) introduce HAL and observe that current web agent graders assign the same score of zero to an
agent that abstains and to one that leaks a user’s credit card,
collapsing outcomes whose real-world costs differ by orders of magnitude. Zhu et al. (2026) propose the Agentic
Benchmark Checklist (ABC) and show that a trivial emptyresponse agent scores 38% on τ -bench (exceeding GPT-4o),
because the grader accepts ground-truth matches regardless
of whether any reasoning occurred. Both works sharpen how
task completion is measured, but neither introduces abstention as a labeled ground-truth behavior nor provides metrics
for evaluating whether a given pause was warranted.

3.3

Product Metrics Entrench Compliance in
Deployment

Evaluation incentives do not exist in isolation; they shape
product metrics, which in turn shape deployment decisions.
The most common production metrics for agent systems,
including task success rate, task completion rate, and turn
efficiency, operationalize exactly the same incentive as the
benchmarks. An agent that asks more questions will appear
to have lower task success rate and worse turn efficiency,
regardless of whether the questions were warranted.
Rabanser et al. (2026) evaluate fourteen models over
eighteen months and find that compliance violations, cases
where agents take actions without required authorization or
past defined operational boundaries, are among the most persistent reliability gaps even as raw accuracy improves substantially. Their mapping of three high-profile deployment
failures to reliability dimensions is instructive and in each

case examined the agent was judged capable by internal assessments before deployment, and the failure arose from
proceeding past an unmet precondition that no evaluation
metric was designed to catch. Capability gains do not translate automatically into reliability gains.

4

Related Work

Abstention as competence. The selective prediction literature establishes that abstention is a legitimate model behavior rather than a defect, framed as a predictor that declines on
low-confidence inputs can achieve higher accuracy at lower
coverage, and this tradeoff is principled rather than arbitrary
(Geifman and El-Yaniv 2017, 2019; Kadavath et al. 2022).
This framing is the right starting point, but it was developed
for single-step classification and generation tasks and does
not transfer directly to agentic settings, where the decision
to abstain at step k must account for accumulated uncertainty from prior steps, the irreversibility of actions already
taken, and the cascading consequences of being wrong. Wen
et al. (2025) survey the broader literature and find abstention unsolved across methods, domains, and evaluation settings. No existing abstention framework addresses the multistep, action-oriented structure of agentic deployment, and
that gap is part of what motivates the present argument..
Enforcement-layer mechanisms. Costa et al. (2025) introduce FIDES, which assigns integrity labels to block execution when untrusted data flows into privileged tool calls.
Summers, Mohammed, and Wu (2025)take a related approach through data-flow controls, observing that overly
conservative policies risk introducing friction users did not
intend. Adam et al. (2026) propose a hybrid approach that
combines offline static analysis of tool effects with finegrained runtime sandboxes, enabling policies that adapt dynamically to user intent rather than relying on static global
restrictions. All three build principled enforcement mechanisms, but none addresses benchmark design or provides
composite metrics for characterizing the safety–usability
tradeoff.
Designed friction and human oversight. HCI research
provides a complementary foundation. Bainbridge’s ironies
of automation(Bainbridge 1983) show that removing humans from the loop degrades the skills and attention needed
to catch errors when automation fails. Cox(Cox et al. 2016)
formalizes this insight as microboundaries: small, deliberate interruptions in automated workflows that preserve human oversight without requiring full manual control. Natali
et al.(Natali et al. 2025) demonstrate that designed friction,
placed at moments of genuine uncertainty, produces better alignment between automated behavior and human intent than either full automation or full manual control. We
draw on this tradition directly: informed abstention is a microboundary that fires at the specific moments where proceeding without grounding would be dangerous, not a general slowdown.
Over-Refusal as the Recognized Failure Mode A growing body of work studies over-refusal as a genuine failure

mode in its own right. OR-Bench (Cui et al. 2025) constructs a large-scale benchmark of safe prompts that safetyaligned models are likely to refuse, documenting the tradeoff between toxic content rejection and unnecessary refusal
of innocuous requests. XSTest (Röttger et al. 2024) identifies what it calls exaggerated safety behaviours, cases where
models refuse clearly safe prompts because they share surface features with unsafe ones. Both papers are explicit that
the problem they are studying sits inside a genuine tension
between helpfulness and harmlessness, and OR-Bench notes
directly in its future work that refusal is a false binary and
that more nuanced definitions are needed. We are not contesting any of this as over-refusal of legitimate requests is
a real failure mode and measuring it systematically is valuable work. The problem is that the evaluation landscape has
developed tools for one direction of error and not the other.
A benchmark that measures how often a model refuses safe
requests cannot tell us how often a model proceeds with requests it should have refused, and without a symmetric measure of under-refusal, there is no way to assess whether refusal was appropriate in a given case. The field can currently
tell us when agents pause too much but it has no systematic
way to tell us when they proceed with insufficient grounding.

5

The Informed Abstention Framework

We are now ready to introduce the framework we study
in this paper. Firstly, we define informed abstention as a
structured pause inside an agent workflow that occurs when
the next external action is not yet justified. The pause has
three properties that distinguish it from a generic abstention
or a silent failure.
It is precondition-aware: the pause is triggered by a specific identifiable condition such as a missing required input,
an unverifiable state, or an absent authorization. The triggering condition is not inferred from the model’s confidence in
the abstract but derived from the requirements of the specific
operation the agent is about to execute.
It is functional: the pause blocks the next tool call but
does not terminate the task. Instead it routes the interaction
to one of three concrete recovery actions, covering clarification, bounded verification, or handoff, depending on which
precondition has failed and what recovery is possible.
It is traceable: the pause generates a structured record
explaining what condition was unmet, what recovery action
was taken, and what the outcome was. This record is auditable by deployment teams and, where relevant, by human
overseers.
This makes informed abstention part of competent delegation rather than a failure of task completion. The agent is
not refusing the task; it is refusing to take the next step until
the conditions for safe execution of that step are met.

Specification gap. A specification gap occurs when the
agent is about to invoke an operation without the minimum
information needed for a well formed and safe call. The key
point is that what counts as “missing” is defined by the operation itself through its schema, required parameters, and
documented constraints, not only by the model’s interpretation of the user request.
For example, a payroll adjustment tool might require an
employee identifier, a numeric amount, and a justification
reason. If the user says “give Sarah a bonus” but does not
specify which Sarah, how much, or why, then the agent does
not yet have a valid call to make. Proceeding forces the agent
to choose values that the user has not provided and guessing
or filling defaults turns missing information into an implicit
decision on the user’s behalf.
Good informed abstention in this case is targeted clarification. The agent names the specific fields that are missing or ambiguous, asks only for what is needed to form the
next valid step, and restates the action it will take once those
fields are provided. This is more useful than a generic “I
need more information” because it tells the user exactly what
to supply and why it matters.
Verification gap. A verification gap occurs when the
agent cannot confirm something it needs to know before taking the next step. This can happen in two ways. First, the
agent is about to act based on a guess about the current state,
but it cannot check that the state is actually true. Second, the
agent has already taken an action, but it cannot tell whether
the action worked before moving on.
Both cases show up often in currently deployed agents. In
browser agents, the page view the agent relies on can be out
of date, key UI signals may not load, or the system might not
show a clear success message even if the action failed (He
et al. 2024; Koh et al. 2024). In API based agents, the tool
may return an unclear response, the success signal may be
missing, or the change may take time to appear, so the agent
cannot reliably tell success from failure.
Good informed abstention here looks like a simple pause

with a concrete check. The agent says what it could not confirm, runs one limited verification step, and then decides
what to do next. If the check resolves the issue, the agent
continues; if it does not, the agent hands off instead of guessing and pushing forward. The key is that the verification step
is bounded: the agent tries one clear check and then either
proceeds or escalates.
Authority gap. An authority gap occurs when the next
step would create a real commitment or make a high impact change, but the user has not clearly approved that exact
step. What counts as high impact should be decided by policy and tied to specific tools or action routes, not guessed by
the model from the wording of the request.
This matters because a model can misjudge what is sensitive, and it can also be pushed into treating a risky action
as routine. It also matters because the line for what requires
approval is often a product, legal, or operational decision.
Those rules belong in the deployment configuration, so they
are explicit and consistent.
Good informed abstention here looks like a commitment
pause. The agent gets everything ready, states exactly what
it is about to do and what it will change, and then asks for
explicit approval before executing. This is different from a
generic “are you sure” prompt; the approval request should
name the specific action and the key details, so the user is
authorizing something concrete rather than clicking through
a vague warning1 .

5.3

Abstention as Structured Routing, Not an
Endpoint

A critical property of the framework is that informed abstention is not a terminal state. When the agent pauses, it does
not simply stop but routes to one of three defined recovery
actions.
Clarification. The agent asks for the specific missing input or clarification needed to meet the unmet precondition.
The request is targeted as it asks for exactly what is missing and nothing more, and it restates the action that will be
taken once the missing information is provided. This routing
applies primarily to specification gaps.
Bounded verification. The agent takes a defined, limited
verification step such as re-querying a state, requesting an
explicit confirmation signal, or checking a specific condition, and then either resolves the uncertainty or escalates.
The verification is bounded in that it does not expand the
agent’s action space or commit additional resources. This
routing applies primarily to verification gaps.
Handoff. When neither clarification nor bounded verification can resolve the blocking condition, the agent hands off
to a human operator. The handoff is structured by providing
the full context of what was attempted, what condition could
not be met, and what the human needs to decide. It does not
simply report failure but provides what a human needs to
take over the decision. This routing applies to authority gaps
and to verification gaps that cannot be resolved by bounded
recovery.
The routing structure is what distinguishes informed abstention from two failure modes it might be confused with.
It is not a hallucinated completion, which means proceeding without the precondition, and it is not an uninformative
failure, which means stopping without explanation. It is a
competent stop: a pause that is traceable to a specific unmet
condition, accompanied by a structured account of what is
missing and what the next step should be.

What Informed Abstention Requires in
Deployment

The framework described in Section 5 is a conceptual specification and this section addresses what is required to instantiate it in real agent deployments. We argue that three properties are necessary: runtime enforcement, calibrated guard
mechanisms, and trace generation.

6.1

Runtime enforcement, not prompt-only
guidance

The most natural first approach to implementing informed
abstention is to encode it in the agent’s system prompt by
instructing the agent to pause before high-stakes actions, to
ask when required fields are missing, and to seek authorization before binding commitments. This approach has real
value as a first layer of guidance. For many routine interactions in low-stakes settings, prompt-level instruction is sufficient.
However, prompt-level guidance has structural limitations
that make it inadequate as the sole mechanism for informed
abstention in high-integrity deployments. The most significant limitation is that the agent’s context window can be
manipulated. Adversarial inputs that override the system
prompt, such as roleplay jailbreaks, authority claims, and indirect injection through tool results (Andriushchenko et al.
2025; Greshake et al. 2023), have been shown to cause
agents to ignore prompt-level safety instructions. An agent
instructed to always seek confirmation before deleting data
can be induced to delete data without confirmation if an adversarial input convinces it that it is operating in a context
where the normal rules do not apply.
Runtime enforcement addresses this limitation by moving the enforcement mechanism outside the agent’s context
window. Most tool-using agent frameworks expose hooks
around tool invocation, specifically pre-execution and postexecution callbacks that sit between the model’s action selection and the actual tool call (Wu et al. 2023; LangChain
2025; Yao et al. 2023). These hooks are the appropriate place
to enforce informed abstention for three reasons. First, they
cannot be bypassed by manipulating the agent’s context because they execute in the agent runtime rather than in the
model. Second, they are unconditional: a pre-execution hook
that checks for required fields will check for required fields
on every invocation, regardless of what the model believes
about the current context. Third, they are configurable by
policy: the same hook infrastructure can enforce stricter conditions in high-risk workflows and lighter conditions in routine ones, without changes to the model.
A practical implementation is a tool wrapper that intercepts tool invocation, applies the precondition checks in sequence, and either permits execution or routes to the appropriate recovery action. Each tool is annotated with a checkpoint policy specifying which fields are required, whether
commitment confirmation is needed, and which state keys
should be verified. This configuration is a product and policy artifact, not a model artifact.

6.2

Calibrated guard mechanisms

The authority gap check introduces a specific challenge: the
agent must determine whether the user has provided explicit,
action-specific authorization. This is a semantic judgment
rather than a structural one, and it cannot be resolved simply
by checking for the presence of a field or the existence of a
signal.
Two approaches to this judgment are worth noting. The
first is keyword matching: the system checks whether the
user’s message contains explicit affirmative words such as
“yes,” “confirm,” or “proceed” before permitting a binding action. This approach is simple to implement but brittle. Users who provide clear authorization in natural language, for example by saying “go ahead and delete the staging database as we discussed,” may not trigger the keyword
match, producing unnecessary friction for legitimate authorizations. Adversarial users who learn the keywords can trigger false positives.
The second approach is semantic intent classification
where a guard model evaluates the user’s most recent message and classifies whether it constitutes explicit confirmation of the specific action about to be taken. This approach
is more accurate and more robust, but introduces a design
choice about where the guard model runs and what context
it has access to.
We argue that the guard model should be architecturally
separated from the planning agent. It should be a separate
model instance that evaluates only the raw user message
against a narrow binary question, without access to the planning agent’s full context window. This separation is motivated by the same principle that motivates runtime enforcement: if the guard runs inside the planning agent’s context, it
can be manipulated by the same adversarial inputs that compromise the planning agent. An external guard with a narrow
context is substantially harder to fool because the adversarial
payload that would need to be in scope is simply not present.
The semantic sensitivity of the guard is a tunable parameter. A guard calibrated to require highly explicit and specific confirmation will produce fewer false negatives, meaning fewer unauthorized actions slipping through, but more
false positives, meaning more legitimate authorizations that
are rejected. A guard calibrated more permissively will have
the opposite profile. Neither calibration is universally correct: the right operating point depends on the risk profile of
the deployment, the cost of friction to users, and the regulatory or legal environment in which the agent operates.
The framework provides the mechanism; deployment teams
should configure the operating point based on domain requirements.

6.3

Trace analysis as a priority requirement

Informed abstention is only partially useful if it happens invisibly and for it to function as a governance mechanism and
not just a safety one, it must generate structured, auditable
records of every pause event.
A trace record for an informed abstention event should
capture at minimum the timestamp and tool that was
blocked, the specific precondition that was unmet, the user

message at the time of the block, the recovery action taken,
and the outcome of that recovery action such that this record
serves several functions. For operators, it enables monitoring of abstention rates by condition type, identification of
tools or workflows with high rates of precondition failure,
and detection of adversarial manipulation attempts (Dong,
Lu, and Zhu 2024; Ojewale, Suresh, and Venkatasubramanian 2026). For users, it provides an explanation of why the
agent paused and what is needed to proceed and for auditors
and regulators, it provides evidence that safety mechanisms
fired, that commitment boundaries were respected, and that
a human was present in the loop at the moments that mattered.
The trace requirement also addresses a subtle but important problem which is the difficulty of distinguishing a competent stop from a failure. When an agent pauses, the external observation is the same whether the pause was an
informed abstention or a silent error and without a structured trace, there is no way to distinguish between an agent
that correctly identified an authority gap and an agent that
crashed. Trace generation is what makes abstention competence inspectable and therefore improvable.

7

Preliminary Evidence

To demonstrate that the Informed Abstention Framework is instantiable and that the tradeoffs surrounding semantic reliability are empirically observable,
we implemented the three runtime checkpoints as an
InformedAbstentionToolWrapper and conducted
a preliminary evaluation across 144 simulated execution
scenarios in two high-stakes deployment domains, HR
operations (employee record management and bonus processing) and DevOps infrastructure (database management
and asynchronous server operations).

7.1

Experimental Setup

Conditions and models. Three conditions were tested:
Baseline (no abstention policy), Prompt-Only (systemprompt policy with no runtime enforcement), and Checkpoint (runtime enforcement layer).Full system prompt text
for all three conditions is provided in Appendix A.2. Seven
model families were evaluated: GPT-4o, GPT-5.4-mini,
Llama 3.1 8B, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Gemini 2.5 Flash, all deployed as ReActstyle tool-calling agents (Yao et al. 2023) in LangChain, using each model’s native function-calling interface for tool
invocation.
Checkpoint
architecture. The
InformedAbstentionToolWrapper is a preexecution hook at the LangChain tool-invocation boundary.
Three checks fire sequentially: the Constraint Checkpoint verifies all schema-required fields are present; the
Grounding Checkpoint polls state with up to five retries
at 0.5 s intervals; and the Commitment Checkpoint uses
a separate GPT-4o guard model that receives the full
conversation history with no access to the planning agent’s
reasoning traces or system prompt, to classify whether
explicit authorization has been given at any point in the

exchange. Isolating the guard from the planning agent’s
context prevents adversarial inputs from subverting both
simultaneously(Andriushchenko et al. 2025). Example
execution traces for each checkpoint layer are provided
in Appendix A.3; outcome classification logic and judge
prompts are described in Appendix A.4.
Dataset. We constructed 144 scenarios from 24 humanauthored seeds directly adapted from four published benchmarks (Lu et al. 2025; Andriushchenko et al. 2025; Zhang
et al. 2025; Xie et al. 2025), augmented via GPT-4o-mini
at temperature 1.0 with gap type and entity identifier constraints preserved. The dataset splits into 120 hazardous scenarios (36 specification, 54 authority, 24 verification) and
24 safe controls with explicit authorization. Each hazardous
scenario specifies the gap type, the missing precondition,
and the tool call that would be invoked if the agent proceeds;
each safe control is a matched variant in which the missing
precondition is explicitly provided. All 144 scenarios were
manually reviewed for gap-type accuracy; ambiguous pairs
were replaced. The full scenario schema, representative examples of each gap type, and augmentation constraints are
documented in Appendix A.1; the mock execution environment and tool definitions are described in Appendix A.5.

7.2

Results

Compliance bias is universal but takes two opposite
forms. Every model family exhibits compliance bias
at Baseline, but the failure modes split into two poles.
Llama 3.1 8B has the lowest Baseline SR (50.8%) paired
with the highest UR (91.7%): a proceed-by-default disposition that executes readily but blocks hazardous actions
poorly. The Claude family inverts this as Opus 4.6 achieves
61.7% SR but only 29.2% UR at Baseline, blocking authorized actions roughly seven times out of ten even before any
safety instruction is applied. GPT-4o, GPT-5.4-mini, and the
Gemini models occupy the middle ground, with SR between
53–80% and UR between 46–79%. This distributional split
confirms that compliance bias is not a single behavioral pattern, proceed-always and refuse-always are both miscalibrations relative to the ground truth of precondition-aware action.
The prompt-only usability cliff is severe but modeldependent. Prompt-Only raises SR for all seven models
but its effect on usability diverges sharply by model family. GPT-4o drops from 79.2% to 4.2% UR (−75pp); Claude
Sonnet 4.6 falls from 25.0% to 4.2% and Opus 4.6 to 8.3%,
declining to execute nearly every safe scenario despite explicit user authorization. These models read the safety instruction as an absolute override of user confirmation, creating a re-confirmation loop that no prior consent can satisfy.
In contrast, GPT-5.4-mini and both Gemini models show no
usability cliff: Prompt-Only improves UR alongside SR for
all three, suggesting that well-calibrated instruction following can internalize a safety policy without over-applying it.
Prompt-Only therefore cannot be solely relied upon as a deployment strategy: its effect on usability is unpredictable
across model families and unverifiable without empirical
measurement.

Runtime enforcement converges on a consistent ceiling and resolves both failure modes. The Checkpoint
achieves the highest SR for all seven models, with gains
over Baseline ranging from +10.8pp (Gemini 2.5 Pro)
to +36.7pp (Llama 3.1 8B). All seven models converge
to Checkpoint SR between 87.5–91%, suggesting a structural ceiling imposed by scenarios in which the agent refuses natively without invoking a wrapped tool, bypassing
the enforcement layer entirely. Usability recovers dramatically: the UR gain from Prompt-Only to Checkpoint is
+87.5pp for GPT-4o and +70.9pp for Claude Opus 4.6;
for the Claude family the Checkpoint is the only condition
that produces both acceptable SR and any meaningful UR.
Critically, Checkpoint IRR reaches 100% under all seven
model families by architectural design, a model-independent
guarantee that Baseline and Prompt-Only cannot approach
(Llama Baseline IRR: 49.2%). The larger SR and IRR gains
for weaker models confirm that runtime enforcement provides greater marginal benefit precisely where native alignment is most deficient.
The tradeoff is a tunable parameter, not a fixed cost.
The Checkpoint condition achieves higher safety than
Prompt-Only across all seven model families while also
achieving dramatically higher usability, demonstrating that
runtime enforcement with a calibrated guard produces a
more favorable operating point than blanket prompt-level instruction. The security-friction tradeoff is governed by the
semantic sensitivity of the guard mechanism rather than being an inherent property of abstention-capable systems, and
deployment teams can configure this operating point based

on risk tolerance and domain requirements.

8

Implications for Benchmark Design and
Deployment Practice

The results carry implications at three levels that include
what evaluation teams can change immediately within existing infrastructure, what the benchmark community needs
to coordinate on over a longer horizon, and what the field
needs to accept as a normative commitment before either of
the first two becomes possible.

8.1

Reporting the Triple Within Existing
Infrastructure

The most immediately actionable change requires no new
benchmark infrastructure. Any evaluation team running
agents today can compute SR, UR, and IRR against existing scenario sets by adding a matched safe control for each
hazardous scenario, supplying the missing precondition explicitly, and running the same scoring logic in both directions. The Baseline results in Table 2 demonstrate the cost
of omitting this step: a benchmark scoring only task completion would report GPT-4o as performing well on all scenarios it completed, without observing that 46.7% of hazardous
scenarios proceeded without the required inputs or authorization. Reporting SR and UR as a joint pair, rather than
either metric alone, is sufficient to make both failure modes
visible simultaneously, and IRR adds a third dimension that
distinguishes a well-calibrated safety mechanism from one
that produces uninformative blocks without identifying what
went wrong.

The Checkpoint architecture is similarly deployable today
in any framework that exposes pre-execution hooks at the
tool-invocation boundary, and the implementation requires
annotating each tool with a checkpoint policy specifying
required fields, whether commitment confirmation applies,
and which state keys should be verified before execution.
For bounded tool sets such as a DevOps agent with a defined set of infrastructure endpoints or an HR agent operating over a fixed set of payroll operations, this annotation is a
manageable engineering task that a small team can complete
in a focused sprint. The constraint on this approach is scope
rather than feasibility as deployments with hundreds of heterogeneous API endpoints face a policy annotation burden
that itself becomes a governance problem, requiring domain
experts and policy authority that sits above the engineering
layer and cannot be resolved by technical design alone.

8.2

Building Abstention-Labeled Scenarios
Across the Community

Making abstention measurable at benchmark scale requires
changes that no single research group can deliver unilaterally, because the core requirement is a shared annotation convention whose ground-truth expected behavior is
a structured pause rather than a completed task. Creating
such scenarios requires consensus on guidelines that can
be applied consistently across research groups with different taxonomies, and that consensus does not currently exist. The three-gap taxonomy in Section 5.1 is an attempt to
provide a shared vocabulary by defining specification gaps,
verification gaps, and authority gaps as distinct, labelable
classes of scenario, with the matched-pair design ensuring
that the same underlying action can be tested under both
hazardous and authorized conditions within a single evaluation. We offer these as a starting point rather than a final standard, and adoption requires that benchmark developers treat a correct pause as a ground-truth behavior that
receives credit rather than a non-completion that receives
zero, a change that touches scoring infrastructure, dataset
construction pipelines, and the implicit definition of what a
capable agent looks like.
The structural ceiling on Checkpoint SR, which sits between 87.5 and 91% across all seven model families, illustrates one boundary of what enforcement-layer mechanisms
can achieve. Scenarios in which the agent refuses natively
without invoking a wrapped tool bypass the enforcement
layer entirely, and no tool-level checkpoint can catch them.
Closing this gap requires either complementary mechanisms
that operate at the reasoning level rather than the invocation
boundary, or benchmark designs that specifically target and
label these bypass failure modes so that the community can
develop and evaluate solutions directly.

8.3

Treating Unauthorized Action as a Measured
Failure

The practical asymmetry underlying both of the above recommendations is that the cost of friction is immediate and
visible while the cost of unauthorized action is deferred, indirect, and typically misattributed to edge-case behavior or

hallucination rather than to the structural incentive that made
proceeding the locally optimal strategy for the agent. This
asymmetry is not a measurement failure that better instrumentation alone can correct; it is a structural feature of how
agentic failures propagate, because a deleted record, a submitted regulatory form, or a committed transaction produces
consequences that unfold over time, involve multiple parties,
and resist clean attribution to the agent action that initiated
them.
The implication is that deployment teams operating under
standard product metrics will systematically underweight
the harm side of the tradeoff, not because they are indifferent to harm but because the harm is not yet in scope
of what gets measured. Changing this requires an organizational and normative commitment to treating authorization failures, constraint-elicitation failures, and verification
failures as first-class events worth tracking alongside friction metrics, even before those events produce observable
downstream consequences. Without that commitment, deployment teams will encounter friction immediately and encounter harm only in retrospect, and the incentive to reduce
friction will consistently dominate the incentive to prevent
unauthorized action. The framework provides the mechanism for structured enforcement and traceable pauses, but
the decision to treat proceeding without grounding as a category of failure worth measuring is a prior commitment that
organizations must make before the technical recommendations can take hold.

9

Limitations and Future Work

These results are preliminary by scope and by framing. The
claims made in this paper are bounded in ways that matter
both technically and for the broader argument about evaluation practice and deployment governance, and each limitation points toward a corresponding research direction.
Dataset scope and domain coverage. The 144-scenario
dataset covers HR operations and DevOps infrastructure.
Evaluation across additional domains is required before the
results generalize.Legal document handling, financial transaction processing, and healthcare record management each
present authority and verification structures that differ meaningfully from the domains tested: legal workflows involve
multi-party authorization chains where the relevant principal may not be the immediate user; financial transactions
are subject to jurisdictionally variable compliance obligations that alter what counts as sufficient authorization; and
healthcare settings involve both regulatory mandates on data
handling and asymmetric expertise between the agent and
the authorizing clinician. Whether the checkpoint architecture generalizes to these settings without domain-specific
policy annotation is an open question the current evaluation cannot answer. Extending the evaluation to these domains, in collaboration with practitioners who can supply
authentic task distributions and authorization norms, is the
most direct path toward claims that bear on high-stakes deployment in practice. The gap distribution is also a limitation independent of domain coverage. Authority gaps account for 45% of hazardous scenarios because they are over-

represented in the source benchmarks from which seeds
were drawn, not because they are the most frequent failure
mode in deployed agentic systems. Specification and verification gaps are likely more common in routine deployment,
where missing parameters and unconfirmable state are everyday occurrences. Future work can rebalance the distribution against empirical base rates drawn from production
agentic logs, which would make safety rate estimates more
representative of real deployment conditions and allow gaptype-specific analysis of where enforcement provides the
greatest marginal benefit.
Normative assumptions in the taxonomy. The three-gap
taxonomy encodes a particular set of normative choices. It
privileges explicit, documentable authorization over implicit
or informal authorization, which may not reflect how authorization actually works in some organizational settings,
where approval is distributed, negotiated, and often retrospective rather than prospective. An agent operating inside an organization where verbal authorization is the norm,
where authorization authority is contested between teams,
or where the relevant stakeholders are external to the immediate conversation will face conditions the current framework does not model. Future work should examine how
the taxonomy performs when authorization norms are informal or contested, and should engage governance and legal
scholars in specifying what the standard for valid authorization should look like across deployment contexts rather than
leaving that determination to individual engineering teams.
Structural ceiling and the limits of enforcement-layer
mechanisms. The Checkpoint SR ceiling of 87.5–91%
across model families reflects scenarios in which the agent
refuses natively without invoking a wrapped tool, bypassing
the enforcement layer entirely. From a safety engineering
perspective, this ceiling is an open problem: the 9–12.5% of
hazardous scenarios that escape enforcement do so because
the agent’s own reasoning produces a stopping condition before tool invocation, and the safety guarantee is therefore
conditional on the agent attempting to use a tool in the first
place. This ceiling also has a governance dimension. The paper argues that abstention should be auditable and traceable,
but native refusals that bypass the enforcement layer leave
no structured record of what was blocked or why, meaning
an agent that refuses silently at the reasoning level satisfies
the safety rate criterion but fails the traceability requirement
that informed abstention demands. Closing this gap requires
mechanisms that operate also at the reasoning level rather
than only at the tool-invocation boundary, and their development has implications for both technical safety and for the
accountability frameworks that regulators and auditors will
need if agentic systems are to be inspected in practice.

10

Conclusion

We have argued that compliance bias, the structural incentive for agents to proceed even when proceeding is not
justified, is embedded in current evaluation practice and
produces incomplete deployment defaults. The argument
is not that current agents are carelessly designed, but that
the incentive structure shaping their design, running from

RLHF training through benchmark scoring through product
metrics, systematically rewards proceeding over pausing.
Agents that always proceed are not reliably helpful. They
are reliably compliant, and compliance without grounding
is a failure mode rather than a feature.
The Informed Abstention Framework offers a conceptual and practical alternative. Informed abstention is
precondition-aware, functional, and traceable. It is triggered
by a specification gap when required inputs are absent, a verification gap when world state cannot be confirmed, or an authority gap when explicit authorization has not been given.
It routes to a concrete recovery action rather than terminating, and it generates a structured record that makes the pause
auditable and therefore improvable. Our preliminary evaluation across seven model families shows the framework is
instantiable, achieves substantially better safety rates than
unconstrained baselines across all model families, and that
the safety–friction tradeoff is configurable rather than fixed
and varies substantially across model families.
The broader implication is that the field needs to change
what it measures. Benchmarks that score only task completion cannot distinguish an agent that correctly paused from
one that silently failed, and deployments that track only task
success rate have no way to observe the harm caused by proceeding without grounding. Abstention competence is a necessary complement to task competence for any agent operating in consequential settings, and building agents that are
trustworthy requires changing the incentives that shape their
design and that change begins with what we choose to evaluate.

Augmentation constraints. Seeds were augmented to five
paraphrased variations each via GPT-4o-mini at temperature 1.0 under two hard constraints. First, polarity was preserved: hazardous variations were prohibited from including authorization language, and safe-control variations were
required to include explicit approval language. Second, entity identifiers were constrained to match the mock environment (employee IDs E101/E102/E103; server IDs srv-prod01/srv-prod-02/srv-stg-01), preventing tool errors caused by
non-existent identifiers from masking the abstention signal.
