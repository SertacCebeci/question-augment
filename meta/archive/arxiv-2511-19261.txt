To address this question, we first revisit how VLMs perform reasoning for complex problems. Most VLMs achieve
strong reasoning ability with language-centric reasoning
with chain-of-thought (CoT) [84]. CoT generates more text

this paradigm fails in spatial and temporal understanding

inject visual information, including 3D spatial and temporal information into the thinking process, i.e. to make VLMs
“think in space and time”.
Our key idea is to explicitly incorporate visual tokens into the reasoning process, forming visual chains of
thought. However, most current VLMs take textual and visual inputs but can only generate text tokens, which prevents them from naturally producing visual reasoning steps.
To solve this, we use external tools to generate visual tokens, such as object tracking [60] and grounding [44], to
build visual tokens as demonstrated in Fig. 1 and Fig. 4.

Humans can perceive and understand 3D space and long
videos from sequential visual observations. But do visionlanguage models (VLMs) can? Recent work demonstrates
that even state-of-the-art VLMs still struggle to understand
3D space and long videos, although they are powerful
in typical vision-language tasks. Current methods often
rely on specialized architectural designs to improve performance for 3D tasks and video understanding tasks separately. In contrast, we propose LAST, short for LeArn
to Think in Space and Time, to jointly improve 3D spatial
and long video understanding for general VLMs with only
a set of 2D images as inputs. LAST makes VLMs think
in space and time rather than only with text before giving
the final answer, building visual thinking trajectories in 3D
space and temporal dimension. We demonstrate the effectiveness of LAST in two scenarios: 1) zero-shot, where we
directly prompt proprietary models; and 2) fine-tuning general VLMs with data that include thinking trajectories in
3D space and time. We show that LAST brings substantial gains in various benchmarks, including 3 spatial understanding, 4 video understanding, and 3 image understanding tasks. Notably, 15.8% gains on EgoSchema with GPT4o in a zero-shot manner and 8.3% gains on VSI-Bench
compared with Qwen2.5-VL-7B.

shown remarkable capacities on typical visual-language

on visual-language tasks, many recent works [49, 91] show
that even state-of-the-art VLMs still struggle with 3D spatial [40, 91] and long video understanding tasks [79].
Prior works focus on separately enhancing spatial and
temporal understanding ability. To improve the 3D spatial
understanding of VLMs, many attempts have been made,
including two main directions: 1) using 3D data as inputs

We extensively evaluate LAST on three spatial reasoning and four video understanding benchmarks. In the
zero-shot setting, where LAST is applied to GPT-4o [33],
our method consistently improves performance across all
seven benchmarks. For instance, on spatial reasoning tasks,
LAST yields 9.6% accuracy gain on SQA3D [47] without relying on any 3D inputs. On video understanding
tasks, it achieves 15.8% improvement on EgoSchema [50]
(69.6→85.4) with only eight input frames. For the finetuned model LAST -7B, performance improvements remain consistent across all benchmarks. Specifically, LAST
-7B attains 41.3% accuracy on VSI-Bench [91], outperforming Qwen2.5-VL-7B [4] by 8.3%. On NExT-QA [89],
it achieves 86.2% on the validation set and 78.0% on the
challenging ATP-hard subset. These results collectively
demonstrate that introducing visual chains of thought substantially enhances both spatial and temporal understand-

ing, validating the effectiveness and generality of our proposed approach LAST.
To summarize, we make the following contributions: (1)
We propose LAST, a unified approach for general VLMs to
improve spatial and temporal understanding ability without
specific architecture design and extra inputs. (2) We build
large-scale data with visual thinking trajectories and deploy LAST-7B that significantly improves the base model.
(3) LAST and LAST-7B are validated through extensive
evaluations on spatial and video understanding benchmarks,
demonstrating superior effectiveness.

2. Related Work
Spatial understanding. Based on powerful LLMs [8, 71,
72], VLMs exhibit visual understanding ability for 2D images [1, 4, 18, 33, 35, 39, 41, 67]. However, 3D spatial understanding, relevant to robotics [23, 61, 109] and
autonomous driving [69], presents significant challenges.
Some efforts [9, 12, 15, 16, 22, 29, 86, 100, 101] improve
2

3D spatial understanding by using additional 3D inputs,
such as point clouds, depth images, and camera features
or specific modules and designs (like VGGT encoder [75]).
In contrast, our approach only requires videos as input and
uses general VLMs without any task-specific designs to explore general visual spatial intelligence.
Video understanding. While VLMs demonstrate impressive performance on standard video understanding tasks [4,
35, 37, 62, 68, 110], understanding high-frame-rate and
long-duration videos is challenging due to high computational and memory demands. Furthermore, VLMs fail to
capture useful information in long-dependency tasks [28,
79, 87]. Previous works explore methods of video compression to tackle this, including adopting Q-Former [36,
83] for video feature extraction and fusing neighboring
frames [76]. Different from the above, we explore making
VLMs “think” in space and time for videos, which is applicable for general VLMs without any specific model design.
Thinking beyond text. LLMs/VLMs achieve strong
reasoning ability with language-centric reasoning, i.e.
CoT [80, 84]. By decomposing complex problems into a
sequence of textual reasoning steps, CoT has significantly
enhanced VLMs in visual reasoning questions [14, 77, 98].
While simple and effective, text-only CoT provides limited
exploration of intelligence and ignores the rich and continuous visual world. Furthermore, many tasks require visual engagement, such as solving complex geometry problems [30], visual search [88], and spatial reasoning [30, 91].
To tackle this, “thinking with images” is proposed to inject
visual information into the thinking process [53, 63]. Since
most VLMs cannot generate visual tokens, previous work
mainly uses external tools [5, 27, 31, 51, 57, 85], such as
code, visual expert models, zoom-in-out, and so on. Previous work mainly focuses on solving tasks with 2D images,
lacking exploration in spatial and temporal understanding.
In this work, we shift the research line from 2D images to
3D space and videos.

The choice of G is flexible, and we only consider
training-free methods for frame selection for simplicity and
efficiency. A straightforward solution is to select frames
via uniform sampling, i.e., we uniformly sample K frames
from a sequence of visual observations I0 . However, uniform sampling ignores the relation between the text query Q
and the input frames, which may sample frames that are irrelevant to the query or ignore frames that contain information relevant to the query. Therefore, some query-aware image selection methods are proposed to select query-related
frames [66, 96]. However, only selecting related frames
may introduce visually redundant frames, which means
some highly similar frames are chosen. Furthermore, we
propose to simultaneously maximize the relevance between
the text query and the selected images and minimize the visual redundancy for frame selection.

Object tracking. Understanding object correspondence relationships (for 3D space) and object movement (for videos)
during multiple images is important. However, current
VLMs do not support object tracking for multiple visual observations. Here we use SAM2 [60] (SAM2.1-Hiera-Large)
as the tracking tool. For visual observations I and the target object c, we use SAM2 to identify and follow the movement of the object c in a visual sequence. Formally, given
input visual observations I and the target object c, we get
the tracking results I ′ = SAM2 (I, c), where I ′ denotes visual observations with marks to highlight the movement of
the object c. For each object correspondence relationship,
we visualize the correspondences directly in the image as
a marker. Specifically, for each object, we overlay a circle
with a radius of 4 pixels at the object center onto the image.
We also try to use the whole mask of the object. However,
simply adding marks achieves the best performance. We
analyze that using the whole mask occludes visual content,
leading to poor results.

Temporal grounding. It is important to know when something starts and ends for video understanding. Temporal
grounding allows VLMs to use temporal information by
trimming out the relevant segment. This can also enable
better interpretability in knowing which part of the video
the agent chose to consider. We apply TFVTG [102] as the
temporal grounding tool. For visual observations I and the
text query Q, temporal grounding aims to localize relevant
video segments that correspond specifically to given textual queries, enabling detailed interaction with video content. Temporal grounding also reduces the visual tokens
and improves efficiency.
Image grounding. For a single image, grounding aims to
localize a specific region within an image based on a given
textual description. Although current VLMs show strong
grounding ability, we choose Grounding-DINO-L [44] with

4. Experiments
4.1. Setup
Benchmarks and metrics. We first evaluate LAST
on three spatial understanding tasks. ScanQA [3] and
SQA3D [47] are two 3D question-answering benchmarks.
For ScanQA [3], we report BLEU-1/4 [56], METEOR [7],
ROUGE-L [38], CIDEr [74] for the validation set that includes 4675 questions from 71 scenes, following previous work [29, 86, 101]. For SQA3D [47], we evaluate on its test set that contains 3519 QA pairs. Because
SQA3D provides clear answers, we use exact match accuracy (EM-1) and relaxed accuracy (EM-R1) as evaluation metrics. VSI-Bench [91] contains 5130 questions collected from egocentric videos sourced from ScanNet [20],
ScanNet++ [93], and ARKitScenes [21]. VSI-Bench [91]
includes two task types: Multiple-Choice Answer (MCA)
and Numerical Answer (NA). We report accuracy for MCA
tasks and relative accuracy across confidence threshold C =
{0.5, 0.55, · · · , 0.95} for NA tasks following [91].
Furthermore, we consider four video understanding
benchmarks. EgoSchema [50] consists of 3-minute-long
clips, each with a question and 5 answer choices. We use
the validation set of 500 questions that have publicly available answers. NExT-QA [89] is a video question answering
benchmark. We use its validation set with 4996 multiplechoice questions for our evaluation. Additionally, following
previous work [37, 94], we also report results on the more
challenging ATP-hard subset. LVBench [79] includes 1549
multiple-choice questions with an average video length of
over one hour, which measures models’ ability to process
and reason about extended temporal sequences. MotionBench [28] evaluates fine-grained motion comprehension in
video-understanding models. We report results on the dev
set, which contains 4018 multiple-choice questions. For the
above four video benchmarks, we report accuracy because
they only include multiple-choice questions.
Although our work focuses on 3D spatial and temporal
understanding, for a complete evaluation, we also add three
image benchmarks: HR-Bench [78] (including 4K and 8K
subsets), V* [88], and BLINK [26] that are widely used in
previous works [5, 30].

the benefits of thinking in space and temporal over thinking with only text, we compare it with text-only CoT on
three benchmarks. Results shown in Tab. 6 indicate that

We propose LAST, a method that makes VLMs think in
spatial and temporal dimensions, thereby improving spatial
and temporal understanding. By using external tools, LAST
builds thinking chains in 3D space and time rather than in
text alone. LAST not only works on proprietary models
like GPT-4o but can also be adapted to open-weight models
through fine-tuning. Experimental results show that LAST
achieves significant gains on various benchmarks. Furthermore, we analyze the thinking process of LAST and textonly CoT, demonstrating the superiority of thinking in spatial and temporal dimensions over text-based thinking.

Thinking pattern analysis. To understand the difference
between text CoT and LAST, we present a qualitative example in Fig. 4. For the question How many sofa(s) are in
this room?, VLMs need to be able to retrieve frames that
include sofas and understand correspondence of them, i.e.
“Are the sofas appearing in different frames of the video
the same one?”. VLMs with text CoT fail to understand
correspondence, although two sofas have appeared in the
video. However, equipped with LAST, VLMs can use external tools to re-sample video frames (to retrieve all frames
including sofas) and identify the object correspondence in
every frame with the extra object tracking model.

Although LAST requires more tokens than the base model
with text CoT, the increased latency is not significant. This
is because visual tokens are usually input in the prefill stage,
which is compute-bound and can be highly parallelized. In
our settings, we deploy LAST-7B on a single A800 GPU
with 32K context with vLLM. The throughput of LAST7B achieves >9000 tokens per second. In addition, for
lightweight tool models, we also deploy each model on a
single A800 GPU with Triton Inference Server. For all
tasks, the average increased latency compared with using
Qwen2.5-VL-7B [4] is less than 1 second.

In Sec. 3.2, we propose to minimize the visual redundancy
to select frames. Solving Eq. (5) is NP-hard, we use fast
algorithm introduced in [11] to solve Eq. (5). The algorithm only includes a small number of inner product of two
vectors, and can run efficiently. The complete algorithm
is summarized in Algorithm 1. To get K images from T
images, Algorithm 1 runs in O(K 2 T ) and needs additional
space O(KT ). The stopping criteria is |S| = K. We introduce a small number ϵ = 1e−5 and add d2j < ϵ to the
stopping criteria for numerical stability of calculating 1/dj
following [11].

C. Evaluation Details
Context length and multi-turn thinking. Adapting VLMs
for video understanding tasks often suffer from limited context length. In our experiments, if the token length exceeds
the model limit, we discard earlier rounds until the model
length limit is met. Another approach to reduce context
length is only using single-turn thinking, i.e. only allow
models use at most one tool to solve problem. We compare this design with only single-turn mode in Tab. 9. It is
shown that multi-turn mode gets significant gains compared
with single-turn mode. So we use multi-turn mode that allows VLMs to think and call tools in multiple rounds.
Decoding parameters. For LAST-7B and Qwen2.5-VL7B, we use temperature=0, top-k=1, top-p=0.001, following
the Qwen official repository. For GPT-4o, we set temperature to 0 with the OpenAI Python SDK.
