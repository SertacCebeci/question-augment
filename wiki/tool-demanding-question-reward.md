# Tool-demanding question reward

A question-writer can be paid for the behaviour the wider system needs from it. In Agent0, a curriculum agent writes problems for an executor that has a code interpreter, and the writer's reward includes a term for how often the executor calls that interpreter on the problem. The term is a count, not a judgement of the call: the number of tool-output markers in the executor's trajectory, scaled and capped. The stated purpose is to make the problems depend on the tool: "we must explicitly reward tasks that prompt the Executor to use its tool."[^a1]

## Mechanism

The count is taken from "the number of tool invocations" in the executor's complete answer, and it is multiplied by a scale γ and clipped at a cap C.[^a2] The cap is there, the paper says, "to prevent rewarding excessive or spurious tool use."[^a3] This term is added to the uncertainty term that the same proposer is paid for, the solver's disagreement on the question, which is the mechanism in [[uncertainty-peaked-challenge-reward]].[^a9] A repetition penalty is then subtracted, and the total is gated by a format check.[^a4][^a10]

The reason for pairing the two is a loop the paper describes. The executor learns to use the interpreter, and that improvement is meant to push the writer toward "more complex, tool-based curricula."[^a5] So the tool term is the writer's half of a co-evolution in which both agents are trained by reinforcement learning from a common base model.

## What was measured

The paper reports three things, none of which isolates the tool term alone against a paired comparison.

First, across curriculum iterations the executor's pass rate on each generated set falls: it "progressively decreases when evaluated on task sets generated by the curriculum agents" from iterations one to three.[^a6] Second, the average number of tool calls per generated task rises over the same iterations, which the authors read as the reward doing its job: "calls per generated task steadily increases across iterations."[^a7] My reading of their Table 5 gives 1.65, 2.10 and 2.60 calls per task for the three sets. Third, the ablation: removing the tool term lowers the score, and the text says the model "drops by 7.2%" when the tool reward is not included.[^a8]

## Limits

- The count does not check the need for a call. A marker is rewarded whether the problem required the interpreter or the executor reached for it out of habit. The cap bounds the reward but does not separate the two. A solver-side design that does separate them is [[benefit-scaled-tool-reward]], which pays for a call only where the same item answered with the tool beats the item answered without it. Agent0 has no such contrast.
- The rising call counts and falling pass rates come from one training run. Table 5 does not show that the extra calls were needed, nor that the harder sets are harder because they demand tools rather than for other reasons.
- The ablation figure does not reconcile cleanly with Table 3. The text's 7.2% does not reproduce under a simple reading of the table columns, and I did not resolve which column the sentence refers to.
- The results are on Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B base models with one sandboxed interpreter. The scale of 0.6 and the cap of 4 are the authors' choices; the parts of the text I read contain no sweep of either.
- My inference, untested: a writer rewarded for markers could learn to set up problems that invite a call adding nothing to the answer. The paper's cap is its only guard against this.
