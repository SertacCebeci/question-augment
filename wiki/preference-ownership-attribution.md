# Preference ownership attribution

An answer conditioned on a preference presupposes that the preference belongs to the person being answered. A history contains more than that person's own statements. It contains hypothetical requests typed to test the assistant, messages written by someone else and pasted in, and preferences of other people the user talks about. Before a preference can constrain the answer, the answerer has to decide whose it is. PersonaMem-v2 names this failure: chatbots "often misinterpret user behavior," and the confusion "can occur when people test the model with hypothetical examples to explore its capabilities, or with messages written by a third person," because "the model fails to distinguish them from the" user's own persona.[^c1][^c2]

The benchmark measures the gap. Across the evaluated models, the accuracy on the user's own preferences is 42.1 ± 4.9%, but on preferences of others the accuracy is "only 17.5 ± 11.7%," with the two cells drawn from the same kind of history.[^c3][^c4] The paper's breakdown is "aggregated across all evaluated models," so the cells describe the population of models, not a single system.[^c5]

The mechanism, as I read it (my inference, not a result the paper states): a model that attributes each stated preference to the person it is talking to will answer that person with someone else's constraint, and it will do so with the same confidence it has for the correct constraint. The benchmark is built to make this costly. All four options are reasonable, and "only one is personalized to the current user,"[^c6] so an answer that follows a third party's preference is a plausible-looking wrong answer rather than a visibly absurd one.

For asking and answering, the consequence is an order of operations. The answerer's first question about a piece of evidence is not what it implies but whose it is, and a question put to the asker can settle that before the implication is used. The paper does not test whether such a question helps; the claim that it would is mine.

## Limits

- The 17.5 figure carries a spread of ±11.7, nearly as large as the value itself. The own-preference cell is firmer than the other-party cell, and the gap should be read as large but imprecisely measured.
- The breakdown is aggregated across models, and the text I read reports no per-model ownership accuracies. Whether one model is much worse than the others is not established here.
- The ownership cases are generated by the same GPT-5 pipeline as the rest of the benchmark, so the third-party messages are synthetic. Whether real pasted messages from other people behave the same way is not tested.
- The ownership cell is not separated from the population-prior effect in the paper. Other-party preferences and anti-stereotypical preferences may overlap in the items, so the two causes are confounded in the reported numbers.
- No intervention is measured. Neither a prompt that asks the model to flag whose preference a piece of evidence is, nor a clarifying question to the user, is tested in the work.
