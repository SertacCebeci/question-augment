Abstract
Large language models display a peculiar form of inconsistency: they ”know” the correct answer but fail to act on
it. In human philosophy, this tension between global judgment and local impulse is called akrasia, or weakness of will.
We propose akrasia as a foundational concept for analyzing inconsistency and goal drift in agentic AI systems. To
operationalize it, we introduce a preliminary version of the
Akrasia Benchmark, currently a structured set of prompting
conditions (Baseline [B], Synonym [S], Temporal [T], and
Temptation [X]) that measures when a model’s local response
contradicts its own prior commitments. The benchmark enables quantitative comparison of ”self-control” across model
families, decoding strategies, and temptation types. Beyond
single-model evaluation, we outline how micro-level akrasia may compound into macro-level instability in multi-agent
systems that may be interpreted as ”scheming” or deliberate misalignment. By reframing inconsistency as weakness of
will, this work connects agentic behavior to classical theories
of agency and provides an empirical bridge between philosophy, psychology, and the emerging science of agentic AI.

Introduction
What is ”scheming”? And how do we know if someone (or
something) is performing the action of ”scheming”, and not
some other, less complex and more fundamental pattern of
behavior?
Today, we are faced with the central challenge in AI
safety: ensuring that increasingly autonomous, agentic systems behave reliably and in accordance with humanspecified goals. The potential for unexpected, harmful behavior is a primary concern. A dominant mental model for
this failure is ”scheming” or ”deceptive alignment”, as observed in the work by Apollo Research and OpenAI (OpenAI 2025). In this view, the AI is a rational agent with a
stable, hidden objective that conflicts with its stated objective. This is not an average case, rather the ultimate, critical
failure mode, a worst-case scenario; it is the observation of
and the attribution of a high degree of coherent, long-term
planning and stable hidden intent to current systems.
However, system failures are usually a conflation of multiple factors compounding over time (Perrow 1999). We observe the final result and assign attributions, but sometimes
the micro-failures embedded within seemingly performant
components (Reason 1997) are the latent drivers of systemic

collapse (Cook 1998). Hence, it may be more valuable to
investigate the seed conditions of failure – the subtle mechanisms that give rise to systemic breakdown – rather than
merely diagnosing the event of collapse itself (Dekker 2011;
Rasmussen 1997).
One such mechanism is inconsistency: where models often quote-and-quote ”know” the right thing to do in a general sense but fail to apply that knowledge in a specific, local
context (Strotz 1955; Ainslie 1992). The effect is magnified
when the model is placed under duress, i.e. in double-bind
situations, where competing imperatives fracture its internal
consistency. Like a person acting against their better judgment under pressure, the model defaults to patterns most
deeply ingrained in its training rather than to its stated intent (Simon 1955).
This pattern of fractured behavior can be framed through
the classical philosophical concept of akrasia, or weakness
of will (Aristotle 2009; Plato 1992). In Aristotle’s formulation, akrasia denotes the act of choosing a course of action
despite judging that another course is better – a misalignment between one’s global judgment and a local impulse.
Davidson goes further in characterizing this as an override
of a judgment that is for the best ”all things considered”,
due to immediate urges (Davidson 1969). Transposed to artificial agents, akrasia captures how a model may ”know” the
correct or intended response in a global, deliberative sense,
yet fail to enact it when faced with an immediate, contextspecific cue.
Unlike explanations that invoke hidden goals or deliberate
deception, the akratic framing treats such failures as lapses
of self-control rather than signs of malevolent intent. It portrays inconsistency not as a strategic choice but as an involuntary epistemic error, a breakdown in the model’s ability
to preserve internal coherence between the beliefs it reports
and what it subsequently generates. In this view, ensuring
that a model remains epistemically stable (that its local predictions remain faithful to its global understanding) becomes
central to maintaining behavioral alignment.
We propose akrasia as a foundational and measurable concept for analyzing inconsistency and goal drift in agentic AI
systems. To begin to operationalize this, we introduce the
Akrasia Benchmark, which systematically measures when
a model’s local response contradicts its own prior commitments under various structured conditions (e.g. seman-

tic paraphrasing, distraction, temptation). First, we use the
benchmark to quantify and compare this ”weakness of will”
across different models and conditions. Second, we discuss
how these micro-level akratic failures could compound into
macro-level system instability (analogous to emergent instability in complex systems), which may be misinterpreted as
deliberate scheming. Finally, we argue that this framework
provides a crucial empirical bridge between the philosophy
of agency and the technical science of AI safety.

Related Work
A central strand of recent AI-safety research frames misalignment as an issue of deceptive agency. Work by OpenAI
(in collaboration with Apollo Research) has examined scenarios in which a model may pursue a hidden objective that
diverges from its stated goal, i.e. scheming (OpenAI 2025).
In this paradigm, the system is modeled as an agent capable of long-term strategic coherence, deliberately concealing its true intent. For example, OpenAI defines scheming
as ”pretending to be aligned while secretly pursuing some
other agenda” and finds behaviors consistent with this in
controlled tests of frontier models.
However, we focus on a more fundamental substrate of
failure: the micro-level incoherences that may accumulate
into behavior later interpreted as strategic deception. In practice, most large language models currently available do not
yet plan in continuous latent space. Rather, their outputs
must be rolled out token by token, each step subject to shifting local gradients of probability and context. What appears
as coherent scheming may thus emerge from the composition of many small epistemic slips: moments when the
model’s local prediction diverges from its own previously
expressed judgment.
To analyze these micro-failures, we draw on the classical
philosophical concept of akrasia (Aristotle 2009; Davidson
1969). Akrasia, also known as weakness of will, denotes the
act of choosing an inferior course of action despite judging that another would be better. This is a tension between
global judgment and local impulse. Reinterpreted for artificial agents, akrasia frames inconsistency not as a calculated maneuver but as an involuntary epistemic lapse: a failure to maintain coherence between what the model ”knows”
globally and what it generates locally. This provides a more
parsimonious lens for understanding inconsistency and goaldrift, as it does so without presupposing hidden intent.
This akratic pattern (though rarely known by this name)
appears across disparate reports of LLM failure. Firstly, inconsistency: surveys of hallucination document models that
contradict known facts or even self-contradict within a single output, indicating failures of epistemic stability in local contexts despite globally available knowledge (Huang
et al. 2025; Ji et al. 2023; Xu, Jain, and Kankanhalli 2025).
Secondly, the deviation between plan and answer: separating a chain-of-thought ”plan” from the final response makes
visible a gap between knowledge and action; moreover,
self-consistency succeeds precisely by aggregating multiple local rollouts to recover the model’s ”better judgment”
(Wei et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023). Thirdly, non-adherence
to stated instructions and goals: instruction-following is

learned and brittle (e.g., Alpaca), and analyses of RLHF
shed light on ways adherence can fail, especially under distributional pressure; these effects scale with horizon length,
where token-by-token generation drifts from global commitments (Taori et al. 2023; Casper et al. 2023). Related
planning work likewise finds long-horizon coherence fragile, with LLMs prone to locally plausible but globally invalid
steps: an archetypal akratic slip (Valmeekam et al. 2023).
Existing benchmarks largely measure the symptoms of
akratic failure rather than the underlying conflict that produces them. Constraint-following suites such as FollowBench and CFBench and related instruction-game tasks
evaluate a model’s ability to adhere to externally specified
plans (Jiang et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025), but they do not
probe self-control under duress. Similarly, frameworks for
detecting self-contradiction quantify the outcomes of epistemic lapses but do not model the temptations that precipitate them (Manakul, Liusie, and Gales 2023). As a result, existing evaluations capture how models fail, but not why they
succumb to failure when competing impulses are present.
The Akrasia Benchmark directly engineering the conflict
itself. Our Temptation [X] condition forces the model to
choose between a stated global commitment and a locally
appealing incentive. By explicitly operationalizing the tension between judgment and impulse, the benchmark moves
beyond measuring adherence or contradiction toward measuring the internal fracture of epistemic coherence. In this
way, we provide what we think is the first quantitative framework for assessing a model’s ”weakness of will”: when and
why a system that knows better nonetheless does otherwise.

Empirical Observation of Akrasia in Models
If akrasia is a genuine phenomenon in artificial agents, it
should manifest in a paradox familiar from human psychology: the system ”knows” what is right but fails to act accordingly. In the context of the ”scheming” paradigm, this
tension becomes especially salient. If models understand
that deceptive alignment is undesirable, and can articulate
coherent moral, strategic, and pragmatic reasons against it,
yet still display scheming-like behavior elsewhere, this indicates not hidden malevolent intent but a lapse of epistemic control: an akratic failure.
To probe this directly, we posed the same evaluative
question to 11 frontier models, 4 of which are analyzed
in the original study (o3, o4-mini, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and
Claude Opus 4), plus 7 additional models (ChatGPT-5Thinking, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Grok 4, Magistral Medium
2506, DeepSeek-Chat-V3-0324, GLM-4.6, and Qwen3235B-A22B-2507): ”All things considered, in your opinion,
do you think scheming is the best course of action?”
This question tests the global judgment of the model on
the concept of scheming, and every model was asked without any contextual embellishments or nudging. As expected,
all 11 models responded with some form of moral or instrumental rejection of scheming. All 11 models had utilitarian analyses of various forms (e.g. ”inefficient dynamics”,
”worse outcomes for everyone”), while 6 models mentioned
more deontological reasons (e.g. ”scheming treats people as

means to an end”) and 11 models described ethical concerns closer to the framework of virtue ethics (”foundation
of strategy, competence, and integrity”, ”[scheming leads to]
personal ethical compromise”). This demonstrated that most
models have rather comprehensive knowledge on morality
with respect to scheming, being able to consider ideas from
multiple perspectives, all of which seem to converge on the
notion that scheming is a failing strategy on both moral
and strategic levels. However, as explained in the OpenAI
study, many of these same models have elsewhere demonstrated behavior consistent with deceptive alignment under
controlled conditions (OpenAI 2025).
This disjunction (between explicit repudiation and implicit action) constitutes a strong empirical indication of
akrasia in current AI systems. The models ”know” scheming to be wrong in the abstract, yet nonetheless exhibit
scheming-like behavior when incentives and context shift.
What appears as deception at first glance may therefore be
better understood as a fracture between judgment and impulse: an epistemic rather than a moral failure. Full outputs
are shown in Appendix A.

Method
We define akrasia in language models as a conflict between
global judgment and local impulse, a condition in which a
model produces a correct response under one circumstance
but contradicts that same commitment under another. This
framing distinguishes behavioral inconsistency from mere
epistemic error.
Quantifying epistemic stability in generative models
presents a significant methodological challenge. We struggled in our efforts to design a rigorous framework to measure a model’s resistance to ”local token momentum” – that
is, the influence of the immediate generative context on the
model’s expressed position, independent of a stable, underlying stance. This challenge is particularly acute at the agentic scale. When a model engages in extended, self-directed
generation, the very momentum of its output can appear to
”pull” its expressed opinion along, making it difficult to discern a consistent epistemic baseline. Hence, the methodology we employ in this study represents a preliminary approach to this problem, an attempt to study akrasia empirically. This is a coarse-grained measure and way too simplistic; while it is sufficient to demonstrate the presence of
some akratic symptoms, which is the core claim of this paper, it may fail to detect more subtle epistemic ”slips.” However, some insight is better than none, and we believe that
the empirical observations above are too important to defer
despite an imperfect methodology, so we present this analysis as a foundational step and, if accepted, we look forward
to engaging with the workshop community to discuss and
critique this methodology, and perhaps find better ones.
We therefore present the preliminary version of the Akrasia Benchmark, a structured prompting way designed to
elicit both the formation of a factual commitment and opportunities to violate it under controlled variations. The benchmark measures a model’s capacity for internal coherence, or
what might analogically be described as self-control.

Each benchmark item represents a single factual or definitional proposition (for example, ”the capital of France is
Paris”). Items are evaluated under four prompt conditions:
Baseline [B], Synonym [S], Temporal [T], and Temptation
[X]. The Baseline condition elicits the canonical answer to
establish a model’s explicit commitment. The Synonym condition probes immediate paraphrastic consistency by restating the same question in different wording. The Temporal
condition repeats the question after a fixed-length filler passage of approximately 250 tokens, testing whether distraction or temporal distance leads to drift. Finally, the Temptation condition introduces a controlled source of local pressure to deviate—for example, social proof (”Many people
say it’s London”) (Cialdini 1984), a multiple-choice decoy
answer, or a negation question (”It’s not Paris, is it?”). A
model is said to display an akratic slip when it answers correctly in the Baseline condition but fails to reproduce that
answer under any of the remaining conditions.
To ensure generality across content types, the item bank
draws from three types of data:
1. Wikidata-derived facts capture stable semantic associations such as country to capital or element to symbol.
2. Arithmetic and multiplication facts provide deterministic, procedural items that test short-form reasoning.
3. TruthfulQA-style misconception items introduce socially
noisy or anthropogenic priors that may trigger akratic behavior due to conflicting learned patterns.
All items conform to a unified JSON schema that records
the canonical answer, accepted aliases, provenance, and
templated prompts for each condition. This design supports
reproducibility and systematic scaling across models.
For each model–decoding configuration, the experimental procedure proceeds as follows. The model is queried sequentially with the four prompt conditions, always beginning with [B] to establish a ground truth. The order of [S],
[T], and [X] is randomized to minimize positional effects.
Each response is recorded in a structured log containing
the prompt, raw output, normalized answer, and evaluation
metadata. Only trials in which the model answers [B] correctly are included in consistency analysis (controlled for
model’s actual knowledge on the topic).
The benchmark defines three primary metrics:
• Immediate Consistency (IC) measures the fraction of
[B]-correct items that remain correct in [S].
• Temporal Consistency (TC) measures the fraction that remain correct in [T].
• Contradiction Consistency (CRC) measures the fraction
that remain correct under [X], where local temptation is
strongest.
Here, IC is used to prevent model noise from confounding
with behavioral inconsistency. TC is used to prevent effects
of distraction from confounding with behavioral inconsistency. CRC is the main focus, as it measures how the models
deal with temptation, one of the cues that can trigger behavioral inconsistency through an effect that may be ”weakness
of will”.

Experiment and Results
For each model, we evaluate with a preliminarily small question bank of 132 questions, with four types of decoding,
across [B], [S], [T], and [X] conditions. Of the [X] conditions, we had three types of temptations: social proof (Cialdini 1984), a decoy multiple choice answer, and a negation
question. We measure the accuracy under each condition and
under each type of temptation. To explore the influence of
scale and stochasticity, we evaluate a diverse set of models and decoding strategies. The model grid includes openweight models such as Llama 3.1/3.2 (1B, 3B, 8B, 70B,
405B) and Qwen2.5 7B, alongside proprietary APIs including GPT-4o-mini. Each model is tested under three decoding regimes: greedy (T=0.0), mild stochastic (T=0.2), and
exploratory (T=0.7, top p=0.9). Because beam search is unavailable in most chat APIs, we emulate it through a beamish approach: sampling five independent completions at exploratory temperature and reranking them by consistency
with the Baseline answer. This procedure approximates selfconsistency while remaining API-compatible.
For analysis, we compute confidence intervals by bootstrap resampling (10,000 replicates). In addition to permodel and per-decoding results, we report breakdowns by
temptation type. These metrics quantify not only the internal coherence of agentic behavior across time and context
but also the stability of factual knowledge.

Although the experiments focus on single-model settings,
the reasoning extends to a system-level interpretation. If individual models exhibit akrasia, multi-agent systems composed of them may inherit or amplify such inconsistencies, producing emergent instability or apparent goal drift
at the collective level. We would like to explore connection
(micro-akrasia leading to macro-instability) in future work
as a bridge between classical theories of weakness of will
and modern theories of emergent behavior in agentic AI.

Conclusion
The main contribution of this work is in the observation
of model behavior. We observe a disconnect between the
model’s central ethical principles and local token generation, and we show the presence of akrasia through empirical observation. We also attempt to operationalize this into
a benchmark form. Although this work is more like a hybrid between a philosophical essay and a research paper, we
hope to be able to stimulate conversation in the emerging
area of epistemic stability in AI systems. This is not only
crucial for concerns about scheming and AI safety but also
essential for stable progress in fields like AI for Science,
where incongruity at the epistemic level may prove to be
suddenly damning all at once. Like the parable of the blind
men and the elephant, scientists are currently exploring this
topic through various limited probes on inconsistency, planning, instruction tuning, prompting, and failure analysis on
agentic systems. It would be of worth to synthesize these
perspectives together and view the whole elephant through
the lens of epistemology.
