Abstract — The escalating complexity of modern cyber
threats demands innovative approaches to security education
that
transcend
traditional
pedagogical
methods.
Conventional training paradigms often fail to engage
learners meaningfully or develop the intuitive reasoning
necessary for effective threat recognition. This paper
introduces an interactive educational framework that
reimagines cybersecurity awareness through the lens of a
structured guessing game. Our approach integrates
explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) principles with
reinforcement learning to create a dynamic learning
environment where users discover cybersecurity concepts
through guided inquiry. The proposed system employs a
policy-based reinforcement learning agent that assumes the
role of a knowledgeable questioner, systematically narrowing
down user-described security scenarios until it can both
identify the underlying threat and provide transparent
reasoning for its conclusion. By framing security education
as an interactive dialogue, we transform passive knowledge
acquisition into active discovery. We present the complete
system architecture, detail the underlying algorithmic
foundations, and demonstrate practical application through
comprehensive case studies examining diverse attack vectors
including the Cyber Kill Chain, phishing campaigns,
ransomware outbreaks, and web application vulnerabilities.
This work represents a significant departure from static
security training methodologies, offering a personalized and
game-based approach to cybersecurity education.
Keywords - Cybersecurity Education, Game-Based Learning, 20
Questions (Q20), Explainable AI (XAI), Learning-to-Explain
(LTE), Attack Vectors, Interactive and Adaptive Learning,
Reinforcement Learning (RL).

I. INTRODUCTION
The cybersecurity landscape is constantly evolving due
to the introduction of new threats and vulnerabilities. The
high volume of innovative security threats increases the
difficulty of educating both the general public and
professionals. Traditional trainings typically include
lectures and reading static materials. These methods do not
engage learners much and fall short of imparting the
practical understanding and intuition required to counter
highly sophisticated attacks.
To fill this gap, we propose an innovative solution that
combines the captivating aspects of games with the
powerful capabilities of explainable AI (XAI). The “20

Questions” (Q20) game, as a framework for this purpose,
is a well-known exercise of logic and reasoning [5, 15].
Within our structure, a user briefly describes a
cybersecurity concept as a brief text input to the game
interface, and an AI agent in the role of a guesser tries to
uncover it by asking detailed questions designed to narrow
down the possibilities. This active process of learning not
only retains attention but also facilitates the understanding
of complex cybersecurity concepts.
Our work builds upon recent advancements in
reinforcement learning for game-playing [1, 3] and
explainable AI in cyber-awareness recommendation
systems [2, 5]. We adapt the policy-based RL framework,
which has been successfully applied to the Q20 game in
guessing famous people [1] or product recommendation
[2], to the domain of cybersecurity. Furthermore, this
methodology is inspired by the work of Wu (2019) where
a "Learning-to-Explain" (LTE) framework was
successfully applied to product recommendations [2]. We
adapt and extend this concept to the domain of
cybersecurity, with an explanation mechanism from Wu
(2019) [2], for a cybersecurity education recommender
within the framework.
We outline the complete architecture of the system
developed,
which
encompasses
policy-based
reinforcement learning and an entropy-based question
selection/elimination policy, with an explanation
generation module. To illustrate the framework's
usefulness, we apply it to intricate cybersecurity topics,
including the Cyber Kill Chain and multiple attack vectors,
along with case studies. This approach is aimed at being a
significant advancement in improving the effectiveness and
reach of cybersecurity education.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section II reviews foundational literature related to LTE
methodologies in cybersecurity and Q20 games. Section III
introduces the proposed LTE framework with policy-based
RL. Followed by Section IV, illustrating representative
examples of attack vectors with case studies. Finally,
Section VI concludes the study by summarizing key
insights and implications.

II. BACKGROUND
The application of game-based learning and artificial
intelligence in education has gained significant traction in
recent years. This section reviews existing literature in the
areas of game-based learning for cybersecurity, the use of
chatbots in education, and the principles of Explainable AI
(XAI) that underpin our approach.
A. Game-Based Learning in Cybersecurity
Game-based learning is widely recognized as an
effective instructional strategy for enhancing student
motivation and engagement, particularly when teaching
complex or challenging concepts. In cybersecurity
education, multiple studies have examined the use of
games to simulate real-world scenarios and provide
practical experience. These methods include tabletop card
games and advanced digital simulations of cyber-attacks.
For example, "Control-Alt-Hack" [16] is a card game
developed to raise awareness about white-hat hacking,
while online platforms, such as "Anti-Phishing Phil" [17],
instruct users on defending against phishing attacks
through interactive gameplay. These cases demonstrate the
potential of gamification to make cybersecurity education
more accessible and engaging. The proposed Q20
framework extends this approach by introducing a
narrative-driven, deductive game that emphasizes
conceptual understanding over technical simulation.
B. Chatbots for Educational Purposes
Chatbots and conversational agents are increasingly
being adopted as educational tools. These offer the
possibility of individualized learning, immediate feedback
on exercises, and natural responses to queries raised by
learners. Research on security training using chatbots has
yielded encouraging results. Although quantitative
improvements in knowledge may not always reach
statistical significance [3], the demonstrated willingness to
learn and the increased engagement reported by users
interacting with educational chatbots are invaluable. This
approach utilizes a chatbot not just passively as an
information repository but actively guiding the learners
through structured dialog discovery and understanding
complex cybersecurity issues [9, 12].
C. Explainable AI (XAI) in the Learning-to-Explain
(LTE) Framework
Explainable AI (XAI) is particularly important in this
work and drives action in generating explanations. XAI
aims to clarify and make comprehensible decisions and
predictions of AI systems. This is particularly true in the
case of cybersecurity, where explaining the reasoning
behind an AI-powered decision could be crucial.
Wu (2019) [2] adapted the Learning-to-Explain (LTE)
model, which is followed in our work. Wu demonstrated
how a Q20-style game, combined with a question-ranking
algorithm, is utilized for explainable product

recommendations. The system recommends a product
based on user responses and also explains the
recommendation by revealing the most critical questionanswer pair that impacted the decision. We apply this
paradigm to cybersecurity education, where the objective
is to guide the user in comprehending specific cyberattack. In this framework, the “explanation” is a brief and
precise description of the attack based on the user’s path
through the Q20 game. This methodology aligns with the
broader goals of XAI, emphasizing transparency,
interpretability, and user-centered understanding. In this
case, the explanation is the outcome of engaging in an
interactive educational process.
With the combination of game-based learning,
educational chatbots, and explainable AI, the proposed
framework offers a comprehensive solution to address the
current gap in engaging cybersecurity education. It strives
to develop an environment that would be just as
transparent and empowering for the user while being
effective and engaging. Figure 1 presents a root cause
analysis that maps a strategic methodology aligned with
enhancing cybersecurity education through the LTE-Q20
game framework.

III. "LEARNING-TO-EXPLAIN" (LTE) FRAMEWORK
The proposed LTE framework for cybersecurity is
designed as an interactive system where users engage with
a question-based chatbot to learn about various cyber
threats. This section details the architecture and the
underlying algorithm of the framework.
The core of the framework is a "cybersecurity attack
vectors", which include Phishing, Ransomware, and Manin-the-Middle attacks. The “questions” are tailored to
solicit details that would differentiate one attack from
another. These questions focus on various endeavors of
cyber-attacks, including the method of delivery, the goals
of the attacker, the intended victim, and the possible
consequences. The policy-based reinforcement learning is

B. Question Ranking Algorithms
1. Policy-Based Reinforcement Learning Agent
The core of our system is a policy-based RL agent that
acts as the questioner in the Q20 game. The interaction
between the user and the LTE framework is modeled as a
finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP). This
setup enables the policy-based RL agent to sequentially
choose questions (up to 20 questions) that optimize the
long-term reward of accurately identifying the user's target
cybersecurity concept [2]. The Q20 gaming process is
defined by the MDP tuple:

Identification and Explanation: The game ends when a
set number of questions (up to 20) have been asked or when
the likelihood of a single attack vector exceeds a specific
threshold. The system then identifies the most probable
attack and provides a detailed justification with an
explanation. This explanation highlights how the user's
decisions led to the identification of the particular threat
and is closely linked to their responses.
The proposed framework is designed to be an
interactive and adaptive learning environment. It consists
of four primary components: the Knowledge Base, the
Question Ranking approach, the Game Environment, and
the Explanation Generation Module.

Training: The agent is trained through continuous
interaction with a user simulator, which is programmed to
think of a cybersecurity concept and answer questions
based on the knowledge base. The policy network is
updated using the REINFORCE algorithm to maximize
the expected long-term reward [2].
The reinforcement learning model is trained through
self-play, where the system simulates thousands of Q20
games against itself. This enables the policy network to
learn the complex relationships between questions,
answers, and attack vectors, and to develop sophisticated,
long-term strategies for guiding users to the correct
identification [5].
The initial phase of the Q20 game can be improved
using an entropy-based method. The goal is to select
questions that provide the most information gain,
regardless of the user's answer. This is achieved by
calculating the "negative Shannon entropy" for each
possible question. Questions that most effectively
decrease uncertainty across potential attack vectors are
ranked higher. This approach ensures that the initial
questions are broad and quickly narrow down the list of
possibilities.

C. Game Environment and User Interaction
The game environment facilitates the interaction
between the user and the RL agent. The user initiates the
game by articulating a general description of a
cybersecurity concept in a few sentences. The agent then
asks a series of yes/no or multiple-choice questions to the
user. The user's answers update the agent's belief state,
which in turn informs the selection of the next question.
The game will be deployed as a chatbot, a web-based
application, or can be integrated into existing learning
management systems.

D. Information Gain and Explanation Generation Module
A key feature of the LTE framework is its ability to
provide clear and contextualized explanations. This is
achieved by tracking the "information gain" associated
with each question-answer pair. The information gain is
defined as the change in probability of the final identified
attack vector after the user answers a particular question
[2].
After the game concludes, the system examines all the
dialogues and determines which question-answer
combination provided the most information. The final
explanation is then built around this "pivotal" piece of
information (for instance, "Based on your answer that the
attack arrived via a deceptive email, the most likely threat
is Phishing"), will be stated explicitly, if the user's
response to a question regarding the attack's delivery
mechanism significantly increases the likelihood of
"Phishing". The learning process is transparent and
individualized due to the direct connection between the
user's input and the derived system output [6, 7].
IV. ATTACK VECTORS AND CASE STUDIES
This framework can be used to explain a varied array
of complex cybersecurity topics. Below are a few
examples:
A. The Cyber Kill Chain
The Cyber Kill Chain is a model developed by
Lockheed Martin that outlines the stages of a cyber-attack
[11]. Explaining this multi-stage process can be
challenging. Our Q20 game can deconstruct it by focusing
on the distinct phases.
Sample Q20 Dialogue for the "Reconnaissance" Stage:
Agent: Briefly describe the incident you are
experiencing
User: … (Brief text input)
Agent: Is the activity you're thinking of, is the very first
stage of a planned cyber-attack?
User: Yes.

Agent: Does this stage involve actively probing the
target's network for vulnerabilities?
User: No.
Agent: Is the primary goal of this stage to gather
information about the target, such as employee names
or network infrastructure details?
User: Yes.
Agent: Are techniques like harvesting email addresses
or researching social media profiles common in this
stage?
User: Yes.
Agent: I believe the stage you are thinking of is
Reconnaissance.
Generated Explanation: "Reconnaissance is the initial
phase of a cyber-attack where the attacker gathers
information about the target. This is often a passive
stage that doesn't involve direct engagement with the
target's systems. The goal is to identify potential
vulnerabilities and plan the subsequent stages of the
attack."
B. Attack Vectors Explained with Case Studies
Attack vectors refer to the techniques employed by
threat actors to obtain unauthorized access to networks or
systems. The proposed framework facilitates user
comprehension of these vectors through interactive
questioning supported by real-world examples. The
inclusion of case studies, followed by the application of
the framework, illustrates how the approach can
effectively contextualize and reinforce key cybersecurity
concepts.
1. PHISHING
Case Study: The 2016 email leak from the DNC
In 2016, spear-phishing email attacks were made to the
members of the Democratic National Committee (DNC)
claimed as Google security alerts, asking them to change
their passwords. A few employees unknowingly allowed
the hackers to access their email accounts by complying.
Eventually, thousands of private emails were stolen and
made public with very dire political consequences [18].
Sample Q20 Dialogue for Phishing cyberattack:
Agent: Briefly describe the incident you are
experiencing
User: I received an email asking me to verify my
account. (Text input)
Agent: How is the attack delivered?
(A: Deceptive email, B: Malicious software, C: Network
interception)
User: A.
Agent: What is the primary goal of the attack?
(A: Steal login credentials, B: Encrypt files for ransom,
C: Disrupt service)
User: A.
Agent: Did the email appear from a trusted organization
(e.g., your bank, university, or IT department)?

User: Yes.
Agent: When you hovered over the link, did the URL
look suspicious or different from the official website?
User: Yes.
Agent: Did the message contain a link or attachment
asking you to log in or download something?
User: Yes.
Agent: Did you or anyone else enter credentials or
download a file from the email?
User: No.
Agent: Have you noticed any unusual account activity
or unauthorized access since then?
User: No.
Agent: Did this attack result in the theft and publication
of sensitive emails?
User: Yes.
Agent: The attack vector you are thinking of is
Phishing.
Generated Explanation: Phishing is a social
engineering attack in which adversaries convince users
to reveal sensitive data. Most often, this happens
through email or text message. In the 2016 DNC hack,
the assailants used spear-phishing emails purportedly
from Google to obtain user credentials. Once those
logins were obtained, access was granted to DNC email
accounts; later, thousands of extremely sensitive
communications were published. This example shows
how phishing bypasses human trust more than any
technical vulnerability but can have devastating effects
both organizationally and politically.
2. RANSOMWARE
Case Study: The WannaCry Attack (2017)
The WannaCry ransomware attack happened in 2017 and
quickly propagated to over 150 countries, affecting
hundreds of thousands of computers. It took advantage of
a flaw within old Windows systems (EternalBlue) to inject
ransomware that would then lock users’ files. A ransom
note would pop up asking for payments to be made in
Bitcoin so that keys could be sent to unlock the files. This
attack brought about a huge disruption all over the world
with business hours lost; it particularly affected the UK’s
National Health Service (NHS) and was one of the most
severely impacted, where appointments were canceled and
treatments delayed, besides considerable financial loss
[19].
Sample Q20 Dialogue for Ransomware cyber-attack:
Agent: Briefly describe the incident you are
experiencing
User: My computer suddenly showed a message saying
my files are encrypted. (Text input)
Agent: What is the primary impact of the attack on the
victim’s data?
(A: Data is stolen, B: Data is encrypted and made
inaccessible, C: Data is publicly exposed)
User: B.

Agent: What is the attacker’s primary motivation?
(A: Financial gain through a ransom demand, B:
Espionage, C: Political activism)
User: A.
Agent: Data encryption and demanding payment - are
these part of the attack?
(A: Yes, a ransom note and encryption are involved; B:
No, the attacker merely steals data; C: The attacker
deletes data without requesting a ransom.)
User: A.
Agent: What must victims do to regain access to their
encrypted files?
(A: Pay a ransom in Bitcoin, B: Reset their passwords,
C: Contact the attacker directly)
User: A.
Agent: The attack vector you are thinking of is
Ransomware.
Generated Explanation: Ransomware is a type of
malware that encrypts a victim’s data and demands
payment, often in cryptocurrency, to restore access. The
2017 WannaCry attack is one of the most well-known
examples. It leveraged the EternalBlue vulnerability in
unpatched Windows systems to spread rapidly across
networks. Victims were locked out of their files and
pressured to pay a ransom in Bitcoin for decryption keys.
The attack had devastating real-world effects, particularly
on the UK’s National Health Service (NHS), where
critical medical operations were disrupted. WannaCry
illustrates how ransomware combines technical
exploitation with financial extortion, making it one of the
most damaging and costly forms of cyberattacks to date.
3. SQL INJECTION (SQLI)
Case Study: The 2014-2015 TalkTalk Hack
In 2015, the UK telecommunications company TalkTalk
suffered a major data breach that exposed the personal
details of over 150,000 customers. The primary attack
vector was an SQL injection vulnerability on their website
[20].
Sample Q20 Dialogue for SQL Injection:
Agent: Briefly describe the incident you are
experiencing
User: Our website started showing strange database
errors, and some user information got exposed after
someone entered unusual characters into the login form.
(Text input)
Agent: Does this attack target a web application?
User: Yes.
Agent: Does it involve manipulating the input fields of
a website, such as a search bar or login form?
User: Yes.
Agent: Is the goal to trick the application into executing
unintended database commands?
User: Yes.

Agent: Can this vulnerability expose sensitive
information such as usernames, passwords, or financial
data from the database?
User: Yes.
Agent: Is this attack possible due to improper validation
or sanitization of user inputs in the application’s code?
User: Yes.
Agent: Does the attacker typically insert malicious SQL
statements into input fields to gain unauthorized access to
data?
User: Yes.
Agent: The attack vector you are thinking of is SQL
Injection.
Generated Explanation: "SQL Injection (SQLi) is a
web security vulnerability that allows an attacker to
interfere with the queries that an application makes to
its database. It generally allows an attacker to view data
that they are not normally able to retrieve. The TalkTalk
breach is a prime example of how damaging this
vulnerability can be".
4. CROSS-SITE SCRIPTING (XSS)
Case Study: The 2018 British Airways Data Breach
In 2018, British Airways' website and mobile app were
compromised, leading to the theft of financial data from
approximately 380,000 customers. The attack was carried
out by injecting malicious code into the website, a classic
example of a cross-site scripting attack [21].
Sample Q20 Dialogue for Cross-Site Scripting:
Agent: Briefly describe the incident you are
experiencing
User: When a user visits our website, malicious popups appear and some pages automatically redirect to
other websites. (Text input)
Agent: Does this attack involve injecting malicious
scripts into a trusted website?
User: Yes.
Agent: Is the malicious script executed in the victim's
web browser?
User: Yes.
Agent: Can this attack be used to steal session cookies
or hijack user accounts?
User: Yes.
Agent: Does this attack rely on manipulating serverside database queries?
User: No.
Agent: Does it typically involve direct access to
backend systems or file servers?
User: No.
Agent: Is this attack often carried out through
vulnerable input fields, comment sections, or URL
parameters that fail to properly sanitize user input?
User: Yes.
Agent: The attack you are thinking of is Cross-Site
Scripting (XSS).

Generated Explanation: "Cross-Site Scripting (XSS)
is a type of security vulnerability typically found in web
applications. XSS attacks enable attackers to inject
client-side scripts into web pages viewed by other users.
The British Airways breach demonstrated how XSS can
be used to skim payment card information in real-time."
V. DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK
The "Learning to Explain Cybersecurity through a Q20
Game" framework offers several advantages over
traditional cybersecurity education methods:
Engagement: The gamified approach fosters active
participation and makes learning more enjoyable.
Adaptability: The RL agent can tailor the line of
questioning to the user's level of understanding.
Explainability: The framework provides clear and
contextualized explanations based on interactive dialogue.
Scalability: The knowledge base can be continuously
expanded to include contemporary and evolving
vulnerabilities.

While the methodology outlined by Wu [2] serves as
the foundation for this study, the proposed LTE
framework introduces an attention-based explainability
mechanism for implementation. The most logical and
synergistic module to integrate with the existing RL policy
in the Q20 game is an Attention-based mechanism,
specifically leveraging the Information Gain/Probability
Jump concept inherent in the original framework. While
SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local
Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) are
powerful post-hoc feature attribution methods - both
primarily providing local interpretability, with SHAP
further enabling aggregated global explanation, an
Attention-based approach offers the advantage of being
inherently integrated within the RL agent’s decisionmaking process. This integration ensures that the
generated explanations are both contextually meaningful
and pedagogically relevant to the game’s interactive flow.
The proposed Attention-based explainability module
will be integrated into the policy-based RL framework to
improve transparency and interpretability in the agent’s
decision-making. By dynamically highlighting key
question-answer interactions, the attention mechanism
will support dialogue-level interpretability, allowing for a
clearer understanding of the reasoning process behind the
agent’s actions.

However, the framework has its limitations. The
quality of the learning experience largely depends on the
detail and accuracy of the knowledge base that provides
contextual explanations to learners. In an ideal system, the
knowledge base would pre-store all types of cyber threats
with extensive data storage. Additionally, developing a
strong and well-trained RL agent requires significant
computational resources and training data.

This article introduces a novel framework that
combines 20 Questions (Q20) and Learning-to-Explain
(LTE) with cybersecurity, creating an interactive and
explainable cybersecurity training tool. By using the Q20
model, this approach transforms the learning experience
into an interactive conversation where users, guided by a
question-based game environment, identify and
understand various cyber threats. This method enhances
user engagement and provides meaningful, context-aware
explanations of complex cybersecurity concepts. The
paper details the design of the framework, demonstrates its
practical operation in justifying complex attack vectors
(through case studies), and explains the underlying
principles of RL that optimize the question-asking process.
The system design offers a scalable, practice-oriented
approach to cybersecurity education.
By carrying the Q20 gaming format, this method
makes education an engaging conversation. Users can
identify and understand different cyberthreats within a
game environment. Along with improving user
engagement with cognitive retention, this approach
provides clear, relevant explanations of complex
cybersecurity ideas.
Initially, the process begins with the user briefly
articulating a concept and then engaging the agent by
asking what it is thinking of. The average number of

questions required for convergence during self-play
simulations, before the Q20 agent successfully identifies
the cyberthreat type and presents the generated
explanation to the player, is determined by the policybased Reinforcement Learning (RL) agent, which
functions as a diagnostic cyber-threat identifying
mechanism.
For future research, the game-based learning
framework will undergo a pilot evaluation to substantiate
its effectiveness with empirical data. As part of the
extended work, accuracy will be introduced as an
additional metric to assess user acceptance and learning
outcomes within this explainable, game-based
cybersecurity learning environment.
