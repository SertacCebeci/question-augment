Abstract
Large language models are now widely used for everyday learning, but the underlying interactions are typically unstructured chats rather than following a curriculum.
Unlike formal online learning systems, these interactions carry no prior record
of the student, so any estimate of what the student already knows must be inferred from the dialogue itself. We show that this gap is not closed by scaling
models alone. Frontier and education-tuned LLMs perform poorly when asked to
tutor a student over an extended session, because doing so requires three things
at once. The tutor must sequence a curriculum, conduct Socratic dialogue, and
infer the student’s knowledge state from that dialogue. We propose separating
these responsibilities. Given a student query, our system constructs a prerequisite
knowledge graph in which subtopics are nodes and dependencies are edges, and
frames tutoring as deciding which node to teach next and how many dialogue turns
to spend on it before moving on. A lightweight PPO policy handles this sequencing
decision, while an LLM conducts the Socratic exchange at the chosen node and
returns a signal of student progress. Across held-out STEM and non-STEM topics,
our PPO-paired tutor outperforms heuristic baselines, frontier general-purpose
models, and a model specialised for Socratic dialogue — on both the rate at which
students reach full curriculum mastery and the number of turns required. Explicit
curriculum structure delivers gains that scaling the underlying model does not.

We study this in the setting of self-directed learning in the wild, where a user initiates a session with
an LLM chat interface and asks a question about something they wish to learn about, with no syllabus,
no instructor-designed sequence, and no prior record of the student. The only signal available is the
dialogue itself, and a good tutor must use it to do two things at once: deliver content in an order
that respects prerequisite relationships between concepts, and figure out where the student currently
stands so that content can be adapted rather than delivered as a fixed lecture.
A growing body of work studies how to make LLMs better teachers. Systems like LearnLM
[LearnLM Team, 2024] improve the quality of individual concept explanations, making delivery
more pedagogically sound and responsive to learner needs, but treat each interaction as self-contained.
They do not reason about which concept to teach next, nor maintain a model of what the student has
and has not yet mastered across a session. A parallel line of work trains models to elicit reasoning
from learners rather than supply answers [Dinucu-Jianu et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2024]. This addresses
how content is exchanged but again leaves curriculum structure unaddressed. Much of this effort has
been evaluated on mathematical problem solving [Zerkouk et al., 2025], where success is legible
because the student either solves a held-out problem or does not. This framing sits uncomfortably
with the self-directed setting for two reasons. First, open-ended subjects, such as the humanities, do
not reduce to a final numerical answer [Garcia and Weilbach, 2023, Favero et al., 2024]. Second, and
more fundamentally, math benchmarks do not require the tutor to reason about curriculum structure
or infer student state from dialogue alone, since the problem itself supplies the scope.
To address this, we adopt Socratic tutoring, in which the tutor probes understanding through questions
rather than direct exposition. This surfaces misconceptions, prompts students to construct their own
reasoning [Shridhar et al., 2022], and generates the kind of open-ended exchange that self-directed
learning actually looks like — across subjects, not just those with clean numerical answers.

Given a student query, our system constructs a prerequisite knowledge graph in which the query sits as
the goal node and supporting concepts sit as ancestors. Curriculum delivery becomes a graph traversal
problem where a tutoring policy decides which node to teach next, with the aim of maximising
mastery of the goal. Deciding what to teach next and actually teaching it require different kinds of
reasoning, and thus we treat them as separate problems. The sequencing problem is addressed by a
lightweight policy trained with reinforcement learning (RL), while the dialogue problem is handled
by an LLM that conducts the Socratic exchange at each selected node and returns a mastery estimate
the sequencing policy uses to decide its next move. For scalability, we use an LLM as a student
simulator; our focus is on LLM tutoring behaviour in unstructured settings and its ability to exploit
structure when provided. We evaluate this design across a mix of STEM and non-STEM topic graphs,
and find that the learned sequencing policy paired with a tutoring LLM outperforms both heuristic
baselines and LLM-only tutors, including frontier general-purpose models and models specialised for
Socratic dialogue. An overview is provided in Figure 1.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We formulate informal LLM-based education as a new task, in which an arbitrary student
query is decomposed into a prerequisite knowledge graph and a tutor must guide the student
through it using only dialogue as evidence of their state.
• We release a Gymnasium-compatible environment [Towers et al., 2025] for this task, covering both STEM and non-STEM domains and supporting evaluation over long horizons,
beyond the short, math-focused interactions of prior work.
• We propose a two-component system that separates curriculum sequencing from dialogue
generation, with a lightweight policy for node selection and an LLM tutor for Socratic
instruction.
• We show that frontier and tutoring-specialised LLMs struggle on this task when used alone,
and that a lightweight Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) policy paired with a tutoring
LLM closes the gap.

2

Related Work

2.1

Tutoring Language Models

A growing line of work trains or prompts LLMs to act as tutors over math problems. These efforts
share a common structure: a tutor guides a student through a single problem via dialogue, often with
RL [Dinucu-Jianu et al., 2025, Wei et al., 2026], and is rewarded by some combination of pre/post
solve-rate gains on simulated students and handcrafted pedagogical qualities such as withholding
answers, recognising incorrect responses, or producing good explanations [Liu et al., 2024, Scarlatos
et al., 2025]. Sessions are typically capped at 16 to 20 turns, reflecting the single-problem framing
[Nam et al., 2025].
This setup works well when learning can be reduced to solving a verifiable problem, but it constrains
the tutor’s role in two ways that matter for everyday learning. The reliance on a verifiable correct
answer rules out domains where understanding is open-ended, and the short horizon rules out the kind
of extended sessions that deeper learning requires. Our setting removes both constraints. We tutor
over arbitrary topics drawn from STEM and non-STEM subjects, where success cannot be reduced to
solving a specific problem, and we evaluate over horizons of one hundred turns to reflect the longer
arc of self-directed study.
LearnLM [LearnLM Team, 2024] is a large pre-trained model that incorporates high-quality pedagogical dialogue into its training data, evaluated via expert preference comparisons against other models
on pedagogical qualities [LearnLM Team, 2025]. Evaluation of LearnLM centres on subjective expert
judgement and does not directly probe whether the model can perform tasks essential for tutoring
success, such as curriculum tracking and estimating a student’s knowledge state. Worden et al. [2026]
reports that frontier models perform poorly on several pedagogical subtasks, including cognitive state
modelling and comparing question difficulty, suggesting that subjective preference scores may not
translate into the structured competencies tutoring requires. Our work contributes here by making
curriculum tracking and competency estimation explicit objectives, and by evaluating current models,
including LearnLM, on their ability to deliver content under them.

2.2

Knowledge Graphs, Knowledge Tracing, and Path Recommendation

A second line of work uses knowledge graphs to encode prerequisite and topical relationships between
learning content [Doroudi et al., 2019, Chang et al., 2015, Siren and Tzerpos, 2022], and pairs them
with Knowledge Tracing (KT), the task of predicting a student’s correctness on future items given
their history of past responses [Piech et al., 2015, Lee et al., 2026]. More recent systems combine KT
with RL to recommend the next item or learning path that maximises predicted gain, and some have
begun integrating LLMs into the pipeline, for instance to label items with knowledge components
from text alone [Ozyurt et al., 2025].
These approaches assume a setting we do not have. They build on interaction logs from online
learning platforms (e.g. Worden et al. [2026], Chang et al. [2015]), which presumes the student
is a returning user on a fixed item pool, and they reduce assessment to binary correctness on item
responses, which discards the information carried in the content of a student’s reply [Liu et al., 2019].
Both assumptions break in our setting. Students arrive with no prior log, the curriculum is constructed
at query time, and the only diagnostic signal available is the dialogue itself. These differences
motivate a tutor that can construct curricular structure on demand and reason about student state from
natural language rather than from logged item responses.
2.3

Foundations from the Learning Sciences

Three findings from educational psychology shape our system’s design. First, tutoring systems that
require students to reason through intermediate steps, rather than produce final answers, approach the
effectiveness of expert human tutors [VanLehn, 2011], which motivates our use of Socratic dialogue
over direct exposition. Second, the order in which content is sequenced determines whether students
have the prerequisite knowledge to engage constructively with new material [Chi and Wylie, 2014],
which is why we treat curriculum sequencing as a core problem. Third, formats that require students
to generate responses under uncertainty improve long-term retention compared to more passive
instructional formats [Bjork and Bjork, 2011], and evidence that structural sequencing decisions
operate largely independently of conversational modality [Litman et al., 2006] supports treating the
two as separable components.

3

Problem Setup

Problem Setting. Effective tutoring requires resolving two distinct dimensions simultaneously.
The first is curricular: A complete understanding of any topic draws on subtopics that depend on one
another in specific ways, and a tutor must enumerate those subtopics and the prerequisites between
them. The second is diagnostic: The tutor must assess what the student already knows.
Consider a student who queries q = "I want to learn more about tariffs and sanctions". An economics
student may grasp comparative advantage and trade balances yet struggle to connect them to sanctions
as a geopolitical instrument. A business owner familiar with tariff schedules and supply chain
disruptions may lack the theoretical grounding to reason about their macroeconomic origins and
effects. The two students share the same query but require different sequences of instruction.
An LLM tutor encountering an arbitrary student resolves the curricular dimension naturally, drawing
on knowledge encoded during pretraining to enumerate the subtopics relevant to q and the dependencies between them. The diagnostic dimension is harder. A student’s knowledge state is private and is
revealed only through dialogue. A curriculum that ignores this proceeds in a fixed order, re-teaching
material the student already knows and bypassing the work needed to identify genuine gaps.
We frame the tutor’s task as allocating a budget of dialogue turns across the subtopics in the curriculum,
with more turns spent where the student has the most to learn and fewer spent where competence is
already in place. We use Socratic dialogue as the mode of interaction, we treat curriculum sequencing
as a learned decision, and we separate the sequencing decision from the dialogue itself.
Knowledge graph. Given a query q, a language model produces a directed acyclic graph G = (V, E)
that represents the curriculum. Each node v ∈ V is a subtopic required to fully understand q, and
each directed edge (u, v) ∈ E indicates that u is a prerequisite of v. The graph has a single root
r ∈ V corresponding to q itself, and leaf nodes L ⊂ V corresponding to foundational concepts with
no prerequisites. In the running example, r is the topic of Tariffs and Sanctions, an interior node
4

might be Diplomacy, and a leaf might be International Relations. We write pre(v) for the direct
prerequisites of v and anc(v) for its full ancestor subgraph. Mastery of v requires mastery of every
node in anc(v). The graph is constructed once at the start of the episode and is fixed for the remainder
of the interaction.

4

Method

We propose a tutoring system built from two complementary components that each address one of
the demands the tutor faces. A learned sequencing policy addresses the curricular problem, observing
summary statistics of the student’s response history and the structure of the prerequisite graph to
decide which subtopic to engage with next. A language model addresses the dialogue problem,
conducting the Socratic exchange at the selected subtopic, generating a question, engaging with
the student’s free-form reply, and producing a binary judgement of whether the reply demonstrated
understanding. That judgement is fed back to the sequencing policy as the signal it uses to choose the
next subtopic. The two components are developed independently and combined only at inference
time, which allows us to train the sequencing policy in fast simulation against a student model without
any task-specific training of the language model’s weights.
4.1

Student Model

The student model describes the student in relation to the generated curriculum, and is structured to
mirror the response models used in KT, which predicts the probability that a student will answer a
question on a knowledge component correctly given their history of past responses [Piech et al., 2015].
Rather than fitting parameters to observed students, we treat the response model as a generative
simulator with a fixed parametric form and use it to train the sequencing policy.
Each node v is associated with a latent threshold representing the number of interactions the student
needs at v before achieving mastery, a single parameter that represents both the intrinsic difficulty
of the topic and the strength of the student’s existing foundations. After n interactions at v, the
probability of a positive response is

4.2

Tutoring as a POMDP

The setup above defines a sequential decision problem under uncertainty. The tutor chooses which
node to teach at each step, observes a noisy response, and must update its belief about hidden mastery
thresholds. This is naturally a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP; Sondik [1971]),
and we adopt that formalism here. The full specification, including the precise observation vector and
transition rules, is given in Appendix C.1.
State. The true state at each step is the student’s mastery across all nodes in the knowledge graph,
together with how much successful practice they have accumulated at each node. Formally, mastery
mv ∈ {0, 1} at a given node transitions from unmastered to mastered (mv = 1) once the student’s
cumulative successful interactions nv meet a required threshold kv , i.e. mv = 1[nv ≥ kv ]. This
threshold is never observed by the tutor, so even a complete record of the student’s interaction history
leaves genuine uncertainty about whether any given node has been mastered.
Actions. At each step, the tutor selects a node at ∈ V to teach next. In the running example, an
action is a choice between asking the student a question about supply and demand, trade balances, or
sanctions as a geopolitical instrument.

evidence of their knowledge state. The true mastery state is not directly accessible, thus, as a human
teacher does, the tutor must infer it through dialogue. We model this by composing the observation
from the student’s utterance together with summary statistics of the response history at each node
(e.g. number of positive responses) and the graph structure G. The graph structure allows the tutor to
reason about how evidence at one node implicates its ancestors and descendants.

4.3

RL Tutor

The RL tutor is a policy πθ trained with PPO [Schulman et al., 2017] on the POMDP described in the
previous section. Training is performed entirely in simulation, with student responses yt generated
from the binary stochastic model in Equation 1 and no language model in the loop. PPO requires
many environment interactions to converge, and rolling these out through a language model across
long conversation horizons would be prohibitively expensive, so we treat the binary simulator as a
sufficient proxy for the structural problem to support training and account for the resulting sim-to-real
gap at evaluation time.
To learn a policy that generalises across topics and students rather than memorising a single curriculum, we apply Domain Randomisation [Tobin et al., 2017]. The core idea is to expose the policy to a
wide distribution of environment configurations during training so that the resulting policy is robust
to conditions not seen during training. At the start of each episode, three features are resampled:
1. Topic and graph. A topic is drawn from a pool spanning STEM and non-STEM domains,
and a knowledge graph G is constructed for it. This varies the prerequisite structure the
policy must traverse. The full list of topics can be found in Appendix E.
2. Difficulty thresholds. For each node v ∈ V, the threshold kv is sampled independently
from a Poisson distribution with mean k̄. The policy does not observe kv and must infer
proximity to mastery from the response history.
3. Prior knowledge. A mastery initialisation parameter ρ ∈ [0, 1] controls the student’s
existing competence. Each node is independently marked as mastered (with kv = 0 and
mv = 1) with probability ρ, producing students that range from complete novices to those
with most of the curriculum already covered.
Item 1 exposes the policy to different topics and items 2 and 3 expose it to different students, requiring
it to probe for prerequisites, interpret the response signal, and allocate turns where they yield the
largest gains in mastery.
Inference with LLMs. At inference time, the trained policy πθ is composed with a language model
to produce a fully interactive tutoring system. The interaction proceeds turn by turn. Given the
current observation ot , the RL tutor policy selects the next node at = πθ (ot ) to teach. The language
model then carries out the actual instruction at node at , generating a Socratic question grounded in
the selected subtopic and engaging with the student’s free-form reply.
Since the student, during inference, responds in natural language rather than emitting a binary signal,
the response yt that the policy expects is no longer directly available. We obtain it from an LLM
judge that reads the student’s reply and returns a positive or negative assessment. That assessment
is fed into the observation update and used to advance the environment, after which the RL policy
chooses the next node. For all our experiments, the LLM tutor and judge are the same models.

Experiments

5.1

Experimental Setup

We evaluate on a held-out set of 10 topics spanning STEM and non-STEM domains (see Table E),
disjoint from those used during training. Each topic has a fixed knowledge graph G and per-node
thresholds {kv }v∈V , sampled once and held constant across all conditions so that performance
differences reflect policy quality rather than problem difficulty. Each topic is evaluated over 30
episodes for non-LLM baselines, 5 for open-source LLMs, and 3 for closed-source models (Gemini,
GPT-5). The smaller counts for closed-source models reflect API cost, and the resulting confidence
intervals are correspondingly wider. We provide examples of the conversations in Figure 4c.
Baselines. We organise methods along two factors that together define the design space of our
experiments, summarised in Table 1 and categorised along two factors as described below.
The action scope factor specifies what the method controls. Under BFS, the visitation order is fixed
in advance by traversing G in breadth-first order from the leaf, and the policy’s only decision at each
step is whether to continue on the current node or advance to the next one, representing methods that
follow a fixed syllabus. Full-scope methods are unconstrained and may revisit or interleave nodes.
The observation factor specifies the signal the method receives, which is either the ground-truth
mastery state (GT), the latent binary response yt (Binary), or a natural-language student utterance
(NL). The Binary and NL conditions share the same underlying student engine and differ only in
whether yt from Equation 1 is exposed directly or rendered as a natural-language reply by Qwen2.514B-Instruct [Dinucu-Jianu et al., 2025] and recovered by an LLM judge. Any performance gap
between the two is therefore attributable to the language-and-judgement layer rather than to a different
environment. Heuristic baselines and our sequencing policy alone are evaluated under Binary, while
LLM-only methods and our full system are evaluated under NL.
LLM-based methods are evaluated on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [Qwen, 2025], TutorRL [Dinucu-Jianu
et al., 2025], gemini-3-flash-preview, and gpt-5-mini-2025-08-07. ThinkingKT [Lee et al.,
2026] serves as a representative method from the KT literature, adapted for the current environment.
Metrics. The primary metric is success rate, the proportion of episodes in which all nodes in G are
mastered within the step budget T = 100. We also report graph mastery, the average proportion of
nodes mastered across all episodes including failed ones, which captures partial progress when the
budget is exhausted before full mastery. And we report average steps to mastery, computed over all
episodes, as a measure of efficiency.

5.2

Main Results

Table 2 reports performance across all methods. We highlight four findings.
LLM-only policies fail on the joint task. LLM-only policies, including frontier models, perform
worse than both random and heuristic baselines, and ThinkingKT performs below random. This
is not a failure to understand the environment, since the LLM oracle variants of the same frontier
models reach near-optimal performance once given access to the ground-truth mastery signal. The
failure lies in performing three tasks at once, namely sequencing the curriculum, conducting Socratic
dialogue to deliver content, and inferring the student’s state from that dialogue, and replicates earlier
findings that frontier models are no better than random on pedagogical tasks [Worden et al., 2026].
KT methods fare no better, since they were designed for settings with rich prior interaction logs and
fixed item pools, neither of which our setting provides.
Separating sequencing from dialogue narrows the oracle gap. Within the Full and NL conditions,
decomposing the task into a learned sequencing policy paired with a small LLM instructor outperforms
every monolithic LLM-only method evaluated under the same conditions. Both PPO + TutorRL and
PPO + Qwen2.5 substantially exceed the LLM-only and heuristic baselines, despite the LLM in each
case being a 7B-parameter model. The deployable system closest to the oracle upper bound is one
that decomposes the task rather than one that uses a more capable model.
Performance degrades modestly under natural language. Holding the sequencing policy fixed
at PPO and varying the observation factor from Binary to NL, success rate drops from 93.3% to
between 78.0% and 82.0%, depending on the dialogue LLM. The drop reflects noise added by the
language-and-judgement layer, since both observation conditions share the same underlying engine.
The remaining gap to the oracle is nonetheless considerably narrower than for any other deployable
system, indicating that most of the difficulty in the joint task comes from the control problem rather
than from the language layer.
Education-oriented models are slightly stronger dialogue partners. In both the LLM-only
and PPO + LLM conditions, models with known exposure to education-related training slightly
outperform general-purpose models at equivalent scale. Gemini outperforms GPT-5-mini, and
TutorRL marginally outperforms Qwen2.5. The differences fall within confidence intervals and we

5.3

Policy Behaviour Analysis

Figure 3 illustrates the sequencing behavior of PPO across a sample episode. PPO adopts a localised,
BFS-like traversal: it focuses on a single branch, exhausts the leaf nodes of a subtopic, and only then
advances to the subtopic node itself before moving on. This yields a structured progression in which
prerequisite concepts are consistently covered before their parent topic.

Conclusion and Limitations

We formulated open-ended LLM tutoring as curriculum traversal over a query-conditioned prerequisite graph, and showed that our two-component tutoring system, with a lightweight PPO policy
9

for sequencing and an LLM for Socratic dialogue, outperforms both heuristic baselines and frontier
LLM tutors. The main failure of LLM-only tutors is not topical knowledge but the joint demand of
sequencing, dialogue, and assessment over long horizons. Promising next steps include extracting
richer mastery signal from student utterances and distilling structural tutoring behaviour back into the
language model.
Limitations. The LLM-generated graphs in our experiments stayed within our cap of Nmax = 20
nodes, and whether the method scales to larger and finer-grained curricula, where the state and action
spaces grow accordingly, remains open. All student responses are generated synthetically by an
LLM-based student, and remain to be validated with human learners. Higher-fidelity student models
[Doroudi et al., 2019, Tio et al., 2025] are a natural avenue for strengthening the simulator in future
work.

A.1

Motivation

In a bid to optimize models for Socratic qualities, we ran a small scale experiment that uses end-to-end
reinforcement learning finetuning to instill these qualities directly into a model. The goal of this
experiment is to test whether a generic instruction tuned model can be shaped into a Socratic tutor
purely through reward signals, without relying on the curriculum knowledge graph that we use
elsewhere in this work. We view this as a natural baseline to compare against our main approach,
since end-to-end RL is a common recipe in recent tutoring work and it lets us probe what behaviors
emerge when the model is left to discover its own dialogic strategy.
14

A.2

Simulator Setup

We reuse the student simulator described in our main experiments (see Section 5.1), but we strip out
the curriculum knowledge graph so that the model has to organize the conversation on its own. The
Socratic qualities we target are taken from the arena-style evaluation introduced by the LearnLM team
[LearnLM Team, 2025], who define what a good tutor looks like across a small set of pedagogical
dimensions. We adopt their framing because it gives us a concrete and externally validated target for
what we want the trained model to do.
In this simplified simulator, we assume that the student has a single objective query, which corresponds
to one node in the curriculum graph used in the main body, and that the student has an innate kv value
associated with that node. The model has two broad moves available to it during a conversation. It can
break the current concept down into a more foundational concept, or it can advance the conversation
by using the prior dialogue as context to discuss a higher level concept. This mirrors the upward and
downward navigation that a human tutor would naturally do when probing what a learner already
knows.
A.3

Training Procedure

We train the tutor model with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [Shao et al., 2024], using
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the policy that interacts with the student simulator. For the reward signal, we
use Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct as a judge model that reads the full conversation and assigns a reward of
1.0 for each of the following pedagogical qualities that the conversation exhibits, namely Manages
Cognitive Load, Inspires Active Learning, Deepens Metacognition, Stimulates Curiosity, and Adapts
to the Learner. The total reward for a conversation is the sum across these five qualities, which means
a perfect conversation receives a reward of 5.0.
We chose a judge based reward over a hand designed reward because the qualities we care about are
hard to capture with simple heuristics. A larger judge model also gives us a smoother training signal
than the policy could provide for itself, and it follows the same recipe that recent works on RL with
LLM judges have used.
A.4

Qualitative Findings

We provide a qualitative visualization of the conversations generated by our finetuned model, the
base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and LearnLM in Figure 4. Looking at these conversations side by side
makes it easy to see how each model handles the same student query and how their styles diverge.
We discover that the finetuned Qwen2.5-7B has collapsed into reward hacking behavior. In particular,
the model leans heavily on a small set of repeated openers such as “That’s great to hear! You
mentioned that you’re. . . ” across almost every turn. This gives the tutor a very artificial quality and
we deem it ill-suited for the kind of natural conversation we would like to see between tutors and
students. In informal reads of the transcripts, the tutor feels formulaic rather than responsive, and we
find that this does not provide an engaging experience for the student. For these reasons we refrain
from using this model in deployed systems.

A second issue cuts across more than just our finetuned model. Earlier works like SocraticLM [Liu
et al., 2024] and TutorRL [Dinucu-Jianu et al., 2025], as well as our finetuned model in this case, are
optimized to encourage the student and are punished for producing discouraging comments. As a
concrete example, the reward favors phrases like “That’s a good try” over phrases like “That’s not
correct.” While this push toward warmth is well intentioned, it creates a side effect that we think is
worth flagging.
We observe an elevated rate of what we call Agreeable Inaccuracies, where the tutor affirms a
factually wrong statement from the student rather than correcting it. A simple example would be the
tutor responding with “That’s a good insight! You mentioned that ice freezes at 32C!” even though
the student has stated something that is not true (Water freezes at 0C or 32F). The encouragement
objective ends up overriding the correctness objective, and the model takes the path of least resistance
by agreeing.
To measure how often this happens, we use GPT-4.1 as a judge to read conversations from each of
the models in question and to flag turns where the tutor either affirms or fails to correct a factual error
from the student. We present the results in Table 3. The picture is mixed once we look at both error
rate per 1,000 words and the absolute number of errors together, since a low rate over a long corpus
can still translate into many incorrect affirmations in practice, while a high rate over a short corpus
may reflect only a handful of slips. For example, our Finetuned model has the lowest rate but still
produces 40 total errors over its corpus, and SocraticLM has both a high rate and the largest absolute
count at 83 errors, so we cannot cleanly claim that any single tutor model is safe on this axis. We
raise Agreeable Inaccuracies as a safety concern for deployed education systems, since a tutor that
systematically confirms wrong answers can entrench misconceptions rather than fix them, and we
flag a more careful study of this trade off as future work.

1. Claims
Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the
paper’s contributions and scope?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: We take concerted efforts to introduce the problem and share our proposed
ideas through experimentation and empirical results.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims
made in the paper.
• The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the
contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A [No] or
[N/A] answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers.
• The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how
much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings.
• It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these goals
are not attained by the paper.
2. Limitations
Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: This is covered in section 5.5.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper has no limitation while the answer [No] means
that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.
• The authors are encouraged to create a separate “Limitations” section in their paper.
• The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to
violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings,
model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors
should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the
implications would be.
• The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was
only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often
depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.
• The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach.
For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution
is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be
used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle
technical jargon.
• The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms
and how they scale with dataset size.
• If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to
address problems of privacy and fairness.
• While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by
reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover
limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best
judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an important role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers
will be specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations.
3. Theory assumptions and proofs
Question: For each theoretical result, does the paper provide the full set of assumptions and
a complete (and correct) proof?
Answer: [N/A]

Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include theoretical results.
• All the theorems, formulas, and proofs in the paper should be numbered and crossreferenced.
• All assumptions should be clearly stated or referenced in the statement of any theorems.

4. Experimental result reproducibility
Question: Does the paper fully disclose all the information needed to reproduce the main experimental results of the paper to the extent that it affects the main claims and/or conclusions
of the paper (regardless of whether the code and data are provided or not)?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: We provide the code, hyperparameters in supplementary materials and in
appendix. To the best of our abilities, we write the process into the main body of the text.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
• If the paper includes experiments, a [No] answer to this question will not be perceived
well by the reviewers: Making the paper reproducible is important, regardless of
whether the code and data are provided or not.
• If the contribution is a dataset and/or model, the authors should describe the steps taken
to make their results reproducible or verifiable.
• Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways.
For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully
might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may
be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same
dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often
one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed
instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case
of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are
appropriate to the research performed.
• While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submissions to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the
nature of the contribution. For example
(a) If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how
to reproduce that algorithm.
(b) If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe
the architecture clearly and fully.
(c) If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should
either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce
the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct
the dataset).
(d) We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case
authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility.
In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in
some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers
to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.

7. Experiment statistical significance
Question: Does the paper report error bars suitably and correctly defined or other appropriate
information about the statistical significance of the experiments?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: We use CI to describe our empirical results.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
• The authors should answer [Yes] if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence
intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the
main claims of the paper.
• The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for
example, train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall
run with given experimental conditions).
• The method for calculating the error bars should be explained (closed form formula,
call to a library function, bootstrap, etc.)
• The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).

• It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error
of the mean.
• It is OK to report 1-sigma error bars, but one should state it. The authors should
preferably report a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis
of Normality of errors is not verified.
• For asymmetric distributions, the authors should be careful not to show in tables or
figures symmetric error bars that would yield results that are out of range (e.g., negative
error rates).
• If error bars are reported in tables or plots, the authors should explain in the text how
they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.

• The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied
