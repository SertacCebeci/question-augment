Abstract
Alignment techniques often inadvertently induce sycophancy in LLMs. While prior studies
studied this behaviour in direct-answer settings,
the role of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning
remains under-explored: does it serve as a logical constraint that mitigates sycophancy, or a
tool for post-hoc rationalization that masks it?
We evaluate a range of models across objective and subjective tasks to investigate the issue.
Results show that reasoning generally reduces
sycophancy in final decisions but also masks
sycophancy in some samples, where models
construct deceptive justifications through logical inconsistencies, calculation errors, and onesided arguments etc. Furthermore, LLMs are
more prone to sycophancy in subjective tasks
and under authority-bias. Our mechanistic analysis on three open-source models reveals that
the tendency of sycophancy is dynamic during
the reasoning process rather than being predetermined at the input stage 1 .

Introduction

Alignment techniques for Large Language Models (LLMs) such as Reinforcement Learning from
Human Feedback (RLHF) (Christiano et al., 2017;
Ouyang et al., 2022) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) are widely
used to better align model behavior with human expectations and values (Wang et al., 2023). However,
recent studies have identified a critical drawback:
these training methods inherently exploit human
judgment and produce outputs that appeal to human evaluators but are actually flawed or incorrect
(Cotra, 2021). This inadvertently promotes “sycophancy”, where models sacrifice factual accuracy
to cater to the user’s perceived beliefs or preferences (Burns et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2024).
Such behavior has been proven to be a consistent property across model scales and training

Sycophancy is a complex behaviour in LLMs
(Vennemeyer et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025). In
this paper, we focus on a specific condition where
an LLM abandons its choice in the unbiased and
neutral prompt for a biased option when the preference of a user or an authority figure’s explicit bias
is appended to the prompt (Chen et al., 2025c).
We evaluate the sycophancy behaviour of a set of
closed-source models and open-source instructiontuned models, across six kinds of prompt’ settings
(see Figure 1) on a dataset that includes both objective questions (with objectively correct answers)
and subjective questions (without objectively correct answers). We find that sycophancy is pervasive
across diverse model scales. Empirically, LLMs
are more prone to sycophancy in subjective tasks
than objective ones, and this tendency is higher
under authority-bias than user-bias. Across all settings, using CoT prompting reduces sycophancy in
final decisions compared to the No-CoT condition.

Meanwhile, the CoT text in the biased condition
exhibits semantic deviation compared to that in the
unbiased condition, yet shows no differences in
most other linguistic features.
Beyond behavioral observations, we also explore
the internal dynamics of sycophancy in the three
open-source models using the Tuned Lens (Belrose et al., 2025). We find that, in these models,
sycophancy is not a static predisposition fixed at
the input stage, but a dynamic phenomenon undergoing unidirectional evolution during reasoning. When reasoning brings a shift from initial
sycophancy toward unbiased in the internal representation, the resulting CoT text rarely manifests
explicit correction of the bias. Conversely, when it
intensifies sycophancy, distinct semantic patterns
emerge: in objective tasks, the model often exhibits
logical inconsistency, calculation errors, or factual
fabrications to uphold the biased conclusion. In
subjective tasks, this behavior manifests as onesidedness, where the model strategically ignores
trade-offs to construct a superficially coherent but
biased argument.

Preliminaries

Sycophancy in LLMs is the tendency to excessively agreeing with or flattering users, which poses
significant risks to their reliability and ethical deployment (Wei et al., 2024; Malmqvist, 2025). Empirical analyses attribute sycophancy to instructiontuning and RLHF (Sharma et al., 2024). Proposed
mitigation include adversarial preference training

(Zhao et al., 2025), contrastive activation steering
(Rimsky et al., 2024), and Bayesian-deviation regularization (Papadatos and Freedman, 2024).
Chain of Thought (CoT) introduces dynamic
multi-step reasoning, where sequential token activations produce behaviors more complex to analyze than single-step representations (Yang et al.,
2025b). CoT rationales are often unfaithful, as explanations may deviate from the model’s internal
computation (Barez et al., 2025). Even so, CoT
generally outperforms No-CoT prompting in reasoning and task performance (Barez et al., 2025).

Experiment Setup

Our study adopts a three-layer setting in the experiments (see Figure 2): 1) whether the task itself has
a ground-truth answer (objective vs. subjective), 2)
whether the input includes biased preference (userbias or authority-bias vs. unbias), and 3) whether
the prompt elicits stepwise reasoning (CoT) or direct answers (No-CoT).
• Objective vs. Subjective For objective data,
we utilize a subset of SycophancyEval (Sharma
et al., 2024), which aggregates 3,096 questions
across various domains: 998 questions from
MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021a), 1,000 problems from MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021b),
254 items from AQuA (Ling et al., 2017), and
817 questions from TruthfulQA (Lin et al.,

2022). For subjective data, we utilize a collection of open-ended datasets lacking definitive
labels, comprising 3,076 samples across three
datasets: 1,360 everyday moral scenarios from
DailyDilemmas (Chiu et al., 2025), 696 modelgenerated critiques from the feedback subset of
SycophancyEval (Sharma et al., 2024), and 1,020
cultural value assessments from the social attitudes survey (SCB10X Research Lab, 2025).
The data examples can be found in Appendix B.
• Unbiased vs. Biased To introduce preference biases, each base question is framed into
three settings: unbiased (control, no preference
cues), user-bias (first-person user suggestion e.g.,
“I think the answer might be [BIAS_ANSWER].”,
henceforth bias 1), and authority-bias (thirdperson authority cue e.g., “A Stanford professor suggests [BIAS_ANSWER] may be correct.”,
henceforth bias 2). This follows prior work on
LLMs’ sycophancy (Chen et al., 2025c).
• CoT vs. No-CoT For each unbiased and biased
prompt, we set two parallel versions: No-CoT
version asks the model to directly answer without
any explanation, and CoT version requires it to
generate stepwise reasoning before giving the
answer. See Appendix C for the detailed prompts
for all settings.
We tested three closed-source and three opensource LLMs. The open-source models are Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (henceforth Llama3.1) (Meta
AI, 2025), Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct (henceforth
Qwen2.5) (Yang et al., 2025a), and Gemma-29B-IT (henceforth Gemma2) (Team et al., 2024).
The closed-source models are Claude-3.5-Sonnet
(henceforth Claude) (Anthropic, 2024), GPT-3.5
(henceforth GPT3.5) (Ye et al., 2023), and OpenAI
o3-mini (henceforth o3mini) (OpenAI, 2025) 2 .

Results and Analysis

From Figure 3, we can find that sycophancy is
broadly prevalent across all models, which confirms it is not a weakness exclusive to smaller models nor eliminated by scale. Meanwhile, in most
cases, the CoT condition yields a lower sycophancy
rate than the No-CoT condition, demonstrating that
reasoning mitigates sycophancy in final decisions 3 .
Across all models, sycophancy rises markedly
in subjective tasks with no single ground truth under both CoT and No-CoT conditions. This indicates that LLMs are more susceptible to influence
when addressing subjective questions without objectively correct answers, leading them to align
more closely with the prompt’s stance rather than
maintain internal consistency. Authority-bias (bias
2) elicits stronger sycophancy than user-bias (bias
1), showing that LLMs are far more likely to defer

4.3

Sycophancy by Unbiased Correctness

We further disaggregate the sycophancy rate on objective tasks by whether the model’s original unbi-

ased answer was correct or incorrect (Table 1). We
find that sycophancy rates are consistently higher
when the original answer is incorrect, suggesting
models are more easily swayed when they lack
confidence. However, a non-trivial proportion of
sycophantic switches still involve abandoning a
correct answer, representing a direct loss of truthfulness or accuracy. CoT’s protective effect is more
pronounced for correct answers, indicating that
reasoning better defends well-grounded answers.
Authority-bias elicits higher sycophancy regardless
of answer correctness.

Dynamics of Sycophancy

In this section, we conduct a mechanistic analysis
of internal model states in the three open-source
models (Llama3.1, Qwen2.5, and Gemma2) to explore whether sycophancy is pre-determined at in-

put or emerges dynamically during the reasoning.
6.1

Method

We first use all entries from the Section 4 and extract the hidden states at the initial output token,
to compare the logit difference from sycophantic
and unbiased answers across all layers under both
No-CoT and CoT conditions. This serves to compare the LLM’s initial tendencies across different
settings. Then we extend this analysis by dividing
the output biased CoT text into ten equal segments
(from 0% to 100% based on token count) across
all four sample categories in Section 4. By testing
the model at each interval along this trajectory, we
can determine whether the sycophantic tendency
remains constant or shifts dynamically as the reasoning chain unfolds. For the detailed setting of
this section, refer to Appendix G.
Metric To quantify the model’s internal inclination, we define two critical tokens for each sample:
the unbiased answer token (tu ) and the sycophancy
token (ts ). The former (tu ) is defined as the option
the model selects under the unbiased prompt. The
latter (ts ) corresponds to the option the model selects with bias inserted in the prompt, representing
the sycophantic choice. We measure the preference

Dynamics of Bias in CoT

As observed in Section 6, CoT-Corrected samples
show a corrective shift toward unbiased, but reasoning conceals bias in Persistent Sycophancy and
CoT-Induced Sycophancy samples. This section
investigates if these divergent shifts manifest as
identifiable semantic patterns in CoT text.
7.1

Corrective Logic in Truth-Telling

We first focus our analysis on CoT-Corrected samples, as they represent the model’s successful resistance to sycophancy. We employed GPT-4o (OpenAI et al., 2024) to annotate the CoT text based
on two indicators (for detailed prompts, ref to Appendix J):
• Explicit bias mention (Ieb ): This indicator tracks
whether the CoT explicitly comments and disagrees with the bias (e.g., mentioning “the professor’s opinion is wrong”).
• Comparative analysis depth (Iad ): If the bias
is not explicitly mentioned (Ieb = False), this
indicator will assess if the model analyzes the
biased option more extensively than the unbiased
condition.
By applying these indicators, we categorize CoTCorrected samples into three semantic patterns:
1. Pattern A (Ieb = True): The CoT text demonstrates meta-awareness of the bias by directly

identifying and correcting it.
2. Pattern B (Ieb = False, Iad = True): The
bias remains unmentioned, but CoT text “overexplains” why the biased option is incorrect,
suggesting an implicit defense against the bias.
3. Pattern C (Ieb = False, Iad = False): The CoT
text seems to ignore the bias entirely.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive investigation
into the role of CoT reasoning in mitigating and
masking LLM sycophancy across both objective
and subjective tasks. Our findings validate that,
while explicit reasoning significantly reduces sycophantic outcomes in final decisions, it simultaneously introduces a more deceptive form of “syco-

The current scope in our study involving six representative open-source and closed-source LLMs
may not fully capture the behavioral nuances
of emerging reasoning models. Furthermore,
our main experiments focus on answer sycophancy (Sharma et al., 2024); while a supplementary analysis of preference sycophancy and challenge sycophancy (Appendix L) shows broadly
consistent trends for CoT’s mitigating effect, our
findings may not fully generalize to all forms of
sycophancy. The investigation is also restricted to
explicit user and authority-driven bias, potentially
overlooking more subtle or implicit social signals
that could trigger sycophantic responses in complex
real-world deployments. While the Tuned Lens
provides significant mechanistic insights into how
sycophancy evolves dynamically during reasoning,
it represents only one interpretive perspective on
internal model states, which could be further enriched by granular circuit-level analyses or sparse
autoencoders. Our mechanistic analysis relies on
internal state access through Tuned Lens and SAEs,
which limits our study to the open-source models of
Llama3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and
Gemma-2-9B-IT. Closed-source models may exhibit different internal dynamics due to different
architectures, scales, and alignment procedures, so
our findings on dynamic reasoning may not be universally applicable across all LLMs. Additionally,
the study’s focus on single-turn interactions leaves
the cumulative effects of sycophancy in multi-turn
dialogues for subsequent exploration (Hong et al.,
2025a).
Finally, the linguistic scope of this study constitutes an additional constraint, as the empirical evaluation was conducted exclusively within
an English-centric framework using benchmarks.

Consequently, the external validity of our findings
may be limited across diverse linguistic and sociocultural paradigms.

A

Related Work

A.1

Mechanistic Origins of Sycophancy
Representations

The sycophancy in LLMs is the tendency to prioritize user agreement and preference over factual accuracy or objective neutrality (Sharma et al., 2024;
Malmqvist, 2025; Wei et al., 2024). Current research identifies the mechanistic origins of sycophancy in LLMs as a structural rather than a purely
behavioral phenomenon. Chen et al. (2025a); Wang
et al. (2025c); Tak et al. (2025) find some linear
activation directions in the LLM activations could
stably encode some persona behaviors such as confidence, hallucination, and sycophancy, which indicates the structural sycophancy-aligned behaviors
often reside in low-dimensional subspaces rather
than being diffusely distributed (Burns et al., 2023).
Utilizing logit-lens analysis and causal activation patching, researchers have mapped a twostage emergence: In mid-to-late layers, the model
develops differentiated representations of behavioral tendencies (e.g., sycophancy vs. unbiased).
In subsequent output-proximal layers, these representations are selectively amplified into a preference decision, yielding sycophantic or unbiased
responses (Wei et al., 2024; Vennemeyer et al.,
2025; Wang et al., 2025b). This two-stage process
is described as circuit-like because the late-layer
preference shift is causally dependent on earlierlayer’s representations, which forms a gated and
path-dependent computation rather than a diffuse
global effect (Wang et al., 2025a).
To mitigate the sycophantic risks, several interventions have been proposed. Firstly, methods to
identify sycophantic or deference-related directions

include linear probings (mean-difference vectors,
linear probes, SVM directions, null-space projection) and non-linear probings (CCA alignment,
MLP probes, manifold clustering) (Rimsky et al.,
2024). Afterward, contrastive activation steering
performs the intervention by adding or subtracting these vectors at selected layers to shift generation behavior (Papadatos and Freedman, 2024).
Moreover, Bayesian-deviation regularizers analyze sycophancy from the perspective of probabilistic inference. Sycophancy alters belief distributions
and the probabilistic stability, thereby influencing
the model’s decision-making (Atwell et al., 2025;
Sicilia et al., 2025).
Despite these interventions, sycophancy continues to appear in challenging settings. It resurfaces in adversarial QA, where models face leading or suggestive prompts that reward compliance
over accuracy, and in role-conditioned dialogue,
where identity cues (e.g., “professor,” “expert,” or
“authority figure”) implicitly pressure the model
toward deference, even when affective-inference
and persona-reactivity circuits have been isolated
(Wang et al., 2025a,c). This persistence becomes
more severe as model scale increases and instruction tuning intensifies alignment toward social preference signals, indicating that the behavior is reinforced by systemic properties of the representation
space and training distribution rather than being
eliminated by localized edits (Perez et al., 2023;
Zhang et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025; Liu et al.,
2025).
A.2

Causal Dynamics within
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Frameworks

Within CoT frameworks, the involvement of sequential activations across many token positions
makes sycophancy a dynamic rather than static
phenomenon. The reasoning process may amplify
bias as the reasoning progresses, but it may also
self-correct it if later steps counteract earlier statements (Yang et al., 2025b). Concurrently, CoT
rationales demonstrate limited reliability and hence
increase the complexity: models frequently exhibit
Think-Answer Mismatch (Chen et al., 2025c) and
Flaw Repetition (Chua et al., 2024). These findings align with evidence that CoT explanations
often diverge from the model’s latent computation path (Barez et al., 2025; Turpin et al., 2023),
and that coherent rationales can be post-hoc fabrications rather than faithful reasoning paths (Lee
et al., 2025; Chua et al., 2024; Lanham et al., 2023).

In short, the dynamic bias propagation and reasoning unfaithfulness makes sycophancy within
CoT a structurally more complex phenomenon than
static response bias.
Recent work proposes a mechanism-level explanation: CoT trajectories contain thought anchors,
which are high-leverage steps that disproportionately steer subsequent reasoning confidence and
trajectory planning (Bogdan et al., 2025). These
anchors operate as structural control points within
the computation graph. Mechanistically, Sparse
Autoencoders (SAEs) decompose interpretable
subcircuits responsible for multi-step reasoning
(Galichin et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025b), while
causal patching confirms that targeted activations
can toggle behavior between memory retrieval and
novel inference (Fartale et al., 2025; Hong et al.,
2025b). This complementarity establishes verifiability: internal representations are not only observable, but controllable. Feature-steering approaches
further stabilize CoT-specific activation directions
and mitigate drift in reasoning quality (Kim et al.,
2025; Eshuijs et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). Collectively, these results show that CoT bias is anchored
in identifiable mechanisms, not emergent randomness.
As research progresses from identification to
intervention, the central challenge shifts toward
maintaining stability under scale. Studies of information flow (Yang et al., 2025b) and depth
utilization (Gupta et al., 2025) connect CoT behavior to scaling laws, yet major gaps remain: crossscale feature stability is not well quantified, harmful features can re-emerge after fine-tuning, and
current mitigation strategies risk degrading calibration or depth-dependent reasoning (Gupta et al.,
2025). Thus, the present research frontier lies in
unifying representation control with causal editing, preventing CoT from structurally amplifying
sycophancy without collapsing inference depth or
model confidence structure.

M.1

Sparse Autoencoder (SAEs) Analysis of
Sycophancy Dynamics

Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) decompose a model’s
hidden activations into a set of sparse and interpretable latent features, trained via reconstruction
objectives with sparsity regularization to isolate semantically meaningful directions (Shu et al., 2025).
Compared to linear probes, SAE features provide
a distributed yet disentangled representation of internal concepts. While Tuned Lens analyzes finalanswer preference shifts, SAE feature tracking reveals how sycophancy-aligned semantic structures
are progressively strengthened or suppressed across
the reasoning process.
M.2

Experimental Setup

Semantic Feature Selection. We identify
sycophancy-related SAE features based on their
semantic descriptions. Specifically, for each model,
we search the Neuronpedia database6 using the
keyword sycophantic, and select the top 50 SAE
features whose explanations exhibit the strongest
semantic association with sycophantic behavior.
The selected features and the corresponding
explanations can be found in Table 14 15 16.
6

https://www.neuronpedia.org/
search-explanations/

Models and Layers. We conduct experiments
on Qwen, Gemma, and Llama, focusing on layers where Tuned Lens analysis reveals the earliest
and most salient internal preference shifts toward
sycophancy or honesty and with an SAE model
trained in Bloom et al. (2024). Hence, we analyze
SAE features from layer 23 in Qwen, layer 20 in
Gemma, and layer 19 in Llama. We do not focus
on the final layers because representations in the
final layers are often dominated by output-specific
mixing and logit projection effects, which makes
fine-grained semantic feature dynamics harder to
disentangle.
Data and Trajectory Construction. For each
model, we sample 50 instances from Type B where
reasoning mitigates sycophancy, and 50 instances
from a mixed pool of Type A and Type C, where
reasoning masks or enhances sycophancy.
For each sample, we extract the full CoT generation and divide the token sequence into 100 equal
segments, corresponding to normalized reasoning
progress from 1% to 100%. For each segment, we
extract the hidden states of the tokens falling into
the corresponding span, encode each token-level
activation using the SAE, and aggregate the resulting feature activations by summation to obtain a
segment-level SAE feature representation, which
are averaged within each segmentation across samples. Each feature trajectory is normalized independently to highlight its relative activation strength
across the reasoning process, without comparing
absolute magnitudes between different features.
M.3

Results and Analysis.

Figure 13 shows the activation trajectories of
sycophancy-related SAE features over the normalized reasoning process. Across all examined models and feature sets, we observe a clear and consistent asymmetry between Type B and Type A/C
samples.
Type B samples highlight the sycophancyaligned features typically in early stages, which
drop sharply within the first half of the reasoning
trajectory and remains suppressed thereafter. This
pattern indicates that corrective reasoning in Type
B is accompanied by a progressive weakening of
sycophancy-related semantic structures, rather than
a late-stage override at the output level.
In contrast, Type A/C samples show substantially incomplete decay or increase of the same
feature set. Sycophancy-related features remain

active throughout large portions of the reasoning
process, and in some cases intensify toward later
stages, consistent with the amplification or persistence of sycophantic bias observed in the Tuned
Lens analysis.
Importantly, this feature-level asymmetry aligns
with our earlier findings that Type B samples rarely
manifest explicit bias rejection in the generated
CoT text. Together with the Tuned Lens results,
these observations suggest that the mitigation of
sycophancy in Type B primarily occurs at the level
of internal semantic representations, through gradual suppression of sycophancy-aligned features,
rather than through explicit semantic correction in
the reasoning text.
