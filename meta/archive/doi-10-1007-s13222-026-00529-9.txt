Learning from Annotator Disagreement Via Weighted Ensemble
Optimisation for Subjective Text Classification

Abstract
Subjective text classification tasks, such as abuse detection and stance analysis, often suffer from high levels of annotator
disagreement. Conventional approaches typically collapse these disagreements into a single ground truth, thereby discarding
valuable supervision signals. We propose MO-WEL (Multi-Objective Weighted Ensemble Learning), a novel framework
that explicitly leverages annotator disagreement by jointly optimising ensemble weights and size under multiple objectives.
Candidate predictors are trained on diverse label projections obtained through random sampling or annotator-specific
selection, and ensemble weights are optimised with respect to three complementary losses: F1 score, cross-entropy and
Manhattan distance, alongside a regularisation term. Experiments on four benchmark datasets (ConvAbuse, HS-Brexit,
MD-Agreement and ArMIS) show that MO-WEL consistently outperforms strong baselines in accuracy, calibration, and
distributional alignment. A case study further demonstrates that MO-WEL produces predictions that balance majority
correctness with minority annotator perspectives, yielding interpretable and reliable outputs. Our findings highlight the
importance of modelling annotator diversity and suggest ensemble optimisation as a principled means of incorporating
disagreement into subjective NLP tasks.

Keywords Subjective Text Classification · Annotator Disagreement · Ensemble Learning · Multi-objective Optimisation

tasks frequently exhibit substantial annotator disagreement.

2 Related Work
Annotator disagreement in subjective NLP tasks is now
widely understood as a meaningful signal rather than noise.
Majority-vote aggregation suppresses this signal by erasing minority viewpoints [4], whereas recent work preserves
disagreement to improve model robustness and fairness [5].
treat each annotator as a separate prediction task, and [3]
demonstrate that modelling disagreement improves uncertainty estimation in hate speech detection. While these approaches highlight the importance of disagreement, they
typically require explicit annotator identifiers or structured
multitask settings. This gap motivates RQ1: How can models capture meaningful disagreement without relying on annotator identities or dedicated multitask architectures?
Perspectivist frameworks argue for preserving multiple
valid interpretations rather than collapsing them into a single gold label [7]. emphasise that subjective tasks should
encode distributions of viewpoints, and AART [8] embeds
annotator identities to protect minority perspectives. However, these methods depend on dense annotator metadata
or identity-aware architectures, which may not generalise
to settings where annotations are sparse, crowd-sourced,
or anonymous. This limitation leads to RQ2: How can we

model perspectivist supervision when annotator-level metadata is unavailable, incomplete, or unreliable?
Ensemble methods have been explored to model heterogeneous annotator behaviour in subjective NLP tasks [2].
show that ensemble predictors outperform single models,
while FLEAD [9] extends this idea to federated settings
across languages and datasets. However, these approaches
typically optimise a single loss function, most commonly
cross-entropy (CE) with respect to a soft target distribution
derived from annotator frequencies [6], or more recently
distance-based objectives such as average Manhattan Distance (MD) to align predictions with continuous or aggregated annotator scores [10]. Although Euclidean distance is
a common metric for comparing probability vectors, prior
disagreement-aware work favours MD (`1 ) because it is less
dominated by large deviations and more sensitive to classwise discrepancies, a property that is particularly important
in subjective settings where minority annotator viewpoints
may otherwise be overshadowed by majority-class effects.
Nevertheless, both CE and MD are typically applied in isolation, and existing ensemble methods do not jointly optimise for complementary desiderata such as hard-label accuracy, probabilistic calibration, and faithful preservation
of annotator disagreement. This raises RQ3: Can a single
ensemble be optimised to simultaneously balance multiple
annotator-aligned objectives rather than only one?
The limitations across these lines of work motivate the
need for a framework that: (i) captures disagreement without annotator meta information (RQ1); (ii) preserves perspectivist information when metadata is sparse (RQ2); and
(iii) explicitly balances multiple complementary objectives
in subjective prediction (RQ3). MO-WEL addresses these
gaps by formulating ensemble construction as a multi-objective optimisation problem, jointly considering accuracy,
calibration, and distributional alignment with annotator disagreement. This allows the framework to navigate tradeoffs that earlier approaches do not explicitly model, providing a principled way to leverage annotator disagreement in
subjective NLP.

3.4 Multi-Objective Optimisation
Subjective NLP tasks inherently require balancing multiple
desiderata: (i) high accuracy on majority labels, (ii) calibrated probability estimates reflecting uncertainty, and
(iii) explicit modelling of annotator disagreement. Rather
than collapsing these into a single objective, MO-WEL
adopts a multi-objective optimisation scheme for ensemble weights w and the ensemble size M to target each
requirement independently. This design directly addresses
RQ1 and RQ2, which ask whether combining complementary objectives improves both predictive performance and
perspectivist alignment.

jointly optimise weights and ensemble size. DE efficiently

as selecting subsets of weak predictors while remaining
lightweight and model-agnostic.

4 Experimental Setup



4.1 Datasets
We evaluate Multi-Objective Weighted Ensemble Learning (MO-WEL) on the LeWiDi 2023 shared task dataset
[16], which covers multiple languages, genres, and text
lengths, providing a strong benchmark for assessing generalisability. The dataset comprises four corpora.
Three corpora are based on social media posts collected
from X. ArMIS contains Arabic posts annotated for misogyny. HS-Brexit includes English posts labelled for hate
speech in the context of Brexit. MD-Agreement consists
of English posts annotated for offensiveness across domains
such as Black Lives Matter, Elections, and COVID-19. We
ignore domain metadata and treat these posts uniformly
during training.
The fourth corpus, ConvAbuse, contains English dialogues between users and conversational agents. Each utterance is annotated on a 5-point abuse scale ranging from –3
(highly abusive) to 1 (non-abusive). Following prior work,
we reduce this to a binary classification task: utterances
with severity < 0 are labelled as offensive, and all others
as non-offensive. Dialogues are flattened into single input
sequences.
All corpora are preprocessed by removing HTML tags,
URLs, user mentions, punctuation, digits, non-ASCII characters, and redundant whitespace. Table 1 summarises the
datasets, including language, genre and annotation properties.

4.2 Evaluation Metrics
To enable fair comparison with prior work, following [16]
and [10], we adopt both discrete and distributional evaluation metrics.
Micro-F1 (F1): measures overall classification accuracy
across classes.
 Cross-Entropy Loss (CE): the negative log-likelihood
of the gold labels under predicted distributions, capturing
calibration and confidence.


Manhattan Distance (MD): the average `1 distance
between predicted probabilities and gold distributions,
quantifying distributional divergence.

F1 reflects correctness of discrete predictions, while CE
and MD assess the quality of probabilistic outputs.

Each weak predictor is trained under the joint objective in
Eq. 2, which combines three complementary signals: the

5.7 Case Study
To illustrate the practical implications of our framework,
we present two short case studies from the ConvAbuse
and HS-Brexit datasets, both of which exhibit substantial
annotator disagreement.
The first example (Table 8) shows a conversation dialogue labelled by three annotators: two marked it as abusive and one as non-abusive. Such disagreement is typical
in abuse detection, where perceptions vary with tone, intent, and cultural context. We compare three modelling approaches: (a) MV, which collapses labels into a single hard
target; (b) Soft-CE, which learns from soft distributions
but does not optimise ensemble structure; and (c) WELRandom, our disagreement-aware ensemble.
As shown, MV ignores minority views and produces an
overly confident prediction, while Soft-CE captures some
uncertainty but remains biased toward the majority. WELRandom provides a more calibrated prediction with strong
MD alignment, demonstrating its ability to preserve annotator diversity while remaining accurate.
A second example from HS-Brexit (Table 9) highlights
disagreement driven by sarcasm, a common source of ambiguity in political discourse. Annotators diverged on whether
the remark expresses hostility or simple dismissal. Here,
MV again over-commits, and BERT-CE reflects only partial ambiguity. In contrast, MO-WEL aligns closely with
the full annotator distribution, producing a moderated-confidence prediction that better captures the contested nature
of the example.

6 Conclusions
This paper introduced MO-WEL, a multi-objective ensemble optimisation framework for subjective text classification. Unlike conventional approaches that reduce annotation
disagreement to a single label, MO-WEL leverages diverse
annotator perspectives by training weak predictors on different label projections and optimising ensemble weights
across multiple objectives. Extensive experiments across
four datasets demonstrated that MO-WEL consistently improves predictive accuracy, calibration, and alignment with
annotator distributions, with complementary benefits from
different label selection strategies. Ablation studies revealed
the distinct contributions of each objective, while regularisation further enhanced robustness. A case study illustrated
how MO-WEL produces outputs that preserve annotator diversity, thereby supporting interpretability in sensitive domains such as online abuse detection.
Future work will explore extending MO-WEL to multilabel and multilingual settings, as well as integrating annotator metadata to better model systematic biases. More
broadly, our results underscore the value of embracing,
rather than suppressing, annotator disagreement in order
to build NLP systems that are both accurate and socially
aware.
