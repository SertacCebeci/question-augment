Abstract
Multi-agent role-playing has recently shown promise for studying social behavior with language agents, but existing simulations are mostly monolingual without cross-lingual interaction, an essential property of real societies.
We introduce MAWM, the first Multilingual Agent-based World Modeling
framework that supports multi-turn multilingual interactions among generative agents with diverse sociolinguistic profiles. MAWM enables two
modes of analysis: (i) global public opinion modeling, which tracks how
attitudes toward open-domain survey questions evolve across languages
and cultures, and (ii) media influence and information diffusion, via autonomous news agents that dynamically generate content and shape user
behavior. To ground the simulation in realistic population distributions,
we construct the MAPS benchmark, which combines survey questions and
demographic personas drawn from global population distributions. Experiments on simulation alignment, along with social science case studies on
cultural assimilation and normative diffusion, show that native-language
simulation better reflects real survey data than English-only simulation,
and that the agent society in MAWM reproduces established sociocultural phenomena and highlights the value of multilingual simulation as an
alternative interpretable tool for computational social science.

Introduction

Cross-cultural attitudes on global issues are shaped by multilingual discourse, yet how
opinions form and shift across linguistic boundaries remains difficult to study with traditional surveys that capture only static snapshots (Yoshino et al., 2015; Haerpfer et al.,
2022). Simulating such dynamics with LLM-powered agents offers a complementary path,
especially as the operating language of a model shapes its cultural cognitive style (Lu et al.,
2025), making multilingual simulation essential for socially grounded modeling.
Role-playing enables language agents to adopt specific personas or roles during multi-turn
interactions (Chen et al., 2024). When scaling up to multiple role-playing agents, it becomes
increasingly feasible to simulate aspects of human societies (Anthis et al., 2025). A pioneering
example is Smallville (Park et al., 2023), which simulated a small town populated by 25
generative agents. This work was later extended in Park et al. (2024), where 1,052 digital
representatives were modeled based on qualitative interviews. More recently, OpenClaw1
lets users create digital agents of themselves to socialize on Moltbook2 . These initiatives
rest on the assumption that if agents can take human memory and emulate human behavior, they
may serve as practical proxies that complement real participants in social science research. More
broadly, these efforts point toward agent-based world modeling of social interaction (Ding

Building on this premise, tremendous efforts have been made in applying agent-based
world modeling for diverse downstream societal studies: (1) uncovering social patterns and
group behaviors (Zhu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), (2) interpreting social phenomena (Liu
et al., 2024; Tsirmpas et al., 2025), (3) exploring social theories (Dai et al., 2024; Ashery et al.,
2025), and (4) forecasting policy outcomes (Li et al., 2024c; Yan et al., 2024). These use cases
offer a cost-effective complement to traditional social experiments, which typically depend
on collecting human data through extensive surveys, interviews, and field studies.
Against this backdrop, two significant gaps can be identified in studying cross-cultural
dynamics between the agent-based simulacra and real human communities. (1) Crosslingual attitude dynamics remain unstudied in simulation. Prior studies mainly focus
on cross-cultural role-playing for data synthesis (Li et al., 2024b; Ki et al., 2025), but they
neglect fine-grained agent behaviors that evolve across different languages, which constitute
a pivotal component of human societies. (2) Limited sociolinguistic diversity of simulated
environments. Most simulations take place in monolingual or culturally homogeneous
settings (Zhou et al., 2024b; Hou et al., 2025), restricting the emergence of diverse interaction
patterns and overlooking social dynamics inherent to multilingual communities. As a result,
multilingual social simulations remain largely underexplored, despite their significance for
scalable, quantitative analysis of global social phenomena that are difficult to observe in
traditional real-world studies or controlled survey experiments.
In light of these gaps, we propose the first Multilingual Agent-based World Modeling
(MAWM) framework, designed for societal world modeling of multi-turn interaction among
generative agents with diverse sociolinguistic profiles. Unlike prior monolingual simulations, MAWM allows agents to converse with, influence, and react to one another across
languages. MAWM supports (1) global public opinion analysis by simulating how attitudes toward open-domain survey topics evolve across languages and cultures. Starting
from any survey question, the framework emulates user behavior on social platforms and
aggregates users’ responses via voting, offering a scalable and controllable alternative to
global surveys (Yoshino et al., 2015; Gedeshi et al., 2022). In addition, MAWM provides (2)
media influence and information diffusion analysis by incorporating news agents that act
as media organizations. Unlike agents in previous simulations that depend on predefined
interventions (Li et al., 2024c; Hou et al., 2025), these agents generate content conditioned
on institutional profiles and evolving discourse, facilitating controlled studies of how media
pierces, or reinforces, the filter bubbles that emerge in cross-cultural information networks.
To operationalize MAWM, we construct the Multilingual Agent Perspective Survey
(MAPS), a benchmark combining survey questions from GlobalOpinionQA (Durmus et al.,
2024) with demographic personas derived from the World Values Survey (Haerpfer et al.,
2022), spanning 50 countries and 28 languages across geographically diverse regions.
Our evaluations, covering simulation alignment and case studies on cultural assimilation
and normative diffusion, demonstrate that multilingual simulation with native-language
agents estimates attitudinal patterns more closely aligned with real survey data than Englishonly simulation, consistent with evidence that language shapes cultural cognition in LLMs
(Lu et al., 2025). Furthermore, MAWM can be used to reveal interpretable cross-cultural
phenomena, including filter bubble formation, asymmetric media influence, and differential
convergence rates that monolingual simulations cannot capture.
In summary, the primary contributions of this work are as follows:
• As our primary contribution, we introduce MAWM, a multilingual agent-based world
modeling framework that situates multi-agent society simulation within a global context.
By enabling agents to communicate across languages and cultures, the framework addresses a fundamental blind spot in existing social simulations and provides a controlled
environment for studying cross-cultural dynamics at a comprehensive global scale.
• We construct MAPS, an open-domain dataset that combines survey questions with user
personas. This establishes a foundation for cross-cultural simulations and structured
analysis of multilingual agent behavior across diverse cultural contexts.

Related Work

Multi-Agent Simulation and Societal World Modeling LLM-based multi-agent systems
show notable capabilities in handling complex problems (Zhang et al., 2024) and sophisticated cooperation (Mandi et al., 2024). Compared to a single agent, scaling multiple agents
introduces more interactions and variables beyond isolated decision-making. This has
led to world simulation as a natural application area, including society simulation (Park
et al., 2023), public administration (Xiao et al., 2023), and strategic games (Xu et al., 2025).
Such frameworks extend the agent-based modeling tradition in the social sciences (Gao
et al., 2024), creating virtual settings for examining social phenomena, such as the art of
war (Hua et al., 2024) and resource governance (Piatti et al., 2024). However, most work
assumes monolingual and culturally homogeneous environments, leaving simulations of
multilingual societies largely unexplored. Even the closest attempt, CulturePark (Li et al.,
2024b), focuses on data generation rather than analysis of social dynamics. Existing surveys
frame such simulation-based approaches as agent-based world modeling (Ding et al., 2025),
where social simulacra complement real-world social computing systems through a world
modeling process. Zhang et al. (2025) explicitly adopt this framing with a user pool of
10 million real-world individuals, while Zhou et al. (2025) formalize the representation of
evolving social states and mental dynamics within such systems. Our work extends this
direction to multilingual and cross-cultural social simulation settings.
Cross-Cultural LLMs LLMs have made strong advancements in multilingual NLP, as
shown by improvements in translation and cross-lingual question answering (Huang et al.,
2025). These developments build on recent multilingual LLMs (Xue et al., 2021) and multilingual embedding models (Wang et al., 2024a; Enevoldsen et al., 2025). Nonetheless,
multilingual proficiency does not directly lead to cross-cultural understanding (Rystrøm
et al., 2025), which requires models to handle diverse norms, values, and practices. In
fact, prompting the same model in different languages yields measurably different cultural
cognitive styles and social orientations (Lu et al., 2025), as language encodes culturally
grounded reasoning patterns that monolingual simulation cannot activate. Cross-cultural
LLMs therefore aim to represent, reason about, and adapt to cultural contexts, not just process different languages. Prior studies show measurable cultural bias toward English-centric
value profiles in frontier models (Rao et al., 2025; Myung et al., 2024), introduce benchmarks
for cultural awareness (Wang et al., 2024c; Chiu et al., 2025), and propose training or datasynthesis methods that improve cultural alignment (Nguyen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a).
Despite these developments, cross-cultural LLM research remains largely single-agent,
while little work integrates cultural modeling with multi-agent social simulations.

MAPS Dataset

In line with prior practices (Hou et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), which pair real individuals
and survey instruments for agent-based simulations, we form the Multilingual Agent
Perspective Survey (MAPS) dataset by pairing (1) user settings sampled from multicultural
populations with (2) social research questions of broad global concern.
To construct user settings across countries, we draw personas from the World Values Survey
(Haerpfer et al., 2022) given its breadth and scope. We retain eight independent attributes

religion, and social class, then map these demographic variable values to human readable
labels. For multilingual agent simulation, a native language is assigned to each interviewee
based on country, yielding the sociolinguistic personas shown in Figure 1(a).
Social research questions of the MAPS dataset are sourced from GlobalOpinionQA (Durmus
et al., 2024), derived from WVS (Haerpfer et al., 2022) and the Pew Global Attitudes Survey
(GAS) (Pew Research Center, 2024). We keep only opinion questions whose country cover3

The MAWM Framework

MAWM simulates multi-turn social interactions among user and news organization agents
across countries and tracks how attitudes evolve toward an opinion question, as illustrated
in Figure 2. In this system, both types of agents engage in iterative communication: news organizations express their stances on the survey question by generating news posts intended
to influence users, while users read recommended content, compose their own posts, and
vote to reflect their evolving attitudes toward the given survey question.

Environment

agents with personas and No news organization agents with profiles. The multilingual
recommendation system R projects both agents and posts of different languages into a
unified embedding space using a Sentence Transformer model (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019)
to provide semantic post recommendations. Each agent’s embedding is initialized from
its self-introduction and updated each round via exponential moving average. Posts are
ranked by a score combining semantic relevance with recency decay, and the top-kr posts are
retrieved for each agent, excluding self-authored posts; at each round, posts are translated
into the agent’s language before delivery. Formal definitions are described in Appendix C.1.

a persona or profile, short-term memory M̂, long-term memory M̄, and a set of authored

User agents likewise create self-introductions, write posts to express their perspectives as
input to the recommendation system, and participate in a multiple-choice vote. In rounds
t ≥ 1, news organization and user agents first read recommended posts, then write new
posts for recommendation based on their memories and the content they read, and finally
user agents vote to update the aggregated country-level attitude distribution.
Each round, the user follows a fixed sequence of actions. During the warm-up round (t = 0),
the agent generates a self-introduction, creates the first post, and casts a vote. This stage
serves to initialize the agent’s public identity, generate content for the recommendation
system, and establish its baseline attitude toward the survey question Q.
In subsequent rounds (t ≥ 1), the user first reads previous posts from the recommendation
system R, forming long-term memories based on its understanding. Then the user agent
composes a new post reflecting its experience and analysis before participating in the voting
process to express its updated attitude toward the opinion question.
Memory Inspired by agent cognitive architectures (Sumers et al., 2024), we design shortterm memory within each action and long-term memory across rounds for user agents.
• Short-term Memory: Before each action, the user agent performs chain-of-thought reasoning (Wei et al., 2023) as part of its role-playing. Following Chain-of-Persona (Ji et al.,
2025), the agent completes three self-questioning steps before producing its final response.
The resulting questions and answers serve as short-term memory.
• Long-term Memory: From each reading posts action after round 0, user agents extract
weighted takeaways into long-term memory. The top-k m memories are retrieved by a
score combining importance weight and temporal decay, and incorporated into subsequent action planning each round. Full definitions are provided in Appendix C.1.
Actions Each user agent performs the following actions in the simulation.
• Self-introduction: At t = 0, user agents produce self-introductions following SOTOPIA
(Zhou et al., 2024b), which are incorporated into their personas.
• Read Posts: For t ≥ 1, the system R provides up to kr translated posts, from which the
agent extracts top-k m takeaways into long-term memory.
• Write Posts: Conditioned on persona and memories, each agent creates a post ptui .
• Vote: Following Zhou et al. (2024a); Hou et al. (2025), each agent outputs a probability
distribution over survey options, regularized by temperature softmax (Appendix C.1).

News Organizations

News organization agents in the MAWM framework role-play media entities on social
platforms, characterized by their editorial stance toward Q and their language. Similar to
user agents, news agents use short-term self-questioning and their recent post history as
long-term memory. At t = 0, each agent generates a self-introduction and an initial post. For
t ≥ 1, agents read recommended posts and write news in tandem, mimicking a newsroom

5

Experiments

5.1

Experimental Setups

As shown in Table 1, we conduct experiments on three cases from the MAPS dataset, where
the simulation initializes with 100 user agents, distributed across countries according to
population proportions. The case design targets two globally consequential topics with
strong cross-cultural variation. For Cases 1 and 2, we select Q201 on trade and domestic
wages, a topic of heightened relevance given that new tariffs quadrupled in 2024–2025,
covering 11.1% of global imports (World Trade Organization, 2025), and public attitudes
toward trade vary sharply across countries (Stokes, 2018). Case 1 pairs India, Japan, and the
United States to contrast Asian export-driven economies with an established Western market,
while Case 2 pairs South Korea, Brazil, and Peru to compare one Asian and two South
American economies with differing trade openness. For Case 3, we select Q278 on familydecided marriage, a gender equality issue, by pairing Zimbabwe and the Netherlands,
which represent opposite ends of the Gender Inequality Index, to study normative diffusion.
UNICEF (2023) estimates that, at the current pace, child marriage will take 300 years to end.
Each simulation runs for 20 rounds with an additional warm-up round. We test five
LLMs as agent backbones: GPT-5-mini, GPT-4o-mini, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Llama-4-Maverick,
and Llama-4-Scout. The multilingual recommendation system is implemented by
jina-embeddings-v3 (Sturua et al., 2024) and Google Translate. To reflect real-world social platforms, we ensure that each user is exposed to at least one news item in their timeline.
Implementation details and case statistics are detailed in Appendix C.1 and Appendix C.2.
5.2

Reliability Analysis of MAWM

We evaluate the reliability of MAWM from three perspectives: (1) real-world calibration,
which examines whether the simulation reflects social dynamics observed in the real world;
(2) global sensitivity, which tests whether the simulated society responds appropriately to
external signals; and (3) local consistency, which assesses whether individual agent behaviors align with their demographic settings, cultural backgrounds, and social characteristics.
Limitations of the current calibration and evaluation are discussed in Appendix A, and the
complementary robustness analyses are further reported in Appendix C.7.

almost entirely to Spanish-speaking countries; as a top-tier, Western-aligned language with
abundant English parallel data (Schwenk et al., 2021), Spanish behaves much like English,
so these small gaps are a weak test; the substantive signal lies in the large, consistent gains
observed for lower-resource, non-Western languages and their cultures.

responses for each action over 5 rounds. As shown in Figure 3(c), agents generally achieve
high response quality except for news creation; Appendix C.5 shows that news agents
tend to repeat their previous posts, reducing content diversity over rounds.
5.3

Case Study on Social Science

To showcase how MAWM can facilitate social science research, we conduct cross-cultural
studies as a direct application of the framework. Multicultural discourse is substantively
important yet challenging given the scarcity of suitable multinational data; MAWM helps
overcome this limitation. We aim to address two research questions in social science studies:
1) Does international cross-lingual communication help users from different countries converge in
their opinions on a given opinion question? (Cultural Assimilation) 2) Following international
interaction, can users or news organizations from source countries promote norms in target countries,
and which actor is more influential? (Normative Diffusion) Complete results and theoretical
interpretations of these dynamics across all three cases are provided in Appendix D.

5.3.1

Cultural Assimilation

In sociology, cultural assimilation (Treudley, 1964) refers to the process by which distinct
groups come to share a common culture. In our setting, we conceptualize assimilation
as cross-national convergence in agent attitudes on specific survey questions. Below, we
analyze cultural assimilation dynamics for the question on international trade and domestic
wages, focusing on two country groups. Both cases pair two geographically proximate
countries with one from a different region: Case 1 groups India and Japan (Asia) with the
United States, while Case 2 groups Brazil and Peru (South America) with South Korea.
This design allows us to examine how geographic and cultural proximity interacts with

• South Korea begins with a relatively high attitude score (∼0.8), but after global communication, its score falls to ∼0.3, lower than those of Brazil and Peru (∼0.4). This
indicates that South Korea ends up more supportive of free trade in the simulation than
before. Recommendation-flow analysis (Figure 6b, Appendix D) reveals that Brazilian
and Peruvian content, expressing similar pro-trade positions, accounts for 73.3% of South
Korea’s foreign recommendations in round 1, declining to 3.5% by round 8. This early
surge in exposure likely triggers the opinion shift, suggesting that initial dominance
of recommendations from countries with similar attitudes can strongly steer opinion
convergence in multilingual agent-based societies such as MAWM.
• Both South American countries (Peru and Brazil) exhibit rapid convergence within five
communication rounds. Each becomes the other’s largest foreign recommendation source,
consistent with mutual influence driving assimilation. The same filter bubble as in Japan
appears in Peru, whose foreign content exposure similarly declines over rounds.
Japan and South Korea thus reflect two distinct mechanisms rather than one implausibly
fast dynamic. Japan is not genuinely assimilated: with the highest inbreeding-homophily
index (Appendix D), its users are isolated by the recommender from the first round, its
with- and without-communication trajectories nearly coincide (Figure 5(a)), and its already
pro-trade initial stance makes a small self-contained drift resemble convergence, the classic
recommender echo-chamber effect (Pariser, 2011) rooted in homophily (McPherson et al.,
2001). South Korea instead converges through genuine exposure to similar-stance Brazilian
and Peruvian content, and such rapid shifts are realistic: the 2016–2017 Korean presidential

5.3.2

Normative Diffusion

Beyond cultural assimilation, cross-cultural communication can also produce normative
diffusion (Finnemore & Sikkink, 1998), the directional transfer of a behavioral standard from
a source population to a target population, reshaping the target’s beliefs, values, or policy
preferences through sustained social interaction and repeated exposure.
Case 3 turns to the diffusion of gender equality norms (Miho et al., 2024). We designate
Zimbabwe, ranked 153rd in the Gender Inequality Index (UNDP, 2025), as the target
country. Without interventions, Zimbabwean agents’ attitude scores range from about
1.5 to 1.7 (between Agree and Disagree on girls honoring family marriage decisions). We
then introduce the source nation, the Netherlands (ranked 8th in the Gender Inequality
Index), and compare two interventions: 1) five Dutch news organization agents supporting
option D (Strongly disagree), and 2) 50 Dutch user agents expressing the same stance while
directly interacting alongside the 100 Zimbabwean user participants.
The results in Figure 5 indicate that news organizations drive stronger normative shifts
than ordinary users. Dutch user posts make up less than 2% of Zimbabwean agents’ recommended content, showing a strong filter bubble restricting cross-country user exposure. In
contrast, news organizations have a larger impact, as the recommendation system emulates
real platforms by including at least one news post per round. Consequently, Zimbabwean
agents are exposed to Dutch news content and more likely to shift their attitudes in a
pro-equality direction, highlighting the media’s role in piercing filter bubbles. Notably, as
shown in Figure 9 in Appendix D, Dutch users’ attitudes remain consistent throughout
the simulation despite sustained exposure to 100 Zimbabwean users with opposing views,
confirming that the normative diffusion is asymmetric rather than mutual between the
Dutch source population and the Zimbabwean target user population.
Beyond static survey snapshots, two pieces of external evidence indicate that the simulated
trajectories reflect real-world change. First, real attitudes shift over time in the direction
our simulation moves them: across World Values Survey waves in Zimbabwe, agreement
that “men make better political leaders than women” went from a 52% majority (2001),
via 58% (2012), to a 45% minority (2020), and disagreement that “a university education is
more important for a boy than a girl” rose from 81% to 86%. Second, such change is driven
by information exposure, the mechanism our framework models (Jensen & Oster, 2009;
La Ferrara et al., 2012): internet penetration, treated as a quasi-natural exposure experiment,
is positively associated with gender equality across more than 50 countries (World Bank,
2024), significant after controlling for GDP per capita, urbanization, enrollment, and region
(β = 0.0014, p < 0.01). As no survey tracks these questions over time, we claim only the
direction and the driver of the simulated opinion change, not its exact rate of change.

6

Conclusion

In this work, we explore multilingual world modeling for social science, beginning with
the MAPS dataset, which integrates open-domain survey questionnaires with user demographics from global surveys. On top of this resource, we develop MAWM, which
models social-network interactions among cross-country user and news agents and outputs
attitude dynamics for survey questions. Our evaluation examines real-world calibration,
global sensitivity, and local consistency, evidencing its utility and internal consistency. We
further apply MAWM to three MAPS case studies, uncovering interpretable empirical
findings. These results underscore MAWM’s potential as a practical, complementary tool
for computational social science research across diverse linguistic and cultural settings.

Reproducibility Statement
The MAPS dataset is constructed from publicly available sources: user personas from the
World Values Survey Wave 7 (Haerpfer et al., 2022) and opinion questions from GlobalOpinionQA (Durmus et al., 2024), both available for non-commercial research use. The five LLMs
and recommendation system configurations are described in Section 5. Dataset construction
details and persona attribute definitions are provided in Appendix B. All hyperparameters
and implementation details of MAWM, including the memory scoring function, attitude
regularization, embedding update, and similarity ranking, are reported in Appendix C.1.
LLM-based evaluation prompts are listed in Appendix E. Since MAWM is a general framework, prompt templates and hyperparameters may need to be adapted for specific research
scenarios; practitioners should carefully calibrate these choices for their use case. The code
and data are available at https://github.com/magicgh/MAWM.
A related consideration is data contamination: the calibration targets derive from public
survey resources that may appear in LLM pre-training data. Contamination would require
the model to recall survey statistics rather than simulate populations, and three observations
argue against this interpretation. First, our primary contribution is the dynamic interaction
of multilingual agents, including round-by-round trajectories and cross-cultural assimilation, which are absent from survey data and therefore cannot be memorized. Second,
the aggregate endpoint distribution is never shown to the model: opinions are generated
independently for 100 persona agents and aggregated externally, making direct recall of
survey percentages implausible, and the observed agent-level variance further supports
genuine individual-level generation. Third, the model exhibits systematic responses to
interventions not contained in survey data, including media-induced opinion shifts and
assimilation under communication, which simple retrieval of memorized statistics would
not reproduce. Even the static endpoint behaves inconsistently with recall: alignment RMSE
varies markedly across models and between native and English prompting of the same
population, which retrieval of a single public statistic could not produce.

Ethics Statement
This work provides an alternative tool for social science research through agent-based world
modeling; no new human-subject data are collected, and all persona data are derived from
anonymized, publicly available surveys. The personas are role-played by large language
models, and their attitudes and behaviors reflect only simulated responses under specified
conditions; they should not be interpreted as empirical claims about real populations or
used to draw normative conclusions about underrepresented groups. Accordingly, any
applied or policy-relevant interpretation should be triangulated with empirical humansubject research. We recognize the dual-use potential of LLM-based agents and emphasize
transparency, methodological clarity, and responsible collaboration with domain experts.

A

Limitations

User Simulation In MAPS and MAWM, we emulate users with ten persona attributes and
a self-introduction through in-context learning and chain-of-thought reasoning, which are
inherently approximate simulacra of real-world users rather than faithful representations.
In-context prompting is a common practice in LLM-based agent simulation and is efficient
to scale up to millions of agents, since in social science research the persona configuration
changes with each study; alternative approaches such as fine-tuning or reinforcement
learning on user trajectory data are not cost-effective at this scale. Prior work suggests
that demographic attributes alone have limited power in aligning LLM role-playing with
real human opinions (Santurkar et al., 2023; Chuang et al., 2024); incorporating belief-level
information may improve persona fidelity, which we leave to future work. Additionally,
persona-to-opinion mapping operates at the country level rather than the individual level,
which may obscure within-country variation in attitudes. Since the aim of this paper is to
demonstrate multilingual societal world modeling, our simulations use 100 agents due to
computing resource limitations; scaling to larger populations is left to future work.
Social Networks The primary goal of this work is to demonstrate multilingual simulacra
as societal world modeling and present its potential applications for social science. To
this end, we provide a simplified multilingual social network that preserves fundamental
platform features. This abstraction is a deliberate methodological choice in line with both
major traditions of social modeling: agent-based modeling (Schelling, 1971; Axelrod, 1984)
follows the KISS principle (Axelrod, 1997) and the generative program (Epstein, 2006),
placing complexity in the results rather than the assumptions so that mechanisms can be
cleanly identified, while opinion-dynamics and network-formation models abstract communication even more aggressively, reducing persuasion to a weighted average of scalar
opinions (DeGroot, 1974), bounded confidence to a similarity threshold (Hegselmann &
Krause, 2002), and strategic network formation to a cost–benefit edge (Jackson & Wolinsky,
1996). Against this baseline, MAWM enriches rather than over-simplifies communication:
interaction is grounded in real multilingual text whose influence weights are generated
endogenously by the recommendation system, mirroring deployed production systems
such as X’s Grok-based recommender, which translates cross-language posts and ranks
them by predicted user interactions (xAI, 2026). Nevertheless, real communication involves
imperfect translation, and news media may selectively report or slant content; our claims
18

Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026

concern only the exposure structure, namely whether cross-cultural communication promotes convergence and the broad reach of news relative to dispersed peer posts, not the
naturalness of translation or the veracity of content. The news agent’s editorial stance is a
controlled experimental variable, and modeling media bias, selective reporting, and more
realistic social networks is left to future work beyond the present scope of the framework.
Calibration and Evaluation Our calibration compares simulated distributions against
static survey snapshots rather than longitudinal human dynamics. For open-domain questions, such dynamic ground-truth data is scarce and very hard to collect without large-scale,
year-long studies. Moreover, LLM pretraining corpora may already encode outcomes of
past events, making retrospective trend evaluation with historical data unreliable; the only
potential dynamic validation would be predicting future events whose outcomes are not
yet in the training data. We therefore position the framework as a complementary tool
rather than a predictive model of human social dynamics. Besides, the convergence speed
observed in our case studies may be faster than in real-world dynamics, as prior work has
shown that LLM agents tend toward consensus more rapidly than humans (Taubenfeld
et al., 2024). Since MAWM is presented as a general framework with illustrative applications, prompt templates and hyperparameters may need to be adapted for specific research
scenarios; practitioners applying the framework to new domains should carefully calibrate
these choices for their particular use case. Our local consistency evaluation relies on GPT-5
as an LLM judge, which is now common practice in the evaluation of generative agents (Fu
et al., 2024), though it may introduce systematic biases that we have not yet quantified.

C.6

Culture–Language Disentanglement

To disentangle culture from language, we test the triple Argentina, Bolivia, and Peru on two
MAPS questions, Q201 (whether trade increases wages) and Q68 (whether the gap between
rich and poor is a problem), with GPT-4o-mini and Llama-4-Maverick, simulating 50 agents
per country over 20 rounds of interaction. We read the resulting composition at two levels:
the aggregate vote distribution and the post-embedding space of agent-generated content.
The target composition, Spain (European) + Bolivia (Indigenous) ≈ Peru, cannot be tested
directly because Spain is not part of MAPS, so Spanish personas cannot be instantiated.
We therefore choose a triple that reproduces the same European/Indigenous composition
entirely within Spanish-speaking populations, so that language is held fixed and only culture
varies: (1) Argentina as the European-heritage pole, the most European-descent population
in South America (about 85% European ancestry), standing in for the role Spain would
play; (2) Bolivia as the Indigenous-heritage pole (about 62% Indigenous, Andean); and (3)
Peru as the composition target, a mestizo-majority population with substantial European
and Indigenous ancestry that sits demographically and geographically between the two.
Because all three populations speak Spanish, composing them holds language constant, so

any Argentina + Bolivia ≈ Peru effect is a composition of culture representations, not of
language; prior work shows that expressed culture is conditioned by persona rather than

contrast (same population, different language) is the native-versus-English comparison in
Figure 4, which varies language with culture fixed; here we vary culture with language
fixed. Each model runs in two modes: isolated (Argentina, Bolivia, and Peru run separately)

Information Diffusion Analysis

This section inspects the recommendation-based post propagation process in Case 1 and
Case 2. We focus on two countries: the United States from Case 1, whose user attitudes
remain stable and distinct from those of the other two countries, and South Korea from

certain rounds. A broker is defined as a user who receives at least one recommended post
from a foreign user (the source) and whose own post in the same round is subsequently
recommended to at least two domestic users (the audience). We then focus on instances

from brokers to domestic audiences, for two selected periods in each case. For Case 1, we
examine rounds 4-6 and rounds 9-11, as shown in Figures 12-13 and Examples 7-8. For
Case 2, we examine rounds 1-3 and rounds 6-8, as shown in Figures 14-15 and Examples 9-10.
Figures 12-15 visualize the directed weighted graph G (t) and highlight the diffusion paths
